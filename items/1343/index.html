An Entropic Factor Model for Robust Portfolio Replication

📰 Latest · 2026-09-06 Full Article
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-09-06

Source: arxiv · 2026-09-03
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03552

A two-stage Fermi–Dirac entropy model estimates bounded factor loadings and then constructs bounded replication weights, trading a small amount of gross tracking precision for markedly lower turnover and stronger stress-period net performance. In the paper’s COVID-19 backtest, EFM cut annualized turnover from 418.51% to 266.93% and raised net annualized return from 25.64% to 27.85% relative to OLS.

Executive summary

Arratia and Gzyl frame portfolio replication as two coupled, ill-posed inverse problems: estimating asset factor exposures and selecting weights that reproduce target exposures. Their Entropic Factor Model solves both stages with a convex Fermi–Dirac-type entropy over empirically bounded parameters, residuals, and portfolio weights. Across five experiments, EFM generally gives up modest gross tracking accuracy in exchange for smoother allocations, lower turnover, and better stress-period net returns; its sharpest result is during COVID-19, where the turnover reduction dominates OLS above an estimated 1.37-basis-point execution-cost threshold.

Problem and two-stage formulation

The method treats replication as a structural inverse problem rather than directly minimizing return error. Stage 1 jointly estimates each asset’s intercept, factor loadings, and time-indexed residuals under box constraints. Stage 2 solves for portfolio weights whose estimated factor exposures equal the target exposure while satisfying budget and position constraints.

  • Stage 1 uses a design matrix formed from an intercept column, the factor-return matrix, and an identity matrix for residuals.
  • Factor-loading bounds are data-driven; the residual envelope is the greater of 1.05 times the largest absolute midpoint-model residual and 0.5 times the asset-return standard deviation.
  • Stage 2 stacks the transposed loading matrix with a row of ones, imposing both target-factor exposure and full investment.

Entropic regularization and operational behavior

The objective is a smooth convex Fermi–Dirac-type entropy defined on each variable’s finite interval. Its gradient becomes increasingly costly near a boundary, encouraging interior solutions and distributing uncertainty between residuals and structural parameters instead of allowing one large observation to dominate a least-squares fit.

  • The numerical solver stops when the reconstruction-gradient norm falls below 10^-5.
  • Unlike Lasso or Ridge, shrinkage is toward the midpoint of an economically defined feasible interval rather than toward zero.
  • For portfolio weights, the authors recommend a soft feasible interval of [-0.05, 0.999] during stress to reduce zero-boundary thrashing.

Baseline and multi-asset evidence

On a 20-stock S&P 500 replication task, EFM had higher gross tracking error than OLS but lower bias, volatility, and drawdown. For an 80% SPY/20% Bitcoin target replicated with the same equities plus Ethereum and Litecoin, the methods were nearly indistinguishable on aggregate metrics, while EFM estimated Bitcoin betas of 0.999 for Ethereum and 1.05 for Litecoin.

  • Baseline EFM reconstruction error was approximately 2×10^-7, indicating that the budget and exposure constraints were solved accurately.
  • In baseline equity tracking, EFM volatility was 14.50% versus 15.13% for OLS, and maximum drawdown was 17.53% versus 19.24%.
  • In the hybrid experiment, EFM gross tracking error was 8.65% versus 8.68% for OLS and net return was 8.66% versus 8.64%.

Idiosyncratic-shock circuit breaker

The authors injected a -25% one-day return into Amazon while SPY remained flat, then trained on a 60-day window containing the corruption. OLS reduced Amazon’s beta but retained a 5.73% allocation; EFM estimated a negative beta and cut the allocation to 0.27%.

  • The clean-data Amazon beta was 1.749; OLS estimated 1.219 after corruption, while EFM estimated -1.261.
  • EFM’s Amazon weight was roughly one-twentieth of OLS’s allocation.
  • On the subsequent test period, EFM produced 17.01% annualized net return versus 15.54% for OLS, although its gross tracking error was slightly higher.

Trading frictions and COVID-19 stress test

In a benign 2019–2023 rolling backtest, EFM reduced annualized turnover only modestly while worsening gross tracking error; the paper estimates a 9.25-basis-point break-even friction in this regime. During the COVID-19 test, however, EFM cut turnover by 151.58 percentage points and improved both net return and downside metrics.

  • The COVID-19 setup used a 126-day training window, rebalanced every 10 trading days, and charged 10 basis points per unit of turnover.
  • EFM earned 27.85% net annualized return versus 25.64% for OLS while showing lower annualized volatility, 33.44% versus 33.80%.
  • The stress result supports a regime-dependent thesis: entropic stability has much greater economic value when covariance estimates and transaction costs deteriorate together.

Key findings

  • EFM consistently exchanges some variance-minimizing precision for bounded, structurally smoother allocations.
  • Under the synthetic Amazon shock, EFM cut the corrupted asset’s weight from the OLS allocation of 5.73% to 0.27% and subsequently achieved 17.01% annualized net return versus 15.54%.
  • During COVID-19, EFM reduced annualized turnover from 418.51% to 266.93%, improved net annualized return from 25.64% to 27.85%, and lowered maximum drawdown from 31.27% to 30.04%.
  • The calculated execution-cost break-even point is strongly regime dependent: 1.37 basis points in the COVID-19 experiment versus about 9.25 basis points in the benign-market experiment.
  • The multi-asset experiment found near-unit Bitcoin loadings for Ethereum and Litecoin and delivered essentially the same aggregate tracking performance as OLS.

Limitations

  • The empirical comparison is limited to OLS; it does not benchmark EFM against modern robust regression, constrained Ridge/Lasso, shrinkage-covariance, or nonlinear replication methods discussed in the related-work section.
  • Most experiments reuse a small universe of 20 surviving US large-cap stocks, so survivorship bias, broader-universe scalability, and generalization to less liquid assets remain untested.
  • Results depend on extrema-derived coefficient bounds, a residual envelope, and a chosen soft weight interval, but the paper does not report sensitivity analyses or statistical confidence intervals for these choices and performance differences.

Open questions

  • Does EFM retain its turnover and drawdown advantage against tuned constrained Ridge/Lasso or robust M-estimators under identical transaction-cost assumptions?
  • How stable are results to alternative beta bounds, residual-envelope rules, soft-margin limits, and rolling windows?
  • Can the two-stage entropic solver scale efficiently to hundreds or thousands of assets and multiple correlated factors?
