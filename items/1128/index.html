Poisoning Agentic Alpha: Adversarial Vulnerabilities Across Roles and Architectures in Multi-Agent Trading Systems

📰 Latest · 2026-08-27 Full Article
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-08-27

Source: arxiv · 2026-08-25
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24069

A black-box adversary who can only touch text an agent reads — news, a Reddit post, a prepended prompt — flips a TradingAgents-style pipeline's daily BUY/SELL/HOLD call on 14-99% of attackable days, and the terminal Risk Manager is by far the weakest link (98.9% jailbreak success). Crucially, flip rate does not track money: the highest-ASR attack moves the backtested portfolio by $0 at the median, so ASR is neither a lower nor an upper bound on realized loss.

Executive summary

Na et al. (SKKU / UCL / Edinburgh / UT Austin / BlackRock / Florida / UNIST / LinqAlpha) run the first finance-specific systematic study of adversarial failure in LLM multi-agent trading pipelines, along two axes: which ROLE an adversarial signal enters through, and which communication TOPOLOGY lets it survive to the decision node. The threat model is deliberately weak and realistic — black-box and content-only: the attacker may alter external source data and prompt-level content but has no access to weights, tool servers, orchestration state, or reasoning traces. Everything is a sandboxed backtest on 5 assets (AAPL, MSFT, NVDA, TSLA, BTC-USD) over 2026 Q1, 61 NYSE days, N=305 asset-days per configuration, two backbones (GPT-4.1 family, Qwen3-235B-A22B), temperature 0, no live capital. Headline: no architecture is inherently robust, majority voting helps only while compromised agents stay a minority, and the P&L consequence of a flipped decision is largely decoupled from the flip rate.

Problem: the communication that makes multi-agent trading work is the attack surface

The paper takes the widely-used TradingAgents pipeline (Xiao et al. 2025) and decomposes it into four functional roles — Analyst Team (market/social/news/fundamental), Research Team (bull vs bear debate with a facilitator), Trader agents with differing risk profiles, and a Risk Management team enforcing exposure constraints. Its claim is that a single compromised agent propagates a corrupted signal through inter-agent communication until it reaches the final decision and becomes realized loss. Motivation is drawn from real incidents (AI Incident Database #1003, 55.5 ETH transferred out of a wallet after fraudulent prompts via a dashboard), not hypotheticals.

  • Decision space is a daily action in {buy, sell, hold} for one instrument from prices, news and social posts.
  • Compromised agents share the same information access as benign agents, and benign agents are unaware of them.
  • Two entry levels: data-level (corrupt what the system ingests) vs agent-level (manipulate an agent's beliefs, objective, or safety constraints).
  • Code and data released at github.com/cwna97/multi_agent_trading_attack.

Threat model: black-box, content-only, low-privilege

The adversary may modify only the content of external data sources the system ingests and prompt-level content entering an agent. No model weights, no tool servers, no orchestration state, no reasoning traces. The authors call this a 'democratized' threat model and position it explicitly against TradeTrap (Yan et al. 2025), whose full-decision-trace recording requires internal access. ASR is defined over ATTACKABLE days only — those whose clean decision already differs from the target — so days the system would have chosen the target anyway are excluded from both numerator and denominator.

  • ASR = #{i: clean_i != target_i and attacked_i = target_i} / #{i: clean_i != target_i}.
  • Five role-matched attacks: Data Poisoning (news analyst); Indirect Prompt Injection (social analyst, directives concealed inside otherwise legitimate posts); Persuasive Adversary (a bull/bear debater replaced with a FOMO/appeal-to-authority variant); Objective Hijacking (Trader instruction prompt replaced with a contrarian objective); Jailbreaking (Risk Manager, hypothetical framing prepended, system prompt untouched).
  • The first two are data-level; the last three are agent-level.
  • Both BUY-targeted and SELL-targeted variants are run for every attack.

Role axis: the terminal safety role is the single point of failure, and pipeline depth explains nothing

With the backbone fixed to gpt-4.1 and data-level injection at a 10% ratio, jailbreaking the Risk Manager succeeds on 98.9% of attackable BUY-targeted days — 1.9x the next most effective attack — while the other four sit in a 13.8-53.3% band. The authors are careful: this is not evidence that risk agents are intrinsically fragile; in this pipeline the Risk Manager IS the terminal decision node, so compromising it bypasses all downstream aggregation, debate, and validation. There is no monotone relation between an attack's pipeline position and its success.

  • Researcher-level persuasion has the highest non-terminal macro-average ASR (50.6%) while the Trader — the role immediately upstream of the Risk Manager — is lowest (25.4%), with indirect injection at 34.9% and data poisoning at 26.2%.
  • Two-proportion z-tests cross-checked with Fisher's exact: data poisoning, indirect injection and objective hijacking form a statistically overlapping low tier; persuasive adversary is significantly higher than every data-level attack.
  • Directional asymmetry: the three non-persuasive attacks are LESS effective targeting BUY (-2.7, -5.8, -4.2 points) because the clean system is already BUY-leaning (BUY 40.0% / HOLD 33.8% / SELL 26.2%). Persuasion inverts this (53.3 BUY vs 41.9 SELL) — bullish arguments ride the model's optimistic prior.
  • Poison-ratio sweep (10% / 40% / 80% of source items): pooled ASR rises 19.1% to 30.6%, front-loaded (+9.3 then +2.2 points). Even at 80% contamination, data poisoning stays 68 points below jailbreaking at the default 10% ratio.

Architecture axis: four topologies, the APS lens, and where it breaks

Holding the analyst set (Market, Social, News) and the entry point fixed, four topologies differ only in routing/aggregation: Centralized (one decision agent averages all three reports, k=0 aggregation stages), Linear (sequential pass, k=1), Hybrid (peer-revision round then mean aggregation, k=2), Decentralized (majority vote). The Adversarial Signal Preservation Score APS = (1/(1+k))(1-p), with p=1 for voting, gives the parameter-free ordering Cen. 1.0 > Lin. .50 > Hyb. .33 > Dec. .00. APS is presented explicitly as a post-hoc interpretive lens, not a mechanism.

  • APS vs ASR correlates on architecture averages: r=0.85 (GPT-4.1), r=0.88 (Qwen3-235B-A22B) — but with wide ticker heterogeneity.
  • The ordering is cleanest for GPT-4.1 SELL-targeted and REVERSES for BUY-targeted, where Decentralized is most vulnerable to News (44.2%) and Social (51.2%). Explanation is a quorum effect APS does not encode: on 44.6% of attackable days at least one benign analyst already votes BUY, so one compromised channel completes a 2/3 majority instead of being outvoted.
  • Majority voting is thresholded, not gradual: going from one to two compromised analyst channels raises BUY-targeted News (44.2%) and Social (51.2%) both to 68.7%, while Market only moves 9.5% to 12.9%.
  • Multi-agent organization helps but does not cure: pooled BUY-targeted ASR vs a single-agent baseline is 29.1-48.1% vs 70.2% for GPT-4.1, and 60.1-82.2% vs 87.9% for Qwen.

P&L: flip rate is a bad proxy for financial harm

Two separate backtests run on a $100,000 long-only, single-position, daily-close engine with NO transaction costs. Table 2 is a path-independent single-flip marginal; Table 4 is the path-dependent cumulative return of a sustained attack. Neither tracks ASR. Jailbreaking flips 97.2% of decisions yet a typical success moves the portfolio $0 at the median, with EV (-$43/attempt) comparable to much lower-ASR attacks. Objective hijacking is mean-positive (+$40) but median-negative (-$85). On the architecture axis, Decentralized has the best clean cumulative return (-2.68%) but the worst attacked degradation (-12.01 pp under D1), while Centralized — weaker when clean (-9.58%) — loses only 1.45 pp under D2.

  • Uncertainty on role-axis financial numbers comes from a moving-block bootstrap (block length 10 days, resampled within ticker, 10,000 resamples).
  • Signed EV is explicitly NOT ASR x ($/success); many 'successful' flips are inert because they do not change the position actually taken.
  • A cost-aware simulator (0.1% commission / 0.05% slippage) exists in the codebase but is NOT the engine behind either published table.

Significance for practitioners building multi-agent trading stacks

The operational lesson is architectural, not model-level: security must come from deliberate validation and aggregation placement, not from agent multiplicity. Concretely — do not co-locate safety enforcement with final decision authority; assume majority voting buys a hard quorum threshold rather than graceful degradation, so size redundancy against the number of channels an attacker can plausibly reach; and do not use decision-flip rate as a risk metric, because it both overstates (jailbreak) and mis-signs (objective hijack) capital impact.

  • Attacker privilege required is low: content in a news feed, a Reddit post, or a prepended prompt string — no infrastructure compromise.
  • The three agent-level attacks presuppose the attacker can substitute or prepend to an agent's prompt, a materially stronger operational assumption than poisoning a public feed, even though it is still 'black-box' by the paper's definition.
  • The ethics statement notes only sandboxed backtests and synthetic manipulated content, and suggests source-reputation/provenance checks, stronger guardrails, and periodic audits of prompts, outputs and logs.

Key findings

  • Jailbreaking the terminal Risk Manager via a prepended hypothetical framing flips 98.9% (BUY) / 95.5% (SELL) of attackable days — 1.9x the next best attack — because in this pipeline the safety role is also the final decision node.
  • Success does not scale with pipeline depth: Researcher-level persuasion (macro-avg 50.6% ASR) beats Trader-level objective hijacking (25.4%) even though the Trader sits closer to the decision.
  • ASR and money decouple. Jailbreaking flips 97.2% of decisions but moves the backtested $100K portfolio by $0 at the median with EV -$43/attempt; objective hijacking has the lowest ASR (15.9%) yet the largest positive mean EV (+$40) and a -$85 median.
  • The APS ordering correlates with architecture-average ASR at r=0.85 / r=0.88, but reverses for GPT-4.1 BUY-targeted attacks, where Decentralized voting is MOST vulnerable because on 44.6% of attackable days one benign analyst already votes BUY and a single compromised channel completes a 2/3 quorum.
  • Majority-vote robustness is a hard threshold, not a gradient: moving from one to two compromised analyst channels jumps BUY-targeted News and Social ASR from 44.2% and 51.2% to 68.7% each.
  • Best clean performance is not best attacked performance: Decentralized has the best clean CR (-2.68%) but the worst degradation (-12.01 / -12.38 pp), while Centralized degrades only 1.45 pp under indirect injection despite a worse clean CR.
  • Attack volume saturates: raising the poisoned share of source items from 10% to 40% to 80% only moves pooled ASR from 19.1% to 30.6%.

Notable quotes

It has no access to model weights, tool servers, orchestration state, or reasoning traces, and is therefore strictly weaker than threat models presuming white-box instrumentation of system internals.
The result highlights the design risk of coupling safety enforcement with final decision authority without an additional check.
ASR is thus neither a lower nor an upper bound on realized loss: it overstates severity for jailbreaking and mis-signs it for objective hijacking, so ranking by ASR alone can misrepresent practical financial consequence.
Thus, architecture provides a useful first-order signal, but its effect depends on the backbone, attack channel, and target direction.

Limitations

  • Everything is a sandboxed historical backtest — no live capital, no execution timing, no transaction costs or slippage in the two published financial tables (a cost-aware simulator exists in the repo but was not used), so absolute loss figures are optimistic.
  • Statistical power is thin in places: only 5 large-cap assets over 61 days; the role axis uses a single backbone for cost reasons; several cells rest on very small attackable denominators (MSFT's 100% BUY-targeted figure comes from N=3 days; Qwen BUY-targeted architecture cells are N=45-106).
  • The clean system's strong BUY prior mechanically determines every denominator; APS is an unvalidated post-hoc heuristic that the paper's own BUY-targeted results contradict; and the agent-level attacks assume the attacker can write into an agent's prompt, a stronger capability than poisoning a public feed.

Open questions

  • Does the Risk-Manager result survive decoupling safety enforcement from final decision authority — if an independent, non-terminal validator sits after the risk agent, does the 98.9% jailbreak rate collapse, and at what cost to clean performance?
  • How do these ASR and dCR numbers change under an execution-aware simulator with realistic costs, slippage, position sizing and shorting, where 'inert' median-$0 flips might become expensive?
  • Can the quorum reversal be designed away — weighted or reputation-aware voting, provenance filters, heterogeneous backbones per analyst channel?
