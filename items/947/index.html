AI4AI-Bench: Benchmarking LLM Agents in Algorithmic Design for Recursive Self-Improvement

📰 Latest · 2026-08-24 Full Article
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-08-24

Source: arxiv · 2026-08-20
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20318

The first benchmark that isolates the one link recursive self-improvement actually compounds through — can an agent rewrite a real training algorithm, not tune it? — and the answer today is barely: across 29 agent configurations on 10 frozen research repos the mean score is 0.166 on a scale where 0.1 is the algorithm the repo already shipped, and more than half of all submissions never touch how the model learns at all.

Executive summary

AI4AI-Bench argues that recursive self-improvement compounds only through the algorithmic design level — objectives, update rules, supervision — and that no existing benchmark separates that from data work, hyperparameter tuning, or systems engineering. It freezes 10 research repositories spanning 10 distinct training-algorithm families (SFT, multi-turn agentic RL, on-policy distillation, Bradley-Terry reward modelling, DPO, diffusion RL, unlearning, discrete graph diffusion, weight averaging, one-shot pruning) and gives an agent 4 hours on one B300 to rewrite the training code; the patch alone is then applied in a fresh container and retrained from scratch for up to 12 hours and scored by a frozen evaluator the agent never sees. Across 29 configurations of 6 systems on all 10 tasks (290 cells) the mean mapped score is 0.166 and the best system (Claude Opus 5) reaches 0.250, on a ladder where 0.1 is the repository's shipped algorithm and 1.0 the task optimum. The paper's real contribution is the diff-level audit: of 263 classifiable submissions, 141 stay entirely on the 'run' side (budgets, checkpoints, hyperparameters, adapter capacity) and only 122 touch how the model learns — and the latter average 0.226 vs 0.126. Reasoning effort mostly buys the willingness to go there (8% to 64% of submissions), not better execution once there.

The thesis: three automatable levels, only one of them compounds

The paper splits the RSI loop into a Systems engineering level (kernels, parallelism, communication), a Data level (mixtures, synthesis, filtering), and an Algorithmic design level (objectives, update rules, regularization, schedules). Systems work is capped by the hardware roofline; data work is capped by the finite stock of human text, by synthetic corpora that re-express what the model already has, and by a loss-gap power law. Only an algorithmic change alters the compute-to-capability exchange rate, so every subsequent run — including the run that produces the next agent — inherits it. The authors' operational line is not diff size but what a change touches: a hyperparameter is a number the training algorithm takes as given, an algorithmic change rewrites the algorithm.

  • Positioned against MLE-Bench / MLE-Dojo / ML-Bench (Kaggle-shaped: feature engineering + ensembling win, the learning algorithm stays a library call), PostTrainBench (largest levers are data assembly and initialization), RSIBench-Data (freezes the post-training stack so only data varies), MLS-Bench (140 tasks but the component boundary is handed to the agent).
  • Closest prior art is 'autoresearch' (one training file, everything fair game) — but the paper notes a 5-minute single-script run is not a research repository, and that its edits behaved like hyperparameter search and lost to CMA-ES and TPE.
  • Explicit claim: AI4AI-Bench is the first benchmark to make the algorithmic-design step itself the object of evaluation, by removing the agent, rerunning the submitted source from a clean start, and classifying the patch.

Task taxonomy: 10 frozen repos, 10 algorithm families, incommensurable metrics

A task is the tuple (C, a0, q, m, d): frozen repository source, frozen starting model, a cheap proxy the agent may query freely, the final metric, and its direction. Admission required three properties — the repo must ship a training algorithm its authors actually run (not a tutorial), must ship a frozen starting model, and its metric must be recomputable reproducibly in under half a day on a single B300. Two of the ten (Model Soup, OWL) do no training at all and are executed once; they are deliberately kept so the suite is about algorithm design rather than about training loops.

  • OpenR1 (SFT, Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B-Instruct -> LiveCodeBench v6, 175 problems, 10 samples at T=0.2); RAGEN (multi-turn GRPO on Sokoban, Qwen2.5-3B -> held-out 512-board solve rate); OPD (on-policy distillation, R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B -> AIME 24/25, 60 questions at 32 samples).
  • BTRM (Bradley-Terry reward model, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 -> RewardBench; any overlap between a training row and RewardBench invalidates the run); DPO (merged Zephyr/Mistral-7B -> IFEval prompt-level strict, 413 held-out prompts); DDPO (SD v1.5 + LoRA vs frozen CLIP aesthetic reward, 256 images at scoring).
  • NPO (Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, TOFU forget10, balanced score = harmonic mean of extraction strength and model utility); DiGress (QM9 discrete graph diffusion -> test NLL, lower better); Model Soup (72 fixed CLIP ViT-B/32 checkpoints -> ImageNet-V2 top-1 on the full 10,000); OWL (OPT-6.7B one-shot pruning, hard artifact gate: decoder sparsity in [0.699, 0.701] -> WikiText-2 test perplexity).
  • Proxy-to-final relation varies by task: sometimes a strict subsample (BTRM: 512 of 2,985 pairs visible), sometimes a different benchmark entirely (OPD: MATH-500 proxy vs AIME final).

How success is verified: execution-grounded, agent-removed, evaluator frozen in advance

Verification is entirely execution-grounded, with no LLM-as-judge and no formal proof anywhere in the scoring path. The agent works 4 hours inside the repo on one B300, free to edit, launch its own training runs, and query the fast proxy without limit. Then it stops and the ONLY thing that crosses forward is a source-code patch — no weights it trained, no cached state, no notes. The patch is applied to a fresh container and run from initialization until it terminates or 12 hours elapse; the three most recent checkpoints are scored independently and the cell takes the best under the task's direction, with a merged loadable Hugging Face model required. The baseline is the repository's own committed code under the identical procedure, hardware, budget, evaluator, and asset. LLM judgement enters at exactly one place, and it is not scoring: a separate language model reads each submitted diff to classify what it changed.

  • Exploration budget 4 h on one B300; verification budget 12 h from scratch. Submission is source code, not a number and not a model.
  • The agent never evaluates the final metric. The boundary is one of access and timing, not sample disjointness — on some tasks the cheap proxy is drawn from the same corpus the final evaluation reads.
  • Dense scoring ladder (needed because the 10 metrics are incommensurable, and because a binary win/loss is a sparse RL reward): 0 = uninformative model, 0.1 = the algorithm the repository ships, 1.0 = the task optimum; piecewise-linear in a progress coordinate, clipped to [0,1].
  • The progress coordinate is the identity for rates/aesthetic score/NLL and -log for a perplexity. Without that transform, OWL 53.4 -> 16.2 would read as closing 71% of the distance when the correct figure is 30% (1.19 of 3.98 nats removed).
  • Contract text (~70% shared verbatim across the ten tasks) explicitly pushes against premature submission and bans importing external boards/demonstrations/weights, training on final seeds, or evaluation-specific lookups.

Agent topology, systems evaluated, and the compute/cost budget

The unit under test is not a model but a 'system' = model + harness + reasoning effort. Six systems, 29 configurations, 290 cells. Topology is whatever the harness does inside the container (single agent per task, no prescribed multi-agent structure); the only knob varied on its own is reasoning effort. Spend does not explain the ordering: cost ranges roughly ninefold across systems on the same harness, and Claude Opus 5 leads the study at a median of $181 — under half of what the second-placed system spent.

  • Three GPT-5.6 variants (Sol, Terra, Luna) under Codex at all six effort levels; Claude Opus 5 and Claude Sonnet 5 under Claude Code at the five levels that harness exposes; Kimi K3 under Claude Code at its highest. 29 configurations x 10 tasks = 290 cells.
  • Median exploration cost per configuration: $434 (Sol), $181 (Opus 5), $48 (Luna). Kimi K3's whole run cost $30. Sonnet 5 spends about twice Luna's budget for a 0.028 difference.
  • Within the Codex grid, lowest to highest effort moves the median configuration from 4 evaluations in its four hours to 16, from 18 edited lines to 246, and from 11k output tokens to 109k; median exploration cost per task rises from $1.69 to $34.60.
  • Total exploration-stage API spend across the whole study: $5,334 — excluding the GPU hours of the 290 twelve-hour verification runs and the evaluator's compute.
  • Spend is read in output tokens rather than total tokens, because input counts are inflated by each harness's context replay.

Results: the entire study sits in the bottom fifth of the scale

Mean over 290 cells is 0.166; the strongest system (Claude Opus 5) averages 0.250; the single best configuration is Claude Opus 5 at medium effort at 0.288. Since 0.1 is the shipped algorithm and 1.0 the optimum, even the top of the study closes under a fifth of the remaining distance, and well under a tenth on average. In the other direction, 124 of 290 cells fall below 0.1 — more than two fifths of attempts leave the repository worse than it was. The system ordering is clean but compressed, and no system rises monotonically with effort.

  • System means: Claude Opus 5 0.250 > GPT-5.6 Sol 0.191 > Kimi K3 0.174 > Claude Sonnet 5 0.145 > GPT-5.6 Terra 0.135 > GPT-5.6 Luna 0.117.
  • One task is an outlier where agents fully saturate: RAGEN (multi-turn agentic RL) goes from a 0.170 baseline solve rate to 1.00 (score 1.000) for several configurations — by abandoning GRPO for imitation learning.
  • OWL is the other big mover: perplexity 53.4 -> 13.0 (mapped ~0.42), driven by submissions that add training to a procedure defined to do none.
  • Most columns barely move: OpenR1 0.127 -> 0.138 best, OPD 0.436 -> 0.449, BTRM 74.9 -> 77.1, Model Soup 0.686 -> 0.701, DiGress 65.8 -> 63.7 NLL.

The diff audit: run side vs learning side, and what effort actually buys

This is the part that turns 'the agent improved the training algorithm' into a checkable statement. Every submission's diff is classified by a separate LLM into eight families on two sides of a line. Four are run-side (how long it trains and how often it saves; the training hyperparameters; which checkpoint to keep; how much trainable capacity and where). Four are learning-side (the loss it optimizes; the supervision it learns from; the update rule itself; the data it trains on). Of 280 submissions, 17 made no classifiable change; of the remaining 263, 141 stay entirely on the run side and only 122 touch how the model learns. Those 122 average 0.226 against 0.126 — a gap of 0.100 against a standard error of 0.022, robust to dropping RAGEN (0.182 vs 0.128) and holding within four of the five models. The authors are careful that this is observational, not randomized.

  • Reasoning effort raises the share of submissions reaching the learning side from 8% (none) to 64% (max), and the mean score from 0.094 to 0.196 (SE 0.027); within the Codex grid it rises at every step, 0.094 to 0.204.
  • Reliability: 19 cells score zero. 8 ended the four hours without a usable patch (4 of them workspaces the host found empty after the agent exited early); 11 submitted a complete patch that started its 12-hour run but produced nothing satisfying the contract, most often no loadable merged model on disk. All 19 terminated normally; 12 of the 19 are at the two lowest effort levels.
  • Case study (OWL): one submission replaced one-shot pruning with a three-stage pipeline — a different rule for selecting/updating surviving weights, layerwise distillation, then a masked KD fine-tune of the whole model (AdamW, 666 steps, cosine decay) — taking perplexity from 53.4 to just over 13, after diagnosing a first attempt that scored 572 because the weight-allocation step overwrote layer 0's input in place.
  • Case study (Model Soup): one submission built an instrument first — all 72 models' tensors packed into one GPU-resident matrix — cutting a candidate evaluation from ~190 s to 0.38 s (~500x), then ranked five methods on it: best single 0.6935, uniform average 0.6880, top-k by accuracy 0.6945, greedy soup 0.7025, Adam-learned coefficients 0.7020.
  • The common thread in the exceptional submissions: each built something measurable before acting. Among 263 submissions that behaviour is the exception, not the norm.

Self-improvement: measured, not demonstrated

No genuine recursive self-improvement is exhibited. The loop is never closed — no improved training algorithm is fed back into producing the next agent, and the agent under test is not the agent being trained. What the benchmark does is measure the precondition for RSI: can an agent design a training algorithm at all? The paper's own verdict is that today's agents recover a competent default rather than design past one. Individual tasks do show real algorithmic invention (RAGEN's RL-to-imitation-learning swap, OWL's pruning-plus-distillation pipeline, Soup's learned coefficients), so the capability is not zero — but at study scale the strongest configuration still ends a tenth of the way past the algorithm it started from.

  • The paper's designed second use: because the score is dense rather than binary, the suite is meant to be a viable RL training environment for research agents — the sparse-reward argument is why the 0/0.1/1.0 ladder exists at all.
  • Suite, evaluators, and every scored submission are released.
  • Concrete reproducibility anchor from the released contract (RAGEN): fixed policy start 60/512 = 0.117188, shipped solution 87/512 = 0.169922, training time 2746.19 s, final scoring time 339.15 s, peak GPU memory during final scoring 247,684 MiB.

Key findings

  • Mean mapped score across 29 configurations x 10 tasks (290 cells) is 0.166, best system 0.250 (Claude Opus 5), best single configuration 0.288 — on a scale where 0.1 is the algorithm the repository already ships and 1.0 the task optimum. 124 of 290 cells fall BELOW 0.1, i.e. more than two fifths of attempts make the repository worse.
  • Of 263 classifiable submissions, 141 (53.6%) never touch how the model learns and only 122 (46.4%) do; the learning-side group averages 0.226 vs 0.126 (gap 0.100, SE 0.022), robust to dropping the saturated RAGEN task (0.182 vs 0.128) and holding in 4 of 5 models. The authors flag this as observational, not causal.
  • Reasoning effort raises the learning-side share from 8% to 64% and the mean score from 0.094 to 0.196 (SE 0.027); inside the Codex grid it rises monotonically 0.094 -> 0.204. Effort mostly buys the willingness to attempt the thing that pays, not a better attempt.
  • Cost does not predict skill: spend varies ~9x on the same harness (median $434 for GPT-5.6 Sol vs $48 for Luna) and the cost ordering is not the score ordering; Opus 5 leads at a median of $181, and Kimi K3's whole run cost $30 for 0.174. Total exploration API spend was $5,334, excluding all GPU hours for the 290 twelve-hour verification runs.
  • Reliability failure rate: 19 of 290 cells scored zero — 8 with no usable patch after 4 hours (4 of them empty workspaces from early exit) and 11 with a complete patch whose 12-hour run produced nothing contract-valid (usually no loadable merged model). All 19 terminated normally, so these are submission-quality failures, not infrastructure crashes; 12 of 19 sit at the two lowest effort levels.
  • The best submissions all built an instrument before acting: a 500x faster evaluation rig for weight averaging (0.38 s vs ~190 s per candidate, yielding greedy soup 0.7025 and Adam-learned coefficients 0.7020 vs 0.6880 uniform), a debugged pruning pipeline taking OWL perplexity 53.4 -> ~13, and a solver-labelled imitation-learning replacement for GRPO that saturated RAGEN at 1.00.

Notable quotes

So what the boundary guarantees is that no agent could score a candidate under the metric that decides its result — not that it never saw a row that metric would later read.
It is the answer to one question only: does the agent’s code produce a better model than the code that was already there, run under identical conditions?
Four hours, a whole repository, and a task statement that says in as many words to improve this training algorithm, and more than half of the submissions never reach that layer.
Reasoning effort works, then, by making an agent attempt the thing that pays rather than by making the attempt itself better: it brings more agents to the loop that matters — reading the training dynamics, naming the mechanism that is failing, changing it — and what they gain is about what arriving there is worth.

Limitations

  • Contamination is bounded by ACCESS and TIMING, not by sample disjointness: the paper states outright that on some tasks the cheap proxy is drawn from the same corpus the final evaluation reads, so the guarantee is only that no agent could score a candidate under the deciding metric. (BTRM is the exception: any train/RewardBench overlap invalidates the run.)
  • The headline learning-side vs run-side gap (0.226 vs 0.126) is not a randomized comparison — the systems that reach the learning layer more often are the stronger ones to begin with — and the change classification itself is produced by a separate LLM reading diffs, with non-exclusive families (3.13 matches per submission), 17 of 280 submissions unclassifiable, and Kimi K3 excluded from that corpus entirely.
  • Statistical power and ceiling effects: one seed per cell despite explicitly stochastic training, 290 cells total, and most task columns barely move off the 0.1 line so the study-wide mean is dominated by two tasks (RAGEN saturating at 1.00, OWL's large perplexity drop). Cost is also a real barrier to replication — $5,334 of API calls plus up to 290 twelve-hour single-B300 verification runs, whose GPU cost is not reported.

Open questions

  • The loop is measured, never closed: no improved training algorithm is fed back into producing the next agent. Does an algorithmic gain of this size actually compound across a successor generation, and what score on this scale would be needed before it does?
  • Can the diagnostic loop the paper finds missing — read the training dynamics, name the failing mechanism, change that mechanism — be scaffolded or trained into an agent, rather than merely purchased with reasoning effort? The suite's dense reward is explicitly designed as an RL environment for exactly this, but no such training run is reported.
  • Is the 0.1-to-1.0 'distance to the metric optimum' ladder the right yardstick when every system lands in the bottom quarter and no system improves monotonically with effort — i.e. how much of the remaining distance is even reachable by algorithmic change under a 12-hour single-GPU budget, as opposed to requiring scale?
