AIDE: AI-Driven Exploration in the Space of Code

📚 Foundations · 2026-09-13
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-09-13

Theme: Verifier-Grounded Autonomous Research and Code Search
These systems represent three increasingly search-oriented research topologies: a role-specialized multi-agent laboratory, a solution tree over executable ML programs, and an evolutionary archive over machine-graded code. Their evidence shows that autonomous research becomes materially stronger as evaluation moves from LLM judgment toward held-out metrics, exact artifacts, executable checks, and production measurements.
Source: arxiv
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13138

A foundational execution-grounded agent design that turns machine-learning engineering into tree search over executable code, using objective evaluations rather than model self-judgment to guide iteration.

Executive summary

AIDE reframes machine-learning engineering as optimization over executable programs: an LLM drafts, debugs, or makes atomic improvements, while a hard-coded tree policy selects candidates and an external evaluator scores them. Its solution tree and summarized history replace an ever-growing ReAct-style transcript while preserving performance evidence from prior attempts. The paper reports competitive execution-based results on Weco-Kaggle and independently conducted MLE-Bench and RE-Bench evaluations; its headline evidence is based on program execution and benchmark metrics, not LLM-judge ratings.

Problem and optimization formulation

Conventional AutoML searches hand-specified hyperparameter or architecture spaces, while general-purpose ReAct-style agents accumulate a long interaction history. AIDE instead defines a solution space of programs and a stateless scalar objective h(s), making independently executable candidates directly comparable and amenable to tree search.

  • The root solution is empty code, and every evaluated candidate is a standalone Python program.
  • The formulation decomposes a monolithic, long-horizon task into measurable improvement attempts.
  • Code-space search lets the LLM propose preprocessing, modeling, architecture, and optimization changes beyond a fixed configuration grid.

Architecture and search topology

AIDE stores programs as nodes in a solution tree and improvement attempts as directed edges. A fixed search policy chooses among drafting diverse initial solutions, debugging failed nodes subject to a depth limit, and improving the best valid solution; the coding operator uses a specialized prompt for each operation.

  • Drafting produces a plan and a single-file implementation from scratch.
  • Debugging consumes error logs and execution traces while trying to preserve the parent approach.
  • Improving proposes exactly one atomic change so its effect can be measured.
  • A summarization operator retains metrics, hyperparameters, and debugging hints without placing the full search history in every prompt.
  • A small data preview supplies dataset dimensions, columns, and split metadata to each coding prompt.

Execution-grounded verification

Each generated program is executed and assigned an external scalar score, which becomes the evidence used for node selection. In Weco-Kaggle Lite, labels for a manually constructed holdout are hidden from AIDE, the program writes a submission file, and performance is mapped to Kaggle leaderboard quantiles; MLE-Bench uses validity checks and Kaggle-derived medal metrics, while RE-Bench scores task execution. These are benchmark-verifier signals, not LLM-judge claims.

  • AIDE can see holdout inputs but not their labels during code generation.
  • The internal validation split chosen by generated code is distinct from the benchmark holdout.
  • Search history records both code and measured performance, allowing failed or inferior branches to be distinguished objectively.
  • MLE-Bench results reproduced in the paper were reported by Chan et al.; RE-Bench results were produced by METR rather than by the AIDE authors.

Results and inference costs

On 16 Weco-Kaggle Lite tasks, AIDE with GPT-4 Turbo exceeded 51.38% of human competitors on average and was above the human median on 50.00% of tasks. In full MLE-Bench pass@1, AIDE with o1-preview achieved 82.8 ± 1.1% valid submissions and 16.9 ± 1.1% any medals; on the low-complexity subset, adding AIDE to o1-preview raised any-medal performance from 7.6 ± 2.6% to 36.4 ± 7.9%. The appendix reports GPT-4 Turbo inference cost as below $1.50 for most Weco-Kaggle tasks and up to approximately $2.50 for higher-prompting tasks, using January–February 2024 pricing.

  • Full Weco-Kaggle averages were 48.23% of humans exceeded and 49.21% of competitions above the human median.
  • On seven RE-Bench environments, AIDE surpassed the human average within six hours and found a Triton-kernel solution faster than any of nine human experts working for up to 64 hours.
  • The RE-Bench advantage diminished over longer horizons as humans caught up.
  • The cost figure reports LLM inference expenditure, not a complete accounting of training compute, benchmark infrastructure, or engineering costs.

Significance and boundary conditions

AIDE demonstrates a reusable topology for autonomous research and coding systems: preserve candidate lineage, use compact evidence summaries, isolate atomic changes, and let executable metrics determine progress. Its strongest gains occur where evaluation is fast and reliable; the paper also shows that a greedy best-node policy is poorly suited to large codebases and changes requiring coordinated multi-step interaction.

  • The approach converts additional inference and execution budget into more measured trials.
  • Localized prompts make long searches possible without continually expanding context.
  • Objective validation reduces reliance on persuasive but unverified model-generated explanations.
  • The design motivates richer exploration policies and state representations for harder research workflows.

Key findings

  • A solution-tree topology retains alternative programs and their measured scores while keeping each LLM call focused on one parent node and one local operation.
  • Separating drafting, debugging, and atomic improvement gives execution failures and performance changes explicit roles in search rather than folding everything into a single conversation.
  • AIDE with GPT-4 Turbo exceeded 51.38% of human competitors on average across 16 Weco-Kaggle Lite tasks, ahead of the reported H2O AutoML, AutoGPT, and human-with-ChatGPT baselines.
  • In independently reported full MLE-Bench results, AIDE with o1-preview obtained 16.9 ± 1.1% any medals, versus 4.4 ± 1.4% for OpenHands with GPT-4o.
  • RE-Bench indicates that rapid executable iteration can beat experts at short horizons, but the greedy search loses ground on long-horizon, multi-step, or large-codebase tasks.

Notable quotes

By employing the tree search method, AIDE systematically explores solutions that optimize validation metrics, breaking down the monolithic optimization task into atomic improvement steps.
Combining these three operations keeps the solution tree structured and ensures that each new node arises from a well-defined modification of a parent node.
This design helps avoid oversized prompts, preserves a clear performance record for each node, and repeatedly refines partial solutions over the entire 24-hour timeframe.
However, AIDE fell short in environments that required handling larger codebases or where a single improvement involved multiple steps of interaction.

Limitations

  • The Weco benchmark holdout differs from Kaggle's private test set, so leaderboard-percentile comparisons can have additional variance; competition-data contamination in the underlying LLMs also cannot be ruled out without live competitions.
  • The simple greedy policy can converge to local optima and repeat local patches instead of discovering new strategies.
  • AIDE struggles when tasks require navigating larger codebases or coordinating a single improvement over multiple interaction steps.

Open questions

  • Can uncertainty-aware, diversity-preserving, or non-greedy tree policies escape local optima without sacrificing AIDE's execution efficiency?
  • How should the topology represent multi-file state and coordinated, multi-step experiments while retaining atomic attribution of improvements?
  • Can evaluation on fresh live tasks measure contamination-resistant generalization and include end-to-end compute, execution, and inference costs?
