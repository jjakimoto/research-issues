Multi-Level Market Making with Reinforcement Learning

📰 Latest · 2026-08-24 Full Article
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-08-24

Source: arxiv · 2026-08-18
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18195

A fully-specified RL formulation for multi-level quoting: the action is a point on a simplex splitting M lots across market orders and limit orders at K price levels on both sides, fed by a deep-set encoder over the agent's own resting orders (level, queue position, size). It beats top-of-book and inventory-skew heuristics in all six (market, lot-size) cells with the gap widest where adverse selection is worst — but every number comes from the authors' own Poisson simulator, with no historical data, fees, rebates, or latency.

Executive summary

Cheridito and Weiss (ETH Zurich) formulate market making as a finite-horizon RL problem where the policy allocates a fixed budget of M lots across market orders and limit orders at multiple price levels on both sides of the book, solved with an actor-critic whose policy is a multivariate logistic-normal distribution on the action simplex. Two ideas carry the paper: a deep-set encoder that turns the variable-length set of resting orders into a permutation-invariant level-indexed representation, and potential-based reward shaping (mark-to-mid inventory revaluation) that densifies the learning signal without changing the optimal policy. Across three synthetic Poisson multi-agent markets, the learned policy dominates three heuristics in expected normalized cash flow in every cell, usually with lower variance. The behavioural analysis is the most convincing part: as adverse selection increases the policy voluntarily quotes deeper, cancels more, and keeps mean inventory pinned at zero.

Problem and the gap in prior RL market making

The stated gap is action-space poverty. Classical analytic market making (Ho-Stoll, Avellaneda-Stoikov, Gueant et al., Guilbaud-Pham, Cartea-Wang) models mid-price plus fill probabilities with a single limit buy and sell order, and prior RL work is similarly restricted to one order per side or a small discrete action menu. The closest richer approaches either control prices of a fixed number of orders at high computational cost (Abergel et al. 2022) or use a Beta distribution to spread limit volume across levels but post the same volume on both sides (Wang et al. 2024). The second motivating problem is evaluation: historical backtests require artificially injecting fills and cannot capture other participants reacting to the agent's quotes, so the authors train and test inside an interactive multi-agent simulator instead.

  • Three claimed contributions: a general state/action-space formulation, an actor-critic algorithm (logistic-normal policy + deep-set encoder + reward shaping), and experiments in three simulated markets exhibiting adverse selection.
  • State space includes the full visible book, the agent's own queue positions, and recent order flows — richer than the mid-price-plus-fill-probability abstraction.
  • Simulator built on Poisson order-flow modelling in the Cont-Stoikov-Talreja tradition.

RL formulation: state, action, reward

Discrete decision times over [0,T], with the agent allocating at most M lots at each step. Market state: best bid/ask, the first K entries of the bid and ask volume vectors, mid-price drift over the last interval, and three signed order-flow features. Private state: time, inventory, counts of resting buy/sell orders, the triples (level, queue position, size) for every resting order, and resting volume as fractions of M per level. The action lives in the simplex S^{2(K+1)}: one component is the fraction of M held back, two are market buy and market sell fractions, and the rest are limit-order fractions at successive ticks behind the best bid / best ask. Continuous fractions become integer lots by Hamilton apportionment, and resting orders are cancelled only when necessary (lowest priority first) so queue position is deliberately preserved.

  • Reward per step = (1/M) * [realised cash flows from fills + (Q_{n+1} p_{n+1} - Q_n p_n) - gamma * |Q_{n+1}|].
  • The mark-to-mid term telescopes over an episode, so it is exactly potential-based reward shaping (Ng et al. 1999) — denser credit assignment, unchanged optimal policy.
  • Terminal constraint |Q_{N+}| <= ceil(nu*M) forces liquidation via a market order that walks the book (main experiments use nu = 0, i.e. full flattening).
  • Transaction costs are implicit only: the spread paid by market orders and the cost of walking the book. No exchange fees, rebates, or latency.
  • Queue position enters the state explicitly, which is unusual and important — fill probability depends on both level and queue rank, and partial fills are supported.

Architecture: deep-set encoder, logistic-normal actor, critic

A padded fixed-length vector of order triples breaks a feed-forward net because reallocation reshuffles which slot holds which order. The fix is a deep-set encoder (Zaheer et al. 2017): a shared small network is applied to each resting order's (queue position, size) pair and the embeddings are averaged within each price level. Averaging discards total volume per level, which is why the redundant fractions-of-M feature is kept. The actor is a multivariate logistic-normal distribution: a network maps the encoded state to the mean of an underlying Gaussian, the covariance is a state-independent diagonal matrix, and the logistic transform maps to the simplex — with a closed-form density, so the policy-gradient score function is cheap. Initialising the output-layer bias to all-ones directly controls initial allocation and guards against the degenerate never-trade local optimum.

  • Encoder: 1 hidden layer, 2 nodes, ReLU. Actor mean network and critic: 2 hidden layers, 128 nodes, tanh.
  • Diagonal, state-independent policy covariance — exploration is not state-adaptive.
  • Combined actor+critic loss with weight c_V = 0.5, chosen over two separate gradient steps for compute efficiency.

Market simulator and experimental setup

Three nested environments. Noise traders submit market, limit and cancellation orders as independent Poisson processes. Tactical traders scale arrival intensities by instantaneous exponentially-weighted volume imbalance, producing short directional bursts. Strategic traders use an exponentially smoothed imbalance signal (beta = 0.1), producing persistent drifts. The book has D = 30 levels; the agent observes and quotes on K = 3 levels per side, giving a 9-component action simplex. Episodes run from -30s to T = 600s with decisions every 30s (N = 20 steps). When tactical and strategic traders are added, noise-trader intensities are cut by 40% to keep total flow comparable.

  • Metric is normalized cash flow, deliberately excluding the running inventory penalty so results are comparable across gamma.
  • 10,000 out-of-sample test episodes per cell, with separate random seeds for training and testing.
  • Training: 1,280 trajectories per gradient step, 800 gradient steps, Adam lr 0.0005, gamma = 0.01, on 128 CPUs and one 24 GB RTX GPU.
  • Benchmarks: TOP1 (M/2 lots at best bid and ask), TOP2 (M/2 lots one tick behind each best), INV (best prices skewed linearly by clipped inventory). All mirror the terminal liquidation and also avoid unnecessary cancellations.
  • Two lot budgets throughout: M = 2 and M = 20.

Results: cash flow, markouts, and what the policy actually does

The LN policy has the highest expected normalized cash flow in all six cells, and the lowest standard deviation in the four that matter most. The benchmark ordering is itself informative: in the pure-noise market TOP1 beats TOP2 and inventory skewing helps, but once strategic traders arrive at M=20 lots, TOP2 (one tick deeper) beats TOP1 and INV goes outright negative at -1.44, attributed to adverse executions incurred while skewing inventory. The markout analysis is run only on the fixed-placement benchmarks, precisely so the environment effect is not confounded with policy adaptation.

  • Largest gap: hardest market, M = 20 — LN 4.99 (sd 1.07) vs TOP2 2.05, TOP1 0.73, INV -1.44.
  • Deeper quoting under adverse selection: at M = 20, filled volume inserted at the third-best price rises 0.00% (noise) -> 2.10% (+tactical) -> 8.30% (+strategic); cancelled volume at the third level reaches 22.52%.
  • Fill rate falls as informed flow arrives: 59.65% -> 43.99% for M = 20; cancellation rate reaches 56.01%.
  • The policy essentially never crosses the spread voluntarily: market orders are 1.90%-2.74% of placed volume, driven almost entirely by the forced terminal liquidation.
  • Mean inventory is flat at ~0 throughout; M = 2 stays within about one lot, M = 20 bands reach about six lots.

Ablations and honest-evaluation assessment

Three ablations. Replacing the logistic-normal with a Dirichlet policy on the same simplex (same encoder, critic, hyperparameters) loses in all six cells and is catastrophically negative (-6.18) in the hardest cell where LN earns +4.99. The running inventory penalty behaves as the microstructure literature predicts — lower variance at lower mean — but the variance reduction is small in state-dependent markets, so the authors conclude a static inventory penalty is not an effective control there. Relaxing the terminal flatten from nu = 0 to nu = 0.5 raises expected cash flow in all six cells, which is simply the transaction cost of forced liquidation showing up in P&L. Every simulator parameter and the sample size are stated, so the experiment is reproducible in principle — but the benchmarks are all non-learning heuristics, no prior RL market maker is compared against, no multi-seed error bars are reported, and no code link appears.

  • LN vs Dirichlet is the most decisive ablation: same everything except the simplex distribution, and Dirichlet loses money in the hardest cell.
  • nu = 0 vs nu = 0.5 is effectively a measurement of forced-liquidation transaction cost, e.g. 5.16 -> 5.73 in the hardest market at M = 20.
  • Weakest link for a practitioner: static-heuristic baselines inside a simulator the authors also designed.

Key findings

  • The logistic-normal actor-critic has the highest expected normalized cash flow in all six (market, lot-size) cells; the gap is widest in the hardest environment at M = 20 lots, where LN earns 4.99 (sd 1.07) against TOP2 2.05, TOP1 0.73 and the inventory-skewing INV heuristic at -1.44.
  • Adverse selection is measured, not assumed: mean markout of TOP1's top-of-book fills at M = 20 degrades from +0.45 ticks in the noise-only market to -0.06 ticks once strategic traders are present, while quoting one tick deeper holds markouts at +0.59 to +0.90 ticks.
  • The learned policy responds as a microstructure practitioner would hope: at M = 20 the share of filled volume inserted at the third-best price rises 0.00% -> 2.10% -> 8.30%, the cancellation rate rises from 40.35% to 56.01%, and the fill rate falls from 59.65% to 43.99%.
  • The policy essentially never crosses the spread by choice — market orders are only 1.90% to 2.74% of total placed volume, driven almost entirely by the terminal liquidation mandated by nu = 0.
  • Inventory control is genuine: average inventory stays flat at approximately zero over every episode in all three markets, with deviations of the mean an order of magnitude smaller than the band width.
  • The choice of simplex distribution is decisive: with identical encoder, critic and hyperparameters, a Dirichlet policy underperforms in all six cells and turns loss-making (-6.18) where LN earns +4.99.
  • Relaxing the forced terminal flatten (nu = 0 -> 0.5) raises expected cash flow in all six cells with essentially unchanged variance, a direct measurement of the transaction cost of liquidating with spread-paying market orders.

The algorithm therefore does not generate its cash flows by leaning to one side of the market, but by quoting on both sides while keeping its inventory centered at zero.

Limitations

  • Every number comes from the authors' own synthetic Poisson multi-agent simulator — no historical LOB data, no real fills, no calibration to a venue. Transaction costs are only the spread plus walking the book: no exchange fees, maker rebates, latency, or competing market makers reacting to the agent's quotes.
  • The baselines are three static, non-learning heuristics. There is no comparison against an Avellaneda-Stoikov-style analytic quoter or any prior RL market-making method, so 'outperforms competitive benchmarks' is measured against a low bar.
  • Statistical rigour is one-sided: 10,000 out-of-sample episodes give tight test estimates, but each cell appears to use a single trained policy, with no multi-seed error bars, no confidence intervals, and no released code. Episodes are short and narrow (600s, 20 decisions, K = 3 levels).

Open questions

  • Does the state-dependent inventory risk parameter the authors propose actually beat the static gamma, and can it be learned jointly with the policy?
  • Does the policy survive contact with reality — training on historical or calibrated LOB data, and evaluation under fees, rebates, realistic latency and reacting competitors?
  • How does the deep-set-plus-logistic-normal architecture scale beyond K = 3 levels and to multiple correlated assets, and how much of the gain comes from the encoder versus the reward shaping versus the multi-level action space?
