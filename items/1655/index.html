    OpenScholar: Synthesizing Scientific Literature with Retrieval-augmented LMs

    Date: 2026-09-21

    Theme: Evidence-grounded autonomous research loops
    Autonomous research systems need both trustworthy literature synthesis and external empirical feedback. OpenScholar decomposes retrieval, citation verification, and iterative synthesis, while Robin carries literature-derived hypotheses into human-executed laboratory experiments and agent-assisted analysis, revealing both the promise and the verification burden of end-to-end research agents.
    Source: arxiv
    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.14199

    Citation-grounded retrieval, iterative synthesis, and rigorous evaluation make OpenScholar a foundational literature-reasoning component for autonomous research systems.

    Executive summary

    OpenScholar combines a 45-million-paper open-access datastore, scientific retriever and reranker, and a language model that iteratively improves literature-synthesis answers through retrieval-backed self-feedback and citation verification. The same pipeline generates filtered synthetic data for an open Llama 3.1 8B model, while ScholarQABench evaluates open-retrieval synthesis across computer science, biomedicine, neuroscience, and physics. The system substantially improves correctness and citation grounding over parametric LMs and standard RAG, although representative-paper retrieval, factual reliability, evaluation breadth, and access to protected literature remain open problems.

    Retrieval and synthesis architecture

    OpenScholar indexes 45 million open-access peS2o papers as 234 million 250-word passages. Candidate evidence comes from a continually pretrained Contriever bi-encoder, Semantic Scholar keyword searches, and academic web search; a scientific BGE cross-encoder reranker then ranks passages while limiting each paper to three passages and incorporating normalized citation counts.

    • The dense retriever returns the top 100 datastore passages before aggregation with API and web-search results.
    • The bi-encoder has 110 million parameters and the cross-encoder has 340 million parameters.
    • The generator receives five passages for single-paper tasks and ten for multi-paper tasks in the main experiments.

    Iterative self-feedback and specialized training

    After producing an initial cited response, the generator writes up to three natural-language critiques covering issues such as missing evidence, organization, and completeness. Feedback can trigger new retrieval queries; the response is revised iteratively and then subjected to post-hoc citation verification. To distill this behavior, the authors generate synthetic questions and pipeline traces, compare initial and final answers, filter for organization and factual/citation quality, and train a Llama 3.1 8B model on 130,135 mixed scientific and general-domain instances.

    • Synthetic question generation starts from one million sampled abstracts and selects 10,000 highly cited papers published after 2017.
    • Initial answers are preferred over iteratively revised answers about 20% of the time, showing that self-refinement can over-edit or add redundancy.
    • Training mixes answer generation, feedback generation, feedback incorporation, fact verification, single-paper QA, multi-paper QA, SciRIFF, and Tulu data.

    ScholarQABench evaluation design

    ScholarQABench combines adapted open-retrieval versions of SciFact, PubMedQA, and QASA with new expert-authored multi-paper tasks. Its new material comprises 2,967 questions and 208 long-form responses across four disciplines. Evaluation separates correctness, sentence-level citation precision and recall, coverage, relevance, organization, and expert-rated usefulness rather than collapsing literature synthesis into a single lexical metric.

    • ScholarQA-CS contains 100 questions with an average of 4.4 expert-written answer ingredients, each supported by an average of 4.4 source quotes.
    • ScholarQA-Multi contains 108 questions and citation-backed answers written by Ph.D.-level experts, who spent an average of 56 minutes per instance.
    • Citation F1 tests both whether citation-worthy claims are cited and whether cited passages actually support the associated statements.

    Automatic results and ablations

    On ScholarQA-CS, OS-8B reaches 51.1 correctness and 47.9 citation F1 versus GPT-4o's 45.0 and 0.1; OS-GPT4o reaches the highest correctness at 57.7. Parametric models fabricate most requested references, whereas retrieval-grounded OS-8B produces no nonexistent cited papers in the reported computer-science and biomedical samples. Ablations show especially large citation losses when reranking or post-hoc attribution is removed, and the specialized 8B model remains effective with more context than vanilla Llama 3.1 8B.

    • Removing reranking from OS-GPT4o lowers ScholarQA-CS correctness from 57.7 to 52.4 and citation F1 from 39.5 to 22.9.
    • Removing feedback from OS-GPT4o lowers correctness to 55.1 and citation F1 to 31.0; removing attribution lowers them to 55.6 and 30.6.
    • Vanilla Llama 3.1 8B deteriorates when context grows beyond ten passages, while the trained OS-8B remains strong through 20 passages.

    Expert evaluation and implications

    Experts compared GPT-4o, OS-8B, and OS-GPT4o with human-written answers on 108 questions. OS-GPT4o was preferred to the human answer in 70.0% of comparisons and rated useful in 80.0%; OS-8B won 50.8% and was useful in 72.1%. Coverage drove much of the preference, but the smaller model was less organized and fluent, and evaluators noted that retrieved citations could be outdated or less representative than canonical work.

    • A length-controlled study shortened OS-GPT4o answers to about 333 words; they were still preferred or tied with expert answers on 75% of 50 sampled queries.
    • OS-8B inference averaged 0.9 minutes, compared with 56.8 minutes for human-written answers in the reported answer-statistics table.
    • The work establishes literature retrieval, evidence attribution, iterative critique, and multifaceted evaluation as separable components for future autonomous-research agents.

    Key findings

    • A domain-specific retriever, reranker, iterative feedback loop, and citation verifier together outperform both retrieval-free LMs and simpler RAG pipelines on realistic literature-synthesis tasks.
    • OS-8B achieves 51.1 ScholarQA-CS correctness and 47.9 citation F1, compared with GPT-4o's 45.0 correctness and 0.1 citation F1.
    • In the paper's sampled reference lists, GPT-4o fabricated 78.7% of computer-science citations and 94.8% of biomedical citations, while OS-8B fabricated none.
    • Expert preference favors OS-GPT4o over human-written answers in 70.0% of comparisons and OS-8B in 50.8%, primarily because retrieved synthesis provides broader coverage.
    • Specialized training improves how a small model uses longer retrieved contexts; merely exposing an off-the-shelf long-context model to more passages can reduce both correctness and citation accuracy.

    Notable quotes

    Our datastore consists of 234 million passages. To our knowledge, this is the largest open-sourced datastore for scientific literature.
    We leverage our inference-time pipeline to synthetically generate high-quality training data through self-feedback, so that the resulting model can get better at generating higher-quality output without going through the self-feedback process (Figure 2 bottom).
    This suggests that even though LMs can process large numbers of passages, they may struggle to effectively use them without specialized training, particularly for smaller models.
    First, as highlighted by our expert annotators, OpenScholar does not consistently retrieve the most representative or relevant papers for certain queries.

    Limitations

    • The human-answer evaluation sets are small, concentrated in computer science, biomedicine, and physics, and may reflect annotator-specific expertise and temporal snapshots from 2024.
    • Automatic rubric weights are heuristic, LLM judges can have stylistic biases, and sentence-level citation scoring may penalize claims supported by citations in adjacent sentences.
    • OpenScholar can miss representative papers and emit unsupported claims; its corpus excludes license-protected papers, while the strongest GPT-4o configuration depends on a changing proprietary API.

    Open questions

    • Can retrieval combine semantic similarity with citation networks, recency, venue quality, and canonical-paper signals without amplifying popularity bias?
    • How should autonomous research systems incorporate restricted-access literature while respecting copyright, provenance, and reproducibility constraints?
    • Can stronger open generators and evidence-aware training close the fluency and factuality gap while preserving OS-8B's citation grounding and low cost?
