    Jacobian Rank Collapse in Decision-Focused Learning

    Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
    Date: 2026-10-04

    Source: arxiv · 2026-09-30
    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39261

    A useful check to run before paying for decision-focused learning in portfolio construction. If your covariance predictor is effectively one-parameter (a shrinkage intensity, which includes a 385-parameter MLP that outputs one scalar intensity), task-loss gradients are collinear with MSE gradients, and across 38 equity configurations DFL improved tracking error by less than 1.8% while costing about 3,300x more to train. Treat the larger wins with caution: the 7-16.5% neural DFL gains were measured against a trailing-reconstruction MSE target and disappeared in a matched forward-target control (5.711% vs 5.715% TE), and the SPO+ wins come from small synthetic tasks where only knapsack survives Holm correction.

    Executive summary

    The paper asks when decision-focused learning (DFL) can give a predictor a learning direction that MSE does not. Its answer is the predictor's parameter Jacobian: when that Jacobian has rank one, every nonzero per-example gradient (MSE or task) lies on the same line, though signs, minimizers and batch updates can still differ. Using sparse S&P 500 / multi-market index tracking with a differentiable QP, it finds that one-parameter covariance models gain less than 1.8% from DFL over MSE across 38 configurations. On synthetic shortest-path and knapsack tasks, full-capacity SPO+ cuts regret by about 11%. The paper is unusually candid about negatives: forward-target financial controls show that better covariance forecasts can worsen tracking, and a matched neural comparison shows no aggregate DFL advantage.

    Problem: where decision-relevant gradient information gets lost

    DFL backpropagates a downstream decision loss through an optimizer into the predictor. The authors separate three restrictions. (1) Output support: in sparse index tracking the QP reads only the selected block and the stock-index cross-covariance. (2) The predictor Jacobian J = d vec(Sigma)/d theta, which maps output-space gradients into parameter directions. (3) Batch aggregation across examples. Rank collapse rules out a new independent direction but not different signs, stationary points or decisions.

    • Two-stage problem: Stage 1 picks K of N assets (market-cap ranking by default, or a tracking score). Stage 2 solves a long-only, unit-sum tracking-error QP on the selected subset. Selection is detached from the gradient graph.
    • The QP reads at most 2NK - K^2 of the N^2 covariance entries (36% in their illustration). Off-block errors do not affect the solution or the regret (Decision Invariance, Prop. 3).
    • Support-energy bound (Prop. 2): under an isotropic normalized-energy assumption, E|cos(g_MSE, g_task)| <= sqrt(2K/N - K^2/N^2). The paper notes this is a reference value, not a measured cosine.

    Theory: rank-one Jacobians, spectral gaps and batch subspaces

    Prop. 4 (Jacobian Rank Collapse): if rank(J) = 1, the parameter-space cosine between the MSE and task gradients is +1 or -1, whatever the covariance-space angle. Theorem 1 gives the exact form, cos = cos(S U^T g_MSE, S U^T g_task). It also gives a near-rank-one bound, |cos| >= cos(Theta), built from sigma2/sigma1 and each gradient's alignment with the leading singular direction. Prop. 5 says batch gradients lie in range(J_stack^T): they are all collinear only if the stacked rank is one, so pointwise rank one is not enough.

    • Counterexamples: a scalar predictor with losses (f-1)^2 and (f-2)^2 has rank one but different minimizers. Two rank-one per-example Jacobians can produce orthogonal batch gradients (1,1) and (1,-1).
    • The 385-parameter conditional shrinkage model (alpha_t = sigmoid(MLP(z_t))) is numerically rank one at every measured input (median singular-value ratio about 1.2e-6), because its output passes through one scalar intensity. Parameter count is not rank.
    • Direct test of Theorem 1 on 162 points (N=97): no violations. The bound is informative at the 108 rank-one or near-rank-one points but vacuous at all 54 points of the 12-parameter structured model, because the task gradient is nearly orthogonal to the leading singular direction u1.
    • Numerical stress check: 5,000 sampled Jacobians. The alignment identity holds to 4.2e-14, with no bound or sign violations in the 3,926 non-vacuous cases.

    Equity experimental setup

    Primary universe: the top 100 S&P 500 stocks by market cap (N=97 after a coverage filter), daily returns 2006-2025. Rolling protocol: 3-year train, 6-month validation, 1-year test, giving 9 folds over 2016-2025. Features: 21-day volatility, 63-day momentum, sector and market-cap quintile. Scale-ups: full S&P 500 (N=478, 5 folds), S&P 1500 (N=1258, 8 folds) and a 20-year backtest (17 folds). Cross-market: FTSE 100, Nikkei 225, Euro Stoxx 50, ASX 200 and Hang Seng, plus Russell 2000 (S&P 600 proxy) and the S&P 500 Tech sector.

    • Models: Shrinkage (1 parameter, Ledoit-Wolf form), Factor, Neural (MLP -> LL^T + D), Structured (12 parameters, per-sector targets) and Conditional (385 parameters). Each is trained under both MSE and DFL.
    • MSE target: Frobenius loss against the trailing 63-day sample covariance. This is a reconstruction target, not a future-covariance label, and the authors flag it as a weak baseline.
    • Task loss: annualized mean squared tracking difference over H=21 forward days plus an L1 turnover penalty. Gradients go through KKT conditions via cvxpylayers. Optimizer: Adam (lr 1e-3, weight decay 1e-4, clip 1.0), with early stopping on validation tracking error.
    • Baselines: Naive, K-only LW, Ledoit-Wolf, POET, GraphicalLasso, ValTuned (grid search), SPO+, LODL and MSE-trained models. Metric: annualized TE. Tests: Wilcoxon signed-rank across folds, and TOST with a 20 bps margin for equivalence claims.

    Equity results: small one-parameter gains; architecture matters more than the loss

    At N=100, ValTuned, SPO+ and DFL meet the 20 bps TOST criterion in all 12 pairwise comparisons, and each improves on Ledoit-Wolf by 7-19%. LODL also matches DFL. Across all 38 one-parameter configurations, gains lie in [-0.57%, +1.76%] (median 0.25%) over K/N in [0.008, 0.43], with no monotone link to sparsity (rho = -0.16, p = 0.35). At N=478, moving from MSE to DFL improves TE by 0.68% / 1.07% / 1.34% for the 1-, 12- and 385-parameter models. By contrast, changing architecture (MSE-trained conditional predictor vs POET) gives 9.8% lower TE.

    • Cost: one-parameter DFL takes about 13 min per fold vs 0.23 s for POET+QP, a ratio of about 3,329x (804x for the structured model). Measured on a single Apple machine.
    • The 12-parameter structured model gains more in some markets: ASX +2.95%, Hang Seng +2.77%, S&P 1500 +2.41% (p <= 0.008). Euro Stoxx, Nikkei and FTSE gain less than 1%.
    • The neural model with the trailing-reconstruction MSE baseline shows much larger DFL gains: -8.8%, -9.4%, -16.5% and -7.0% TE at K = 5/10/20/30 (p <= 0.014), but only -0.2% at K=50. The K=20 gain peaks in 2021-2022 (-40.0%).
    • Dynamic tracking-score selection: pure neural DFL worsens TE by 8.6% at K=10 (N=100), while BD-DFL (DFL plus a relative MSE penalty, beta=0.001) improves it by 8.2%. With corrected harness at N=451, K=10: pure DFL -13.0%, BD-DFL -15.6% (p=0.0024).
    • Transaction costs: DFL trades about 15x more, but at c=100 bps the added cost is only 3-4 bps, against a 49-57 bps TE reduction.

    Controls beyond finance and forecast-vs-decision tests

    Synthetic setup: PyEPO generators produce a 4x4 shortest-path grid (24 edges) and a 12-item 2-D knapsack, with ten independent datasets each (512/256/512 split). Every capacity starts from a shared ridge initializer, with separate learning-rate selection per loss and capacity. Full-capacity SPO+ reduces regret by 11.59% (path) and 10.59% (knapsack); after Holm correction over eight tests, only knapsack is significant (p_H = 0.0156 vs 0.0684). At 1, 2 and 8 update directions, changes range from -0.61% to +1.89%. Holding expressivity fixed, an invertible coordinate scaling with epsilon from 1 to 0.01 cuts ordinary SGD gains from 11.79% to 1.19% (path) and from 10.17% to 0.52% (knapsack). Compensating the update by D_eps^-2 restores the original trajectories exactly, so this is a coordinate/conditioning effect, not lost expressivity.

    • Fresh-data sensitivity (three minibatch orders, 20/80 epochs, a no-update option allowed): full-capacity gains are 12.12/12.54% (path) and 13.67/13.76% (knapsack). Scalar-capacity gains stay below 0.6%.
    • Forward-target scalar control (frozen 100-stock universe, 10 annual folds 2016-2025, K=20): future-target MSE improves covariance error by 37.25% but worsens mean TE by 4.13%. Task-validated alpha improves TE by 1.46%; Ledoit-Wolf by 0.69%.
    • Matched 197-parameter residual neural covariance network with 21-day forward labels: mean TE 5.711% (MSE) vs 5.715% (DFL), so DFL is 0.07% worse despite winning 6 of 10 years. Validation kept the unchanged initializer in 17/30 MSE fits and 12/30 DFL fits.
    • Archived PyEPO capacity sweep: gain rises with sampled stacked rank (Spearman rho = 0.93), but the equity-calibrated threshold does not transfer. perturbedOpt is significantly worse than two-stage at intermediate capacities (as low as -30.3%).

    Significance and honest-negative framing

    The paper is a careful negative and scope-limiting result rather than a DFL win. Rank one restricts local learning directions but does not decide test performance; held-out decision quality is still the only test of practical benefit. The authors list eight unresolved findings, including that no measured quantity explains why Hang Seng and ASX exceed 1% at d=1. They also disclose that AI-assisted auditing with Claude and Codex found evaluation-harness defects: validation holdings could extend into test dates. Correcting these changed some conclusions; for example, the N=451, K=10 comparison now favors pure DFL.

    • Practitioner recipe from the paper: first build a predictive baseline whose labels match the intended forecast horizon. Then compare objectives within the same architecture, initialization and validation budget, letting both losses keep an unchanged baseline.
    • For quant shops: with scalar-shrinkage covariance models, validation grid search on TE (ValTuned) matches DFL at a tiny fraction of the cost.
    • For autonomous-researcher builders: this is a worked example of an agent-audited paper that narrows its claims, reports provenance, and keeps failed predictions in the record.

    Key findings

    • Across 38 one-parameter equity configurations (6 markets, N from 47 to 1258, K/N 0.008-0.43), DFL gains over MSE lie in [-0.57%, +1.76%] with median 0.25%. There is no association with sparsity (rho = -0.16, p = 0.35), and one-parameter DFL costs about 3,329x POET's per-fold wall-clock.
    • A 385-parameter conditional shrinkage predictor is pointwise rank one (median sigma2/sigma1 about 1.2e-6), so parameter count does not measure available learning directions. At N=478 its MSE-trained version beats POET by 9.8% TE, while switching to DFL adds only 1.34%.
    • Full-capacity SPO+ reduces test regret by 11.59% on shortest path and 10.59% on knapsack, but only knapsack passes Holm correction (p_H = 0.0156). Low-capacity (1-8 direction) changes range from -0.61% to +1.89%.
    • At fixed expressivity, invertible coordinate scaling (epsilon from 1 to 0.01) cuts SPO+ gains from 11.79% to 1.19% (path) and from 10.17% to 0.52% (knapsack). D_eps^-2-compensated SGD restores the full gains, which points to an optimization-coordinate effect.
    • Forecast accuracy is not decision quality: future-target MSE improves covariance error by 37.25% but worsens tracking error by 4.13%. A matched 197-parameter neural MSE-vs-DFL comparison gives 5.711% vs 5.715% TE, with no aggregate DFL gain.
    • The neural DFL gains of 7.0-16.5% at K <= 30 (vs a trailing-reconstruction MSE target) were not reproduced by the matched forward-target neural control. The authors cannot attribute the gap to any single factor (architecture, support, target or protocol).

    Notable quotes

    A pointwise rank-one measurement does not establish a one-dimensional training problem.
    These descriptive results expose a forecast-to-decision gap rather than establish DFL superiority.
    Thus this neural control does not reproduce the larger historical DFL gains.
    Decision losses and predictors impose distinct restrictions: output support determines which errors a decision observes, the predictor Jacobian maps gradients into parameter directions, and stacking determines the batch subspace.

    Limitations

    • The historical equity MSE branch reconstructs the trailing 63-day covariance (for shrinkage, its own input) rather than forecasting future covariance. The forward-target controls use a frozen, survivorship-biased 100-stock universe, omit transaction costs, and are descriptive only.
    • Statistical power is limited. Chronological folds share history, 5-fold tests have a p-value floor of 0.031, and many comparisons are unadjusted. The synthetic tasks are tiny (4x4 grid, 12-item knapsack) with linear predictors, and changing capacity also changes expressivity, so rank is not causally identified.
    • Historical real-data training was not independently re-run in the release. Earlier results came from an evaluation harness that leaked validation holdings into test windows, and the archived 33-case diagnostic predates the corrections.

    Open questions

    • Why do Hang Seng and ASX 200 exceed a 1% one-parameter DFL gain when heterogeneity, sparsity and universe size do not explain it?
    • Can Theorem 1 be sharpened for task gradients that are nearly orthogonal to the leading singular direction, where it is currently vacuous (e.g., the 12-parameter structured model)?
    • Does neural DFL beat a horizon-aligned MSE forecaster under broader architectures, longer training and point-in-time index membership? How does stacked, trajectory-level Jacobian geometry relate to out-of-sample decision quality?
