    Mixture-of-Experts for Cryptocurrency Order Execution: Training Stability, Tail Risk, and Failure Modes

    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03369

    This is a careful negative result. Across 100 seeds per model, mixture-of-experts DDQL execution agents do not beat vanilla DDQL, and none of the learned agents beats TWAP. MoE's apparent 'stability' gain came from a training-specification bug (exploration that never anneals), and plain annealing reproduces it. It is a good example of why execution-RL claims need repeated-seed evaluation, matched-capacity controls and schedule baselines.

    Executive summary

    The authors test K-means-routed mixtures of Double Deep Q-Learning experts (K=2/4/8) against vanilla DDQL and parameter-matched dense nets (cap4/cap8). They liquidate 20 BTC over 10 five-minute bars in a frictionless replay of Binance BTC/USDT 10-level LOB data, with 100 training seeds per configuration on 500 fixed out-of-sample windows. No learned model significantly beats DDQL on mean implementation shortfall, and all of them lose to TWAP (0.39 bps) and immediate liquidation (0.21 bps). MoE's one clear effect is that it removes a degenerate 'never-trade' collapse (11/100 DDQL runs). A specification decomposition shows annealed exploration alone gets the same result, and the reward, exploration and replay changes interact non-additively.

    Problem and framing

    Deep RL execution policies vary a lot across training seeds, so a claimed architectural gain may just be a lucky seed. The paper treats MoE (following Li et al. 2024) as a way to spread learning across experts and evaluates it for training stability rather than as a mean-cost improvement.

    • 100 independently trained runs per configuration, all evaluated on the same 500 deterministic OOS episodes (paired comparisons).
    • Capacity-matched dense controls (widths 64 and 93, within 5% of the K=4/K=8 parameter budgets).
    • The authors explicitly disclose negative results: a fixed router-lag bug, a fill-price floor that never binds, and an attribution that flipped between 30 and 100 seeds.

    Data, environment and method

    Binance BTC/USDT LOB data from Kaggle covering Jan 9-20, 2023: train on Jan 9-14, evaluate on Jan 16-20. Snapshots are mean-aggregated into 5-minute bars, so each bar's book is time-averaged. The agent sells 20 units over 10 steps with market orders that walk the 10-level bid book. In the final two steps fills start from the fifth-best bid, which acts as an imposed urgency penalty. Replay is frictionless: the agent's trades have no impact on later prices.

    • DDQL setup follows Macri and Lillo: 5 layers, 30 hidden units, LeakyReLU, Adam lr 1e-4, buffer 15,000 that drops its oldest half on overflow, gamma = 1.0.
    • Epsilon schedule is x0.9995 per 100 actions with a 0.01 floor, but it never actually anneals (final epsilon about 0.88). Exploration draws are Binomial, so they are TWAP-like on average.
    • The router fits K-means on 4 episode features (realised vol, cost proxy, schedule imbalance, and an impact ratio that is constant, so routing is effectively 3-D). Each episode is routed using the PREVIOUS episode's features.
    • Episode start bars are sampled independently, so the lagged routing features carry no information about the current episode. By construction the router cannot condition on regime; it only splits data across experts.

    Main 100-seed results

    No configuration improves mean IS over DDQL. All paired bootstrap CIs straddle zero, and the K=8 CI still allows differences of about ±0.1 bps, roughly 10% of the mean, so equivalence is not established either. K=8 has the lowest across-seed dispersion (SD 0.37 vs 0.50), but the effect is only significant unadjusted (Brown-Forsythe p=0.034), fails Holm correction (p_adj=0.17) and is not monotone in K. Within-policy tail risk moves the opposite way and gets worse as K grows.

    • Per-run CVaR-95 vs DDQL: +5.4 (p=0.18), +9.9 (p=0.023), +11.5 (p=0.006) bps at K=2/4/8. cap8 is -6.6 (p=0.21), directional only.
    • The authors attribute the tail deterioration to per-expert data dilution (K=8 gives about 312 of 2,500 episodes per expert).
    • Collapse to never-trade: 25 of 600 runs (11 DDQL, 3 MoE K=2, 7 cap4, 4 cap8, 0 for K>=4). Fisher p=0.0007 for DDQL vs K=4 and vs K=8.
    • Removing the collapsed DDQL runs does not shrink dispersion: SD goes from 0.50 to 0.51.

    Why the baselines win: environment artifacts

    Early liquidation costs almost nothing here. The ten visible levels span a median of only 0.60 bps, so going past the displayed book costs about 0.067 bps per extrapolated level, and the 50%-of-mid fill floor never binds (zero difference across 90,000 evaluations for floor values of 30/70/90%). The training reward (per-step slippage vs the current mid) is not the evaluation metric (shortfall vs the arrival price), so a never-trade policy earns zero reward until a single terminal penalty.

    • 46% of bars show less depth than the order size, so overflowing the book is common. It is just priced as almost free.
    • The authors disclose a device effect: CPU and CUDA runs differ on 61% of values, and per-run means shift by up to 1.58 bps. Seeds reproduce results on the same device but not across devices.
    • The 500 windows are heterogeneous: TWAP is +0.39 bps on the full set but -1.66 bps on the first 120 episodes.

    Specification decomposition

    The authors retrain DDQL, MoE K=8 and cap8 on CPU against a device-matched baseline, changing one or more of: reward alignment, epsilon annealing, and replay retention. Annealing alone removes collapse: DDQL goes from 12/100 to 0/100 (exact McNemar p=4.9e-4) and cap8 from 7/100 to 0/100 (p=0.016). It also pushes every configuration below TWAP. Adding the aligned reward on top of annealing brings collapse back (19/30), and applying all three changes gives 48/100. No MoE K=8 run collapses under any of the six specifications.

    • The authors warn that the near-zero IS under annealing (K=8 at 0.01 bps) is essentially arrival-price execution, which is only possible because the replay is frictionless. They call it 'a different diagnostic, not a better testbed'.
    • K=8's dispersion edge narrows under annealing (0.42 vs 0.44), and cap8 becomes lowest at 0.32.
    • At 30 seeds the annealing effect was not significant. The attribution only became clear at 100 seeds.

    Key findings

    • No MoE or capacity-matched model significantly beats DDQL on mean IS. K=8 vs DDQL is -0.03 bps (95% CI [-0.15,+0.09]), and equivalence is not established either.
    • Under the as-reported specification, TWAP (0.39 bps, CVaR-95 74.0) and immediate liquidation (0.21 bps) beat every learned agent (0.95-1.15 bps, CVaR-95 104-122).
    • 11/100 DDQL runs collapse to a never-trade policy (IS 1.47 bps), against 0/100 for MoE K=4 and K=8 (Fisher p=0.0007). Annealed exploration alone also gets DDQL from 12/100 to 0/100 (McNemar p=4.9e-4).
    • Within-policy tail risk gets worse as K grows: CVaR-95 is +11.5 bps vs DDQL at K=8 (p=0.006). K=8's lower across-seed SD (0.37 vs 0.50) fails Holm adjustment (p_adj=0.17).
    • Strong non-additive interaction: annealing plus an aligned reward brings collapse back to 19/30 DDQL runs, and all three changes give 48/100. Changing the reward alone pushes cap8 to 21/30.
    • The fill-price floor never binds (zero difference across 90,000 evaluations), and the 10 visible levels span only a median 0.60 bps, so executing past the displayed book is nearly free in this simulator.

    Notable quotes

    Expert partitioning reduces across-seed dispersion at a real cost in within-policy tails; reporting only run-level SD and calling it “risk reduction” overstates what the mechanism delivers.
    This is a simulator limitation rather than a robustness result: executing beyond the displayed book is very nearly free here, which is part of why immediate liquidation is hard to beat.
    Expert partitioning is therefore not necessary to suppress this failure under the as-reported reward; its apparent stability benefit can be reproduced by annealing alone.
    The correct reading remains diagnostic: the architectures reshape the distribution of training outcomes, while the training specification determines whether any of them clears a schedule baseline.

    Limitations

    • The environment is a toy for execution: frictionless replay with no self-impact, a 5-minute mean-aggregated book rather than instantaneous snapshots, bid side only, one instrument over two weeks (one evaluation week), and a single fixed draw of 500 windows. That draw is heterogeneous: TWAP scores +0.39 bps overall but -1.66 bps on the first 120 episodes. The across-run CIs reflect seed variance only, not uncertainty over market periods.
    • The training reward differs from the evaluation metric, epsilon never anneals, and the router cannot use regime information by construction. These are self-inflicted design choices, so the MoE test does not fairly evaluate genuine regime-conditioned MoE. VWAP cannot be evaluated because the data has no volume series.
    • The decomposition is incomplete: it covers 3 of 6 configurations, half the arms use only 30 seeds, two of the three two-change arms were not run, and the variance-test families were defined after the analysis (descriptive, not confirmatory). Seeds do not reproduce results across CPU and CUDA.

    Open questions

    • Does any MoE stability or tail effect survive in an environment with endogenous impact, such as ABIDES or JAX-LOB, or in live paper trading, where arrival-price execution cannot be reached for free?
    • Would contemporaneous (non-lagged) routing features, or time-step-level soft gating, give real regime conditioning, and would that change the mean-cost result?
    • What explains the reward-by-exploration interaction? Why does aligning the reward with the evaluation metric bring the never-trade collapse back once exploration anneals?
