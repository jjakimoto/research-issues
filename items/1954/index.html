    DSPy: Compiling Declarative Language Model Calls into Self-Improving Pipelines

    ← Go backDocVQA: A Dataset for VQA on Document ImagesNextAutoGen: Enabling Next-Gen LLM Applications via Multi-Agent… →
    📚 Foundations · 2026-10-03 Full Article
    Read full article →View on GitHub

    Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
    Date: 2026-10-03

    Theme: Programmable and optimizable agent systems
    These works replace ad hoc prompt chains with software abstractions: AutoGen represents workflows as conversations among specialized agents, while DSPy represents language-model pipelines as declarative modules compiled against explicit metrics. They are foundations for coding and research agents, but their evaluations also expose unresolved questions about matched-budget baselines, verifier quality, and operational reliability.
    Source: arxiv
    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03714

    Establishes declarative, optimizable LM pipelines as a foundation for programmable agentic coding and autonomous-research systems.

    Executive summary

    DSPy replaces hand-written prompt strings with natural-language signatures, composable modules, and metric-driven optimizers called teleprompters. Its compiler bootstraps successful multi-stage traces, selects demonstrations or fine-tunes module-specific models, and can optimize higher-order structures such as ensembles. Case studies on GSM8K and HotPotQA show that concise compiled programs substantially improve GPT-3.5 and Llama2-13b-chat and can distill a multi-hop pipeline into T5-Large.

    Problem and design objective

    The paper argues that manually tuned prompt templates are brittle across models, tasks, domains, and interacting pipeline stages. DSPy instead represents LM systems as imperative text-transformation graphs whose behavior is specified declaratively and optimized against an explicit metric.

    • The target abstraction resembles neural-network programming: users compose reusable operators while an optimizer learns their parameters.
    • DSPy programs retain ordinary Python control flow, including loops, branches, and exceptions.
    • The evaluation asks whether concise modules can replace expert prompt strings without sacrificing quality or expressive power.

    Programming model: signatures and modules

    A signature declares input and output fields, plus optional instructions, describing what a text transformation should accomplish rather than the exact prompt used. Modules such as Predict, ChainOfThought, ProgramOfThought, MultiChainComparison, and ReAct implement reusable prompting strategies over arbitrary signatures; retrieval and other tools can be composed with them in define-by-run pipelines.

    • Module parameters include the LM, prompt instructions and field prefixes, and demonstrations.
    • Predict formats calls, parses structured output fields, and records traces during compilation.
    • The same signature can be paired with different modules, allowing direct prediction, reasoning, reflection, or tool use without rewriting a task-specific prompt.

    Compiler and teleprompters

    A teleprompter receives a program, training inputs, and a metric, then returns an optimized program. The compiler generates candidate instructions or demonstrations from successful teacher traces, selects candidates through random or Optuna-style search, or uses demonstrations to fine-tune module-specific LMs; a higher-order stage can transform control flow through ensembles.

    • BootstrapFewShot runs a teacher or zero-shot student and retains multi-stage traces whose final predictions satisfy the metric.
    • Intermediate labels are unnecessary because successful end-to-end traces supply candidate demonstrations for every Predict module.
    • Teacher composition allows an expensive compiled ensemble to supervise a cheaper student, including from inputs without final-answer labels.
    • Dynamic test-time bootstrapping and automatic backtracking-like control flow are proposed as future compiler extensions.

    GSM8K case study

    The authors compare direct prediction, chain-of-thought, and a reflection program that samples five reasoning chains and produces a final answer through MultiChainComparison. Using 200 training examples, 300 development examples, and the official test set, bootstrap compilation improves every program across GPT-3.5 and Llama2-13b-chat.

    • For vanilla prediction, bootstrap×2 raises test accuracy from 25.2% to 61.7% for GPT-3.5 and from 9.4% to 36.5% for Llama2-13b-chat.
    • The bootstrapped reflection program reaches 76.0% GPT-3.5 and 40.2% Llama2-13b-chat test accuracy.
    • A GPT-3.5 chain-of-thought ensemble reaches 81.6%, while a Llama2 reflection ensemble reaches 46.9%.
    • The programs use two to four DSPy modules and teleprompters rather than task-specific templates.

    HotPotQA case study

    On open-domain HotPotQA, the study compares vanilla answering, one-shot retrieval-augmented generation, ReAct, and a custom two-hop pipeline that generates retrieval queries before answering from accumulated context. Compilation is especially effective for the multi-hop program, and a teacher ensemble is used to fine-tune a 770M-parameter T5-Large student.

    • The bootstrapped multi-hop pipeline obtains answer exact match of 39.6% with GPT-3.5 and 36.4% with Llama2-13b-chat, versus 31.2% and 31.3% for its few-shot form.
    • Its ensembles reach 45.6% exact match for GPT-3.5 on half of the test sample and 41.0% for Llama2-13b-chat.
    • Compiled T5-Large scores 39.3% answer exact match and 46.0% passage accuracy on development data using 200 labeled and 800 unlabeled questions.
    • The results illustrate that program structure, retrieval behavior, and optimization strategy must be reported alongside the underlying LM.

    Significance and scope

    DSPy reframes prompt engineering as programming plus optimization: researchers specify modular transformations, control flow, supervision metrics, and tools, while the compiler adapts execution details to a model and dataset. This directly applies to autonomous-research and coding agents whose multi-step loops can be improved from end-to-end outcomes instead of maintained as interdependent prompt strings.

    • The abstraction separates stable system intent from model-specific invocation details.
    • Metric-driven trace selection provides a concrete mechanism for self-improving multi-stage agents.
    • The paper demonstrates the concept on two benchmark families; broader claims about reliability, developer productivity, and safety remain unevaluated.

    Key findings

    • Declarative signatures and generic modules can replace task-specific prompt strings while preserving arbitrary Python composition and tool use.
    • Successful end-to-end traces can bootstrap demonstrations for unlabeled intermediate steps, making multi-stage pipelines trainable from final-output metrics.
    • Bootstrap compilation substantially improves both evaluated models on GSM8K and HotPotQA.
    • Teacher composition can transfer a compiled multi-hop pipeline into a locally deployable 770M-parameter T5-Large model.
    • The relevant experimental unit becomes the LM together with a program and compilation strategy, rather than the LM alone.

    Notable quotes

    A key source of DSPy’s expressive power is its ability to compile—or automatically optimize—any program in this programming model.
    While LMs can be highly unreliable, we find they can be rather efficient at searching the space of solutions for multi-stage designs.
    Perhaps most importantly, we can make llama2-13b-chat competitive with GPT-3.5 by simply compiling our programs.
    In the interest of space and to maintain reasonable scope in this paper, we leave reporting on such tasks under controlled experimental conditions to future work.

    Limitations

    • Controlled experiments cover only GSM8K and HotPotQA; the paper explicitly leaves rigorous evaluation of other observed applications to future work.
    • The evaluation focuses on reducing expert-written prompts rather than computational efficiency, developer efficiency, operational reliability, or safety.
    • Some evidence has dataset-specific constraints: HotPotQA testing repurposes its validation set, and the GPT-3.5 multi-hop ensemble is evaluated on only half of the test sample due to cost.

    Open questions

    • How reliably do metric-filtered traces bootstrap complex agents when initial programs rarely succeed or metrics are noisy, gameable, or incomplete?
    • Can compiler optimization extend beyond demonstrations and ensembles to robust instruction search, dynamic adaptation, backtracking, and control-flow synthesis?
    • Do DSPy’s gains persist in controlled evaluations of long-running coding and autonomous-research agents with state, tool failures, safety constraints, and distribution shift?
