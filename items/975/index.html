Do LLMs Know a Good Hypothesis When They See One? Logit-Based Energy Scoring Outperforms Prompted LLM-as-Judge for Scientific Hypothesis Ranking

📰 Latest · 2026-08-24 Full Article
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-08-24

Source: arxiv · 2026-08-18
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17270

A cheap, mechanistic selector beats the LLM-as-judge: scoring a candidate hypothesis by a model's own logits over the hypothesis tokens reaches 33.0% Hit@1 pooled (53.1% for the best model-scorer pairing) against 16.6% for GPT-5 prompted to rank the same 16 candidates, across 1,323 papers. In head-to-head comparisons GPT-5-as-judge picked the right hypothesis 37.4% of the time -- worse than a coin flip -- while a 1B Llama scored by raw target-logit energy got 82.2%.

Executive summary

Researchers at Oak Ridge National Laboratory test whether an LLM can pick out the hypothesis a paper's authors actually pursued, given only the paper's background survey and research question. They reuse ResearchBench (1,323 papers, 12 disciplines, each with 1 gold hypothesis and 15 expert-curated distractors) and compare two model-intrinsic scorers -- mean per-token softmax NLL, and mean negated raw target logit ('energy') -- against a zero-shot listwise GPT-5 judge. Intrinsic scoring wins decisively in aggregate (Hit@1 0.331 NLL / 0.328 Raw vs 0.166 prompted; Hit@5 0.621 / 0.594 vs 0.417), and the single best cell is the SMALLEST model in the study, Llama 3.2 1B under the raw-logit criterion (Hit@1 0.531, MRR 0.641, pairwise 82.2%). The authors are unusually careful about their own result: they flag 53.1% as a post-hoc maximum over 14 model-by-scorer combinations, note that Raw is not uniformly better than NLL (Gemma 2 2B collapses to chance under it), and warn that expert-written gold hypotheses may simply be more fluent than the distractors.

The task: discrimination, not generation -- and where the ground truth comes from

The paper steps back from 'can an LLM invent a hypothesis' to 'given the background and research question that motivate a study, can a model recognize which of several candidate hypotheses is the one that domain experts actually pursued', which it calls distinct from and arguably prerequisite to novel generation. The dataset is NOT built by the authors: they take ResearchBench, where each instance is one published paper decomposed into a background survey, the paper's research question, and a pool of 16 candidate hypotheses of which exactly one is the gold hypothesis. Ground truth is therefore 'the hypothesis this published paper actually pursued', and the 15 distractors were curated by the ResearchBench authors after publication.

  • 1,323 papers, 12 disciplines: Astronomy, Biology, Business, Cell Biology, Chemistry, Earth Science, Energy Science, Environmental Science, Law, Material Science, Math, Physics.
  • 16 candidates per paper => random Hit@1 = 6.25%, random pairwise accuracy = 50%.
  • Metrics: Hit@k for k in {1,2,3,5} and MRR over the gold hypothesis's rank; 95% percentile CIs from 2,000 bootstrap resamples.
  • Memorization check: the two best models (Llama 3.2 1B and 3B) have a December 2023 data cutoff, before the papers were published; models with LATER cutoffs did worse -- the pattern runs opposite to what memorization would predict.

The scorer: two logit energies over an isolated hypothesis span

Each candidate is scored independently (no comparison, no sampling) with a fixed template: 'Background: ... Research Question: ... Hypothesis: [candidate]'. One forward pass gives next-token logits at every position; only positions in the hypothesis span count, because the background and research question are identical across all 16 candidates and would add shared noise. The span is located by tokenizing the fixed prefix and using its token length as an offset. Two token-level scores are derived from the same logits: the normalized softmax NLL, and the unnormalized raw target-logit energy. Each is averaged over the span to give one scalar per candidate; the unnormalized sum is also reported as a length-sensitivity check. Both are lower-is-better. The only difference between the two criteria is the partition term -- NLL divides it out, Raw retains it -- which is why Raw is sensitive to logit scale and sharpness.

  • Cost: exactly one forward pass per candidate, 16 per paper, no sampling and no extra model calls; deterministic given the weights, and linear in the number of candidates.
  • Open-weight models scored: Llama 3.2 1B, Llama 3.2 3B, Gemma 2 2B, Gemma 4 12B, Mistral 7B, Phi-4 (14B), GPT OSS 20B -- 7 models x 2 scorers = 14 combinations.
  • Hardware: shared node with NVIDIA A100-SXM4 80GB GPUs; PyTorch 2.6.0 / CUDA 12.4, Transformers 5.12.1.
  • No fine-tuning, no reference hypothesis, no embeddings -- the signal is purely the model's own next-token distribution over the candidate text.

The LLM-as-judge baseline it is compared against

GPT-5 (via the Azure API, logits not exposed) gets one zero-shot listwise prompt per paper containing the background, the research question, and the indexed list of all 16 candidates, and is told to rank them and return ONLY a JSON object with the top-5 indices. The authors chose top-5 rather than a full ordering deliberately, reasoning that selecting the most plausible subset is more tractable than exhaustively ranking the pool. Because everything outside the top 5 lands in one tied terminal rank, MRR is omitted for GPT-5 and only Hit@k for k <= 5 is reported; its Hit@5 is literally its top-5 inclusion rate (41.7%, 552 of 1,323 papers). Its rank mass is 16.6% / 6.6% / 4.8% / 5.7% / 8.0% at ranks 1-5, with the remaining 58.3% of papers at the terminal rank.

  • One prompt, one proprietary model, one prompting strategy -- the authors list this as a limitation of the comparison.
  • The judge is instructed to reason about relative merit and emit a structured ranking; the intrinsic scorer is never told a ranking task is happening.

Results: intrinsic confidence beats prompted ranking, and size does not help

Pooled over all seven open-weight models, NLL gets Hit@1 0.331 [0.312, 0.352] and Raw gets 0.328 [0.314, 0.342]; GPT-5 prompted gets 0.166 [0.146, 0.186]. Hit@5: 0.621 / 0.594 vs 0.417. Every NLL-scored model beats the prompted baseline, and every Raw-scored model does too except Gemma 2 2B. The best single configuration is Llama 3.2 1B under Raw (Hit@1 0.531, Hit@5 0.774, MRR 0.641) -- the smallest model in the study beating a far larger proprietary judge. In pairwise (gold-vs-one-distractor) terms the contrast is starker: NLL sits in a tight 68.8-74.3% band across all seven models, Llama 3.2 1B under Raw hits 82.2%, and GPT-5 listwise gets 37.4%, i.e. BELOW the 50% a random ordering would achieve.

  • Biggest per-discipline gaps favour intrinsic scoring where prompting collapses: Earth Science Hit@1 0.421 (NLL) vs 0.046 (GPT-5); Chemistry 0.345 vs 0.065; Environmental Science 0.381 vs 0.081; Business 0.439 vs 0.130.
  • GPT-5 is nominally best only in Physics and Material Science, which the authors explicitly refuse to call a finding at N ~ 110-125 per discipline.
  • The NLL-vs-Raw difference pooled (0.3315 vs 0.3276 Hit@1) is inside the margin of a single estimate at N = 1,323; variance ACROSS MODELS is far larger than variance across scorers.
  • Prompted ranking is a strictly harder pathway: it needs the model to hold 16 candidates in context, follow the instruction, and externalize a judgment that may not track its own internal plausibility estimate.

Where it breaks: model-dependence, a post-hoc maximum, and low cross-model agreement

The authors foreground rather than bury the non-uniformity. Raw substantially helps the three small models, degrades Gemma 2 2B (Hit@1 0.352 under NLL down to 0.135 under Raw; pairwise 73.2% down to 45.8%, indistinguishable from chance), and leaves GPT OSS 20B, Gemma 4 12B and Phi-4 roughly unchanged. Their mechanistic conjecture -- offered as a conjecture, not a result -- is that Gemma 2 applies tanh-based soft-capping to its final logits, compressing exactly the raw logit magnitude the Raw scorer reads while leaving the normalized NLL intact; Gemma 4 12B, without the same capping, recovers to 0.263 under Raw. They also state plainly that 0.531 is the maximum of 14 combinations chosen after seeing the results. And the two scorers differ sharply in consistency: all seven models agree on the gold hypothesis being rank 1 for 167 of 1,323 papers (12.6%) under NLL but only 3 of 1,323 (0.2%) under Raw.

  • Practical reading for a builder: NLL is the safe default (stable 69-74% pairwise across every model tested, at any scale); Raw is the high-ceiling but model-dependent option that must be validated per checkpoint.
  • A cheap per-model sanity check falls straight out of the pairwise table: measure the gold-beats-distractor rate on a labelled set and reject any scorer near 50%.
  • Low agreement under Raw leaves open whether the models share a signal tracking correctness or each picks up different surface cues that happen to favour the gold hypothesis.

Why this matters if you are sceptical of LLM-as-judge novelty scores

The paper's framing is a direct hit on the prompted-judge pattern: the authors situate their work against known position bias, verbosity bias and self-preference bias in LLM judges, and against embedding similarity, arguing both presuppose that correctness and similarity-to-reference are tightly coupled -- a much weaker assumption for forward-looking hypotheses, which by definition are not already documented in a form the evaluator has seen. Their positive claim is that a model's stated judgment and its internal probabilities can diverge, and that for choosing among a specific hypothesis and plausible alternatives you should read the likelihoods directly. What they explicitly do NOT claim is that this measures novelty or open-ended scientific reasoning.

  • The claimed novelty is the comparison axis: prior work on this benchmark compares prompted models against each other; this work compares models against their own internal likelihoods.
  • Their proposed next step is the honest test for anyone running an autonomous loop: expert rankings of genuinely novel, UNPUBLISHED hypotheses, evaluated with rank correlation (Kendall tau / Spearman rho) rather than Hit@k.
  • Data is public (ResearchBench); code and artifacts are promised on acceptance, so the scorer is trivially reimplementable -- a handful of lines around a single forward pass.

Key findings

  • Pooled across seven open-weight models, intrinsic logit scoring reaches Hit@1 0.331 (NLL) / 0.328 (Raw) and Hit@5 0.621 / 0.594, versus 0.166 / 0.417 for GPT-5 prompted to rank the same 16 candidates listwise -- roughly a 2x Hit@1 gap on identical inputs.
  • The best single configuration is the SMALLEST model: Llama 3.2 1B under the raw target-logit energy criterion, Hit@1 0.531 [0.505, 0.558], MRR 0.641 -- but the authors state this is the maximum over 14 model-by-scorer combinations picked after seeing the results and should be read as an upper bound.
  • In head-to-head gold-vs-distractor comparisons, GPT-5-as-judge scores 37.4%, worse than the 50% a random ordering would give, while NLL scoring clusters at 68.8-74.3% across every model regardless of scale and Llama 3.2 1B under Raw reaches 82.2%.
  • Raw target-logit energy is NOT uniformly better than NLL: it helps the three small models, leaves the larger ones roughly unchanged, and destroys Gemma 2 2B (Hit@1 0.352 -> 0.135; pairwise 73.2% -> 45.8%), which the authors conjecture is caused by Gemma 2's tanh-based soft-capping of final logits.
  • Cost/consistency trade-off: scoring is one forward pass per candidate (16 per paper), deterministic, no sampling, no API judge; but the two scorers differ sharply in cross-model agreement -- all seven models rank the gold first on 167/1,323 papers (12.6%) under NLL versus only 3/1,323 (0.2%) under Raw.
  • Memorization does not obviously explain the result: ResearchBench papers are from 2024 or later, yet the strongest models (Llama 3.2 1B and 3B) have a December 2023 cutoff while models with later cutoffs performed worse.

Notable quotes

In other words, asking a large proprietary model to explicitly rank all 16 candidate hypotheses in a single pass is, in aggregate, a less reliable indicator of the true hypothesis than measuring how probable a much smaller open-weight model already finds the hypothesis text to be, without any explicit ranking instruction.
Likelihood-based scoring is stable across models, clustering between 69% and 74% regardless of scale, which suggests the signal it captures is a general property of the models rather than an artifact of any one of them.
Listwise prompting of GPT-5 performs worst at 37.4%, falling below what random ordering would achieve.
In addition, gold hypotheses were written by domain experts and may simply be more fluent than the distractors.

Limitations

  • The benchmark rewards recovering a KNOWN, already-published hypothesis whose distractors were curated after publication, so the task is closer to identifying a known hypothesis than to evaluating open-ended scientific reasoning or novelty.
  • Gold hypotheses were written by domain experts and may simply be more fluent than the distractors, so likelihood-based scores may partly capture writing quality rather than scientific plausibility -- the design cannot separate the two.
  • The judge baseline is a single proprietary model with a single zero-shot listwise prompt (top-5 only, forcing a tied terminal rank and the omission of MRR), and the open-weight models differ in size, training data, tokenizer and alignment, so the comparison cannot isolate the cause of any performance difference.

Open questions

  • Why does the raw-logit energy criterion help some models enormously and fail for others? The soft-capping story is offered only as a mechanistic conjecture, and the authors say characterizing that subset is itself the open problem.
  • Does the signal survive on genuinely novel, unpublished hypotheses? Their planned test is expert-ranked open research questions scored by rank correlation rather than Hit@k -- until then, textual familiarity cannot be ruled out.
  • Does the low cross-model agreement under Raw scoring (0.2% of papers) mean the models are exploiting different surface cues, and would ensembling NLL across several small models beat any single scorer?
