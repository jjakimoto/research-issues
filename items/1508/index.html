    Convex Modeling of Price Cross-Impact over Time

    Read full article →View on GitHub

    Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
    Date: 2026-09-14

    Source: arxiv · 2026-09-04
    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04712

    Standard multi-period portfolio models price transaction costs contract-by-contract and day-by-day, which systematically overprices relative-value trades; this paper's convex quadratic model of transient cross-impact recovers the netting between correlated legs and, in a WTI index-roll backtest, lifts monthly net PnL from 0.4 bp (baseline) to 3.1 bp.

    Executive summary

    Wang and Udell (Stanford MS&E) propose a multi-period price-impact cost that is convex quadratic and captures both cross-impact and transient impact (power-law decay). Per-period liquidity forecasts build a PSD cross-impact matrix; a power-law kernel couples periods, symmetrized so the full contract-by-time matrix stays PSD even when liquidity varies — ruling out negative-cost round trips (price manipulation). PSD-ness is proved by writing the power-law kernel as a nonnegative mixture of exponentials, each PSD as the characteristic function of a Cauchy distribution. A WTI calendar-spread study around the S&P GSCI index roll (2004-2011, 96 months, public EIA settlements) shows separable baselines badly overprice impact and barely trade.

    Problem: separable cost overprices relative-value trades

    Two empirical regularities are taken as requirements: trades in related contracts interact, and impact persists so a later same-direction trade pays residual impact while an unwind recovers part of the entry cost. Existing multi-period frameworks (Boyd et al. 2017) model impact as separable contract-by-contract and period-by-period, exactly mispricing correlated legs built and unwound over days.

    • Motivating application: relative-value trading in macro/commodity markets where edges are a few basis points.
    • Liquidity varies predictably: roll calendars, expiry cycles, and the EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report (Wednesdays 10:30 a.m. ET).
    • S&P GSCI rolls one fifth of its position on each of the fifth through ninth business days of the month.

    Single-period cross-impact matrix

    Self-impact scales with forecast return volatility over forecast dollar volume. Cross-impact sandwiches a PSD unit-diagonal impact correlation matrix between the diagonal single-contract impact terms, adding cross-impact without disturbing the self-impact diagonal. Return correlation proxies impact correlation; the cross-impact and risk matrices share cross-sectional structure but are allowed different scales.

    • Impact grows with volatility, shrinks with dollar volume.
    • Impact correlation could be further calibrated from limit-order-book data.
    • Operates at portfolio-construction timescale, not order-execution timescale.

    Transient impact and the symmetric coupling trick

    A nonincreasing power-law decay kernel couples trades across dates. Kernel values form a lower-triangular causal matrix, then are replaced by its symmetrization, which leaves the quadratic form unchanged but guarantees convexity. Appendix A gives a two-period counterexample where the true causal cost with time-varying liquidity admits a negative cross term when an illiquid period is followed closely by a liquid one.

    • Power-law decay chosen over exponential because empirics show slower-than-exponential decay.
    • Non-Markovian decay forces joint optimization of the whole trade sequence.
    • Block form reduces to the single-period model at zero lag.

    Convexity proof (no price manipulation)

    For linear impact, PSD-ness of the full contract-by-time matrix suffices to rule out negative-cost round trips. Step 1 rewrites the power-law kernel as a nonnegative mixture of exponential kernels; Step 2 shows each exponential kernel is PSD as the characteristic function of a Cauchy distribution.

    • Stated consequence: every trade sequence costs at least half the sum of its single-period costs.
    • A collection of PSD single-period matrices alone does not ensure the property when liquidity changes over time (Fruth et al. 2014).

    Numerical study: WTI calendar spreads around the GSCI roll

    Each month two contracts trade — the nearby the index sells and the deferred it buys. The trader maximizes forecast return minus impact and bid/ask costs, subject to an equal-dollar spread constraint, a gross notional cap, and a daily CVaR-style tail-loss limit. Prices are daily WTI settlements from the U.S. EIA, 2004-2011. Dollar volumes are licensed, so simulated from prior-year average WTI volume scaled by a logistic nearby-share function with Wednesday multipliers.

    • Risk limit: each day's tail loss capped at 50 bp of gross capacity, i.e. at most 0.5% loss on the worst 5% of days.
    • Impact scaling set so trading one day's volume moves price by about one day's volatility.
    • Three specifications: separable 3/2-power baseline, self-impact only, full transient cross-impact.
    • Wednesday inventory-report scaling: volume x1.08, volatility x1.02.

    Results and honest caveat

    Over 96 months the nearby-minus-deferred spread falls 9 bp over business days two through four, falls another 32 bp over the roll window, and rebounds 65 bp by day 15 — consistent with documented 30-40 bp compression and two-week recovery. All three models short the spread ahead of the roll, flip long near its end, and flatten before the nearby's liquidity dries up. The authors flag that the transient cross-impact model is both the simulator's cost model and one of the tested models.

    • Simulated impact uses the estimated cross-impact matrix and kernel; predicted impact is each model's own forecast.
    • Roughly 55-62% of index-roll activity is documented to fall inside the five-day roll window.

    Key findings

    • The separable 3/2-power baseline predicts 1.0 bp of impact cost but incurs only 0.0 bp simulated — it barely trades, netting 0.4 bp per month versus 3.1 bp for the transient cross-impact model.
    • The self-impact model predicts 6.9 bp of impact but incurs 2.7 bp, overpricing by roughly 2.6x because it cannot see the netting between the long and short legs.
    • Only the transient cross-impact model's predicted impact (5.9 bp) equals its simulated impact (5.9 bp), and it captures the largest alpha, 12.9 bp per month.
    • The power-law decay kernel is proved PSD as a nonnegative mixture of exponentials, each the characteristic function of a Cauchy distribution — convex for any time-varying liquidity path.
    • Any trade sequence under the model costs at least half the sum of its single-period costs, a quantitative floor ruling out price manipulation.
    • The averaged WTI spread falls 9 bp over business days two to four, a further 32 bp over the roll window, and rebounds 65 bp by day 15, matching Irwin, Sanders and Yan (2023).

    Notable quotes

    Second, price impact persists: a later trade in the same direction pays the residual impact of earlier trades as an additional cost, while an unwind recovers part of the entry cost.
    Power-law decay is not Markovian, so we optimize the sequence of all trades jointly under the predicted liquidity over the planning horizon.
    The argument has two steps: we write the power-law kernel as a mixture of exponential kernels with nonnegative weights, and each exponential kernel is positive semidefinite because it is the characteristic function of a Cauchy distribution.
    The index roll schedule is public and fixed in advance.

    Limitations

    • The transient cross-impact model is simultaneously the backtest simulator's cost model and one of the tested models, which the authors acknowledge biases the comparison.
    • Dollar volumes are licensed and therefore simulated from a logistic heuristic plus prior-year average WTI volume; the impact scaling parameter is set by analogy rather than learned.
    • The model deliberately does not reproduce the square-root law of metaorder impact, and estimates are frozen within each month rather than re-solved daily.

    Open questions

    • How much of the gain survives when impact correlation and scaling are calibrated on real limit-order-book and execution data?
    • Can a nonlinear (square-root-like) impact model be made convex and manipulation-free in the same multi-period, time-varying-liquidity setting?
    • Does the advantage persist out of sample after 2011 and in a daily re-solved receding-horizon implementation?
