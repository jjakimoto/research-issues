    Lean Pool: An AI-Maintained Archive of Formalized Mathematics

    Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
    Date: 2026-09-27

    Source: huggingface-daily-papers · 2026-09-21
    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25199

    A running example of a repo that agents grow, repair and optimize, with the Lean kernel as the hard correctness check and LLM review as a softer gate: daily agent jobs, repair agents fanned out on dependency bumps (95 of 97 repair jobs succeeded on the stable-Lean migration), and maintainers approving merges. Useful as a blueprint for autonomous-researcher pipelines, but it is an operational report: maintenance cost and human labor were not logged, LLM-review accuracy was never labeled, and the review service is currently paused.

    Executive summary

    Lean Pool (Vasily Ilin, University of Washington; co-created with Justin Asher) is a single Lean/Mathlib environment holding 211 completed, attributed formalization projects (3.23M lines of Lean). AI agents discover and import permissively licensed projects, bump the Lean/Mathlib version and fix what breaks, shorten proofs and cut compile cost, and announce new results. Proof correctness rests on the Lean kernel plus axiom and linter checks. An LLM review service judged whether statements match the claimed results, but its accuracy was never measured. The author says only one page of the paper is human-written; AI produced the rest from retained operational records.

    Problem and vision

    Generation is becoming cheap and verification is now the bottleneck. Mathlib grows only linearly because of strict human review, and it lacks much of research-level mathematics. Lean Pool aims to be a maintained formal counterpart to arXiv: a paper goes on arXiv, its formalization goes into Lean Pool, and later work imports it, so formal dependencies can mirror citations.

    • Grows two ways: agents find and pool Apache-2.0/MIT formalization projects, and humans submit their own (human, AI-written and mixed proofs are all eligible).
    • Only serious, complete formalizations of named known results are admitted. Open challenges are kept on a separate board.
    • Documentation: an index, an Exposition site with dependency graphs, and daily announcements on Zulip.

    Architecture: what agents do vs. what the kernel and LLM review check

    A separate automation repo holds maintained prompts and a host runner. The runner refreshes its checkout, prevents duplicate instances of a job, and can hand a failed job to a configured fallback backend. Daily jobs: recent discovery, older discovery, open-PR review/repair, project optimization, maintainer-issue resolution, and announcements. A separate scheduled workflow detects new Lean/Mathlib releases, probes every project, assigns failures to repair agents, assembles their patches, builds the combined archive and opens a draft PR. Maintainers oversee merges.

    • Hard gate (kernel/CI): full-library build, warnings rejected, Mathlib environment and style linters, no sorry/admit, only the axioms Classical.choice, propext and Quot.sound, no set_option/unchecked declarations/linter waivers/broad Mathlib imports, plus a compiled-environment audit for attempts to bypass the checks.
    • Project cards must list authors, upstream source, proof provenance, subject tags and main results paired with informal statements. Generated cards must agree with the registry.
    • Soft gate (LLM): a mathematical review service scored faithfulness, novelty, significance, sources and code quality. Greptile gave advisory reviews. Per-file profiling is advisory only, whereas Tau Ceti makes performance checks a merge requirement.
    • Solutions to challenge-board statements are checked against the registered statement by an independent kernel comparison.
    • The paper does not say which LLMs ran the daily jobs. The later review service used Codex account quota on Azure.

    Scale of the archive (census of Sep 21, 2026)

    211 completed projects across logic, number theory, algebra, analysis, geometry, probability and CS. The largest is the AI-written Navier–Stokes/Euler finite-time breakdown development (641,073 lines), followed by a complex structure on the six-sphere (260,646 lines, AI) and the classification of compact surfaces (143,423 lines, AI). Other examples: Gödel's incompleteness theorems (human, 55,430 lines), polynomial Freiman–Ruzsa, Kurosh subgroup theorem, and language generation in the limit.

    • Provenance: 70 human, 102 AI and 39 mixed projects.
    • Exposition export: 203 projects, 173,362 declarations, 124,412 theorems/lemmas, 1,389,967 dependency links.
    • Reuse: the paper reports Lean Pool as the most-reused external repository in the (anonymous) LeanEval structural audit, and records specific downstream reuses (Kurosh, Feige, Poincaré, Mazur).

    Reliability of maintenance: dependency upgrades

    Replays of historical upgrades show how often unchanged source breaks under a new Lean/Mathlib. For the stable 4.34.0 migration, 95 of 97 initial repair jobs succeeded (the 2 failures were the graph fundamental-group and incompleteness projects). Even so, the migration needed further agent-assisted integration for build errors, warning changes and API changes before it merged with 198 projects. Some repairs strengthened auxiliary hypotheses (measurability, sigma-finiteness, maximality).

    • Compiler-failure rates per upgrade ranged from 2% (3/148) to 75% (44/59).
    • Line churn per accepted upgrade ranged from 4,676 to 25,583 lines. The paper uses this as its proxy for effort because monetary charges and human labor were not logged.
    • A green build shows the accepted statements compile. It does not show that every declaration type is equivalent before and after the upgrade.

    Optimization and LLM review in operation

    Library-wide compression and elaboration-cost PRs removed tens of thousands of lines and cut clean-build time by a few percent. Shorter proofs did not always build faster: contributor golfing cut memory but slowed the build by 5.4%. A module/import optimization cut peak single-process RSS from 12.6 GiB to 2.9 GiB on a fixed workload. The review service kept 296 structured reports (14 flagged as only partially covering the input). Most approved their PR, and findings covered statement mismatches, attribution, incomplete results, duplicate definitions and unnecessary code. The workflow was manually disabled before the observation date.

    • Review cost: historical API reviews had a median of $0.20 per report. The newer Codex-based reviews of large changes were estimated at a median of $89.03 per report (API-equivalent, not cash).
    • Consistency: only 37 of 69 pairs of repeat reviews on the same PR gave the same verdict. There were 0 same-revision pairs, so pure model consistency is untested.
    • A clean build of the full archive takes 60.3 min, 14.3 CPU-hours and 20.0 GiB on a 16-CPU Azure VM, vs 37.4 min and 7.35 GiB for the matching Mathlib.

    Governance, licensing and release

    Imported source must be Apache-2.0 or MIT, and project cards keep upstream authorship even when an agent does the import. The author says the archive, its maintenance workflows and the Exposition site are provided (index at vilin97.github.io/lean-pool). The automation repo with prompts and runner is separate. The paper is CC BY 4.0. Raw records and analysis programs are 'retained separately' from the submission, and the paper does not say they are public.

    • 18 commit contributors, 17 with merged PRs, and 63 merged community PRs (the top community contributor has 13).
    • Human and AI shares of work are not measured, because GitHub accounts do not show how work was split between a person and their agents.

    Key findings

    • Scale: 211 completed projects, 3,228,485 Lean lines, 193,862 declarations and 837 registered main results. 102 projects are AI-written, 70 human and 39 mixed.
    • Upgrade breakage: compiler-failure rates per upgrade ranged from 2% (3/148) to 75% (44/59). On the stable 4.34.0 migration, 95 of 97 repair-agent jobs succeeded, but follow-up integration was still needed before merge (639 files, 5,951 lines of churn).
    • Optimization: library-wide compression removed 45,217 lines (3.3% faster build) and elaboration-cost work removed 54,965 lines (5.8% faster). Contributor golfing removed 12,515 lines but made the build 5.4% slower.
    • LLM review cost ran from a median of $0.20 per report (historical API, 285 reports, $308.52 total) to a median of $89.03 per report for the Codex-based reviews of large changes (6 reports, $752.38, API-equivalent). The paper reports no cost per theorem and no total operating cost.
    • Review reliability is weakly evidenced: only 37 of 69 pairs of repeat reviews on the same PR agreed, accuracy was never independently labeled, and the review workflow was manually disabled before the observation date.
    • The full archive takes 60.3 min and 20.0 GiB to build clean, vs 37.4 min and 7.35 GiB for the matching Mathlib, on the same 16-CPU Azure VM.

    Notable quotes

    Daily jobs search for recent and older formalizations, inspect open PRs, address maintainer issues, optimize existing projects, and announce accepted contributions.
    The recorded outcomes are merge and closure decisions; review accuracy was not independently labeled.
    These records provide an observable proxy for maintenance effort; historical monetary charges and human labor were not logged.
    Its successful build establishes compatibility of the accepted statements; it does not establish equivalence of every declaration type before and after the upgrade.

    Limitations

    • This is an observational, single-archive operational report with no controlled experiments. Monetary cost and human labor for maintenance were not logged, so there is no cost per theorem or measure of human intervention, and the models behind the daily jobs are not named.
    • The LLM review is the only check on whether statements match the claimed results, yet its accuracy is unlabeled, repeat reviews on the same PR agreed only 37 of 69 times, and the service was paused before the latest imports, so recent additions have no structured review.
    • Most of the paper was AI-written from retained records, and the reuse claim leans on an anonymous, unpublished LeanEval audit. Build gains are single before/after runs on a shared VM with background load.

    Open questions

    • Can checking whether statements match the claimed results be made reliable and cheap enough (compare the $0.20 vs $89 median per report) to run on every import at arXiv scale, and how often do faithfulness errors actually get through?
    • As the archive grows, will build cost (already 60 min and 20 GiB, versus 37 min for Mathlib) and per-upgrade breakage keep agent-driven maintenance workable without mandatory performance gates like Tau Ceti's?
    • How much human maintainer time and model spend does each upgrade and import actually take once it is instrumented?
