BDH-CQ: In-Context Learning with Recurrent Latent Reasoning

📰 Latest · 2026-08-24 Full Article
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-08-24

Source: huggingface-daily-papers · 2026-08-10
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09888

A 150M-parameter non-Transformer (BDH) system solves 29.5% of public ARC-AGI-1 at a self-computed $0.00070/task by doing its reasoning in latent space instead of tokens — a cost-efficiency SOTA claim, not an accuracy one, and the cost comparison pits measured GPU-seconds against other systems' leaderboard/API prices.

Executive summary

Pathway (plus co-authors at Bielik AI and NYU) present BDH-CQ, a reasoning system built on their Dragon Hatchling (BDH) architecture that fuses in-context learning with recurrent latent reasoning: demonstrations sequentially update a recurrent memory state, then the query is solved by iterating a continuous workspace over depth and decoding only the final grid — no verbalized chain of thought, no test-time parameter updates, no growing KV cache. A 150M-parameter configuration reaches 29.5% pass@2 on the 400-task public ARC-AGI-1 eval at a computed $0.00070/task (0.85 H200 GPU-seconds at $3/H200-hour), which the authors claim breaks the previously reported ARC-AGI-1 cost-accuracy Pareto frontier. Most of the paper is a behavioral audit that localizes failures to conditional rule selection, undemonstrated parameter values, panel segmentation, and dependency depth. The headline is real but narrow — accuracy is below the compared frontier model (34.2%), the architecture is explicitly proprietary, and ConceptARC appears in both the training mixture and the evaluation.

Architecture: a recurrent memory for context, a separate latent workspace for reasoning

BDH-CQ is built on BDH, a post-Transformer sequence model with high-dimensional positive activations, low-rank communication, and a recurrent associative state; BDH layers combine ReLU-low-rank transformations with linear attention in a large neuron/feature space. BDH-CQ splits state into two roles. In-context learning: given demonstrations and a query, the model does NOT compress demonstrations into a single task vector — it processes their elements sequentially so later inputs see associations accumulated from earlier pairs, playing the role of attention's context-dependent associations 'while avoiding a growing explicit key-value cache'. Reasoning: after all demonstrations and the query are ingested, the latent workspace is iterated over depth and only the answer grid is decoded.

  • Intermediate reasoning states are never decoded into language; only the answer grid is decoded.
  • No parameters are updated at inference; no task identifiers and no evaluation-task demonstration pairs are used in training — the explicit contrast with transductive solvers (HRM/TRM) that need a backward pass per new puzzle.
  • Reproducibility caveat stated by the authors: 'Dimensions, exact update rules, and implementation details remain proprietary.' Same for the training recipe. The 'complete evaluated system' also includes input transformations, candidate construction, and ranking — i.e. a pipeline, not just a forward pass.
  • Lineage: BDH layers previously used for a recurrent Sudoku constraint-satisfaction solver; BDH-CQ adds the demonstration-conditioned interface.

Training setup: 150M parameters on an ARC-specialized mixture

A single 150M-parameter model is trained on a curated ARC-style collection: privately curated examples plus the ARC-AGI-1 training set, RE-ARC, ConceptARC, ARC-Heavy, and ARC-GEN100K, with additional augmentations. The objective is to predict exact target grids after preceding examples have been folded into recurrent context. For the effort-scaling experiment, the model is trained with varying levels of latent reasoning so effort can be selected at inference.

  • Parameter-count fairness cuts both ways: 150M is ~3 orders of magnitude below the frontier LLMs it is priced against, but it is also trained specifically on ARC-style data, so this is a task-specialized model versus general-purpose ones.
  • Contamination flag the paper itself does not resolve: ConceptARC is in the training mixture and is also the main behavioral evaluation set. The paper concedes the opaque-identifier replication 'does not make ConceptARC a fresh benchmark, rule out exposure through training or checkpoint selection, or isolate the two interventions factorially.'
  • Controlled experiments were generated AFTER the model was frozen — the cleaner part of the evaluation.

ARC-AGI-1 result and the cost-accuracy Pareto claim

Evaluation is the 400-task public ARC-AGI-1 eval split under the leaderboard's two-attempt pass@2 convention. The system scores 118/400 = 29.50% pass@2 (Wilson 95% CI [25.24, 34.15]) and 97/400 = 24.25% pass@1, in approximately 0.85 H200 GPU-seconds per task; at $3 per H200-hour that is a computed $0.00070 per task. Against ARC Prize's reported costs as of July 2026 the paper claims ~57x cheaper than GPT 5.6 Luna (Low), which scores 34.2% at $0.040 — and ~11x cheaper after accounting for an 80% API price cut on 2026-07-30 not reflected in ARC Prize data.

  • The claim is explicitly about the cost axis: 'no plotted system attains at least this accuracy at equal or lower reported cost.' BDH-CQ does not beat the compared model on accuracy (29.5% vs 34.2%).
  • Not apples-to-apples by the paper's own admission: BDH-CQ's dollar figure is computed from measured hardware time, while 'Other plotted systems use the costs reported by the leaderboard, which may represent hardware estimates or API prices.' Amortized training cost and the candidate-ranking pipeline are outside BDH-CQ's number but inside an API price.
  • Statistical caution: the 95% Wilson upper bound on 29.5% is 34.15%, essentially touching the baseline's 34.2% — on 400 tasks the accuracy gap is at the edge of resolvability.
  • The 'independent' audit that reproduced 29.5% pass@2 was a black-box evaluation without weight access conducted by co-authors.

Behavioral probes: dense binding is solid, consistency and composition are not

The most useful content is the failure map. Contextual binding is strong: fresh per-task color permutations are applied to all 96 held-out outputs at rank one, holding as simultaneous bindings scale from two to eight. Extrapolation is uneven: boundary propagation is 48/48 correct across distances 2-8 and motif copying 48/48 across one to four sites, but ordering is near-saturated through five objects then collapses to 8/24 at seven and 1/24 at eight, and nesting holds through depth four then falls to 29/36 at depth five. Adding one demonstration at the test complexity fixes nesting (19/24 to 24/24 pass@2) and only partly rescues ordering (0/24 to 13/24). Composition depends on the operation and the representation.

  • Rule consistency is the headline weakness: an 18.5-point gap between ConceptARC pair accuracy (77.92%) and strict task accuracy (59.38%); 52/160 tasks have one or two correct test inputs but fail as tasks.
  • Weakest ConceptARC families: Copy 2/10 and Order 2/10 pass@2 versus 9/10 for FilledNotFilled and TopBottom2D — but with ten tasks per family the Wilson intervals overlap, so this is a profile, not a ranking.
  • Confound control that worked: replacing semantic task_ids with cryptographically opaque labels moved nothing in aggregate (96/160 vs 95/160 tasks), though only 442/480 first candidates agreed.
  • Appendix ladders localize sharper limits: conditional rule selection drops 100.0% to 56.7% when a corner marker must select between two demonstrated rules; a parameter value absent from the demonstrations drops 30.0% to 0.0% (0/120, including 0/40 for an interpolated unseen value); panel union goes 65.0% (two panels) to 2.5% (three); support-chain dependency decays 80.0% -> 27.5% for 1 to 8 objects while three INDEPENDENT objects stay 40/40.
  • Determinism is real: repeated identical requests were byte-identical at both effort tiers.

Effort scaling — and an unexplained internal cost inconsistency

Because latent reasoning depth was varied during training, effort is selectable at inference. Table 5 reports pass@2 of 29.5% (HIGH), 27% (MEDIUM), and 21% (LOW) against 'cost reduction' of 0%, 11%, and 22%. Section 6.6 separately reports a MIN effort tier costing one third of standard ($0.00088399 vs $0.00265246 per task) and scoring 111/400 rather than 118/400 pass@2, with two-sided exact McNemar p=0.167 — the paper's own verdict is that the comparison is statistically unresolved.

  • The per-task dollar figures do not reconcile: Section 6.6's STANDARD effort costs $0.00265246/task, roughly 3.8x the headline $0.00070/task used for the Pareto claim, and Table 5's 11%/22% 'cost reduction' contradicts Section 6.6's 'one third of standard'.
  • Even at the most pessimistic figure the cost claim vs $0.040 API pricing survives directionally, but the '57x' headline multiplier does not.
  • Section 9.2 asserts that BDH-CQ shards like BDH, is 'particularly easy to train at 1T scale', and that early experiments confirm Transformer-like pretraining scaling from 1B to 600B parameters — presented in a single paragraph with no table, figure, or numbers.

Evaluation-set profiling: surface features explain little, generators confound a lot

Appendix A profiles three cohorts: the public 400-task set (118 solved, 29.5%), a 400-task generated set calibrated to the public distribution (149, 37.2%), and a 1,131-task set stratified across 16 transformation 'mechanics' (337, 29.8%). Coarse surface descriptors barely predict success — of 39 bucketings of grid size, color count, object count and demonstration count, nine exceed the permutation null on the public set and none on the generated set. Mechanic stratification spans 65.7 points, from flood fill 68.6% to gravity-and-stacking 2.9%, but the ranking is not intrinsic: every stratified task was authored by GPT-5.6 from a prompt naming the mechanic, and hand-written comparison families flip the picture.

  • Task-authoring confound, quantified: hand-written gravity tasks score 80-100% versus 2.9% for generated gravity-and-stacking, and hand-written counting 85-95% versus 18.6% generated; public-vs-generated mechanic ranks barely correlate (rho = 0.300).
  • Failure anatomy is 'near miss' shaped: 89.7% of public-set failures have correct output dimensions, 78.0% correct palette, and among shape-correct failures the median cell error is 8.3%.
  • Honest scope statement: the evaluator returns grids, not traces, so a correct rule applied incompletely is indistinguishable from a narrower rule applied completely. Also flagged: at least one generated task whose stated output contradicts its own demonstrations, and LLM mechanic labels agreeing with the requested label on only 82.9% of a 659-task subset.

Key findings

  • A 150M-parameter BDH-CQ scores 29.5% pass@2 (118/400) and 24.25% pass@1 on the public ARC-AGI-1 eval in ~0.85 H200 GPU-seconds per task = a computed $0.00070/task, claimed as a new cost-efficiency SOTA — but accuracy is below the compared GPT 5.6 Luna (Low) at 34.2%/$0.040, and the 95% Wilson upper bound (34.15%) sits right at that baseline.
  • The comparison is not apples-to-apples and the paper says so: BDH-CQ's cost is measured hardware time while other plotted systems use leaderboard costs that 'may represent hardware estimates or API prices'. The claimed multiplier drops from ~57x to ~11x once the 80% price cut is applied — and Section 6.6 prices STANDARD effort at $0.00265246/task, ~3.8x the headline figure, an inconsistency the paper never reconciles.
  • In-context binding is genuinely strong: fresh per-task color permutations are applied correctly on all 96 held-out outputs at rank one from two up to eight simultaneous bindings, with zero test-time parameter updates — unlike transductive HRM/TRM ($1.48 and $1.76 per task) which optimize on evaluation-task demonstrations.
  • Consistency, not raw capability, is the bottleneck: an 18.5-point gap between ConceptARC pair accuracy (77.92%) and strict task accuracy (59.38%), with 52/160 tasks getting one or two of three test inputs right. 89.7% of public-set failures still have correct output dimensions and a median 8.3% cell error.
  • Demonstration coverage, not scale, gates extrapolation: one extra demonstration at test complexity lifts depth-five nesting from 19/24 to 24/24 and length-eight ordering from 0/24 to 13/24; a parameter value absent from the demonstrations drops solve rate to 0/120 even when it is interpolated inside the demonstrated range.
  • Composition is representation-dependent: rotation composes with relocation 72/72, reflection only 47/72, and a demonstration-defined color swap is learned only in the fixed-layout motif family (26/72) and never composes (0/72). Conditional rule selection costs 43.3 points.
  • ConceptARC sits in BOTH the training mixture and the behavioral evaluation; the paper admits the replication does not 'rule out exposure through training or checkpoint selection', so the 59-60% ConceptARC figures are not held-out numbers.

Notable quotes

Rotation composed with relocation is solved on 72/72 tasks, while reflection composed with relocation is solved on 47/72 tasks.
hand-written gravity tasks score 80–100%
Early experiments confirm Transformer-like scaling laws apply during pretraining at scales from 1B to 600B parameters, while preserving the latent reasoning capabilities specific to BDH-CQ.

Limitations

  • Not reproducible as described: 'Dimensions, exact update rules, and implementation details remain proprietary', as is the training recipe, and the evaluated artifact is a pipeline (input transformations, candidate construction, ranking) rather than a released model — so the cost-efficiency claim cannot be independently re-derived. The 'independent' black-box audit was performed by co-authors.
  • Benchmark hygiene and accounting problems: ConceptARC is both trained on and evaluated on; the generated cohorts are authored by GPT-5.6 (hand-written gravity tasks score 80-100% against 2.9% generated, mechanic ranks correlate only rho=0.300 with the public set); and the two cost accountings ($0.00070 vs $0.00265246 per task for the same standard setting) disagree by ~3.8x.
  • Statistical resolution is thin where the paper leans hardest: 400 public tasks with a [25.24, 34.15] interval, ten tasks per ConceptARC family, 40 tasks per ladder condition from a single puzzle family, and the standard-vs-MIN effort comparison unresolved (McNemar p=0.167). The 1B-600B scaling claim is asserted with no supporting data at all.
  • Scope is ARC-like visual transduction only — no language, math, or agentic evaluation, no ARC-AGI-2 result, and the cost advantage is demonstrated for a task family the model was specifically trained on.

Open questions

  • Does the cost-accuracy advantage survive at scale and outside ARC? The paper shows no accuracy-vs-size curve for BDH-CQ itself, no ARC-AGI-2 number, and no text/math in-context result.
  • Why is a correctly bound transformation applied inconsistently across the three test inputs of one task, given repeated requests are byte-identical? Deterministic outputs plus 52/160 partially-solved tasks point at the candidate-construction/ranking stage rather than the latent reasoning.
  • What makes composition representation-dependent — rotation composes perfectly while reflection reaches 47/72 and color swap 0/72 — and can conditional rule selection and undemonstrated-parameter generalization (0/120) be fixed by training-mixture coverage, or are they structural limits of writing a task into a fixed-size recurrent state?
