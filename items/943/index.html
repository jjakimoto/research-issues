Concentrated Liquidity Provision: a Reinforcement Learning Perspective

📰 Latest · 2026-08-24 Full Article
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-08-24

Source: arxiv · 2026-08-19
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19389

Casts Uniswap-v3-style concentrated liquidity provision as a stochastic impulse-control problem (when to rebalance + which tick range to post) and solves it with PPO, showing that learned LP policies trade fee concentration against impermanent loss and gas in economically interpretable ways — widening ranges under risk aversion, expanding the inaction region under high gas, and exiting the pool entirely when volatility and gas are both high.

Executive summary

The paper formalises dynamic liquidity provision in a constant-product market with concentrated liquidity (CL) as a stochastic impulse-control problem: at chosen intervention times the LP pays a fixed gas cost and picks a new tick interval, maximising fees minus impermanent loss (IL) minus gas, optionally with a running inventory penalty for risk aversion. Because the HJB-QVI is intractable in this discrete-tick, jump-driven setting, the authors solve it with PPO in a purpose-built simulator (GBM external price, arbitrage-driven order-flow intensities, one-tick jumps, 0.3% fee tier) and benchmark against deploy-once, tuned arrival-based rebalancing, and the Cartea-Drissi-Monga (CDM) closed-form strategy. Two structural lemmas show fee revenue and IL are both linear in the LP's own liquidity depth, so PnL is affine in it — giving a risk-neutral incentive to concentrate maximally, which the fixed-two-tick PPO_narrow agent exploits (49.91 vs 42.31 USDC mean PnL at sigma=0.01, g=2) at the cost of higher IL and gas. The headline behavioural result is that risk-averse agents compress the PnL left tail (giving up about 4 USDC of mean PnL for about 27 USDC of CVaR improvement at sigma=0.02, g=4) and, under sigma=0.03 with g=6, learn endogenous market exit rather than better active management.

Formulation: impulse control, not continuous control

The LP is a controller who intervenes only occasionally because each intervention costs gas. The control is an impulse strategy alpha = (r_n, Z^l_n, Z^u_n): an increasing sequence of stopping times plus, at each one, a lower and upper tick from a discrete grid. On rebalancing the LP collects accrued fees, pays a fixed gas cost g, and redeploys her wealth into the new range. The external midprice S_t is exogenous GBM; the pool's marginal exchange rate Z_t moves only via one-tick liquidity-taker jumps whose up/down counting processes have intensity lambda^{+/-}_t = max{lambda_0, lambda_1 +/- lambda_2 (S_t - Z_{t-})} — baseline noise flow plus arbitrage pressure proportional to the pool/external mispricing.

  • Risk-neutral objective (7): sup E[P_T - IL_T - C_T] = fees minus impermanent loss minus cumulative gas.
  • Risk-averse objective (8) adds a running inventory penalty -phi * integral (y_t - y_hat)^2 dt around a target risky-asset level y_hat; without the target the criterion would just force liquidation.
  • Lemma 3.1: under one-tick jumps the LP's fee revenue is linear in her own depth kappa-tilde and independent of the other LPs' liquidity (the pro-rata share cancels the tick depth).
  • Lemma 3.2 / Theorem 3.3: IL is also linear in kappa-tilde, so PnL is affine in own liquidity with gas entering as a depth-independent constant — the theoretical case for maximal concentration in the risk-neutral case.
  • Assumes the LP is small relative to the pool, so her actions do not feed back into future liquidity-taking flow (no market impact).

The MDP: state, action, reward

Two PPO agents are trained. PPO is 'flexible' and chooses both bounds of the range; PPO_narrow is pinned to the minimum two-tick width and only learns where to place liquidity — isolating the value of learning 'where' from learning 'how wide'. Actions are taken only at intervention times, consistent with the impulse-control formulation and with on-chain execution being discrete. Choosing the same interval as before means 'hold' and incurs no gas; choosing (0,0] at the first step means 'do not provide liquidity' at all.

  • Observation: mispricing S_t - Z_t, distances from the current pool price to the position's boundaries, time, gas cost, cumulative fee income, and the LP's current holdings in the risky and numeraire assets.
  • Action: the new (lower, upper) tick pair, restricted to [-15, 15] ticks around the current tick; repeating the current interval = hold.
  • Reward: sample-path realised PnL (fees - IL - gas) for the risk-neutral agent; the inventory-penalised version for the risk-averse agent, with phi in {20, 50, 80} setting risk aversion.
  • Decisions every K_dec = 100 environment steps out of N_steps = 1000 (so 10 intervention opportunities per episode).

Simulator, calibration and training budget

Everything is model-based — a synthetic CL simulator replicating full Uniswap-v3 mechanics rather than a historical backtest (the authors explicitly contrast this with prior RL-for-CL work that trains on historical data). Price parameters are set within the range of empirical estimates from Cartea-Drissi-Monga; the remaining parameters are chosen to be economically sensible.

  • External price: S_0 = 1000, dt = 0.001, T = 1, mu = 0, sigma swept over {0.005, 0.01, 0.015, 0.02, 0.025, 0.03}.
  • Order flow: lambda_1 = 15, lambda_2 = 4000 — flow dominated by arbitrage pressure, so the pool price tracks the external price closely.
  • Frictions: fixed gas cost g in {2, 4, 6} (low/medium/high); proportional fee tier tau = 0.3%; starting capital 1000 numeraire units.
  • 72 scenarios (6 sigma x 3 gas x 4 risk settings), each trained with 10 independent seeds; 200 parallel trajectories per rollout, 2000 decision transitions per rollout, E_train = 300 -> 6e5 decision transitions total.
  • PPO hyperparameters: lr 3e-4, batch 200, gamma 0.99, GAE lambda 0.95, clip 0.15, entropy coefficient 0.03, two 256-unit MLP hidden layers.
  • Evaluation: 1000 independent simulations on held-out seeds; hierarchical paired t-test for PnL and a paired bootstrap (1e4 replicates) over seeds and trajectories for CVaR.

Baselines and headline performance

Baselines all operate in the same [-15, 15] tick window: DeployNarrow ([-1,1], never rebalances), DeployWide ([-15,15], never rebalances), ArrivalRebalance ([-1,1], rebalancing every N_arr liquidity-taking arrivals with N_arr grid-searched per scenario from 10 to 800), and CDM, the closed-form model-based strategy of Cartea-Drissi-Monga. Notably CDM is given an optimistic frictionless treatment — it rebalances every K_dec steps free of gas — so it is a deliberately generous analytical benchmark. In the benign regime (sigma=0.01, g=2) PPO_narrow matches the tuned ArrivalRebalance on mean PnL (49.91 vs 49.72) with better 5% CVaR (9.15 vs 6.86). In the medium regime (sigma=0.02, g=4) CDM beats both RL agents on mean PnL with significant p-values, but the risk-averse RL agents dominate it on downside risk.

  • PPO_narrow generally beats flexible PPO because the fixed two-tick range hard-codes the concentration that Theorem 3.3 makes optimal — but the PnL decomposition shows it pays for this with higher IL and higher gas from more frequent rebalancing.
  • Risk aversion at sigma=0.02, g=4 costs about 4 USDC of mean PnL and buys about 27 USDC of CVaR improvement versus the risk-neutral counterparts.
  • Under sigma=0.03, g=6 every strategy loses money except the risk-averse RL agents, which learn essentially not to deploy (PnL ~ 0.00, CVaR ~ -0.68 / -0.16) — endogenous market exit, versus CVaR around -52 to -63 for everything else.
  • Standard deviations across the 10 training seeds are small (mostly <1 USDC), which the authors read as stable training.

What the learned policies actually do (interpretability)

The paper profiles the policies as state-dependent functions rather than only reporting PnL. The risk-neutral PPO_narrow agent uses mispricing as an order-flow direction signal: deeply out-of-range positions get recentred in the direction of expected arbitrage flow, with heavier asymmetry towards the external price when mispricing is large, while positions already favourably placed are held to avoid paying gas. Raising gas from 2 to 6 makes every response line 'dashed' — the agent holds regardless of the mispricing signal, i.e. high gas both mechanically reduces PnL and widens the no-trade region. The risk-averse flexible PPO agent additionally modulates width by inventory: with low ETH (more numeraire) it widens to dilute exposure to unfavourable prices; with high ETH it concentrates on the profitable rates; and when S - Z > 0 it simply holds a symmetric (-1,1) position and lets incoming arbitrage flow swap its risky token into the numeraire.

  • Mean quoted width rises with volatility, gas, and risk aversion; rebalancing rate falls along all three dimensions.
  • Superimposing the average positions of PPO agents with heterogeneous risk profiles plus a wide passive background liquidity produces a bell-shaped aggregate liquidity profile around the pool price.
  • That superposition is offered as a qualitative account of real on-chain CL shapes: flatter Ethereum-style distributions correspond to stronger risk aversion / higher gas, while the peakier Optimism and Arbitrum shapes look like more risk-seeking, narrow-quoting LPs.
  • Summary claim: policies use mispricing to infer order-flow direction, gas to decide whether intervening is worthwhile, and inventory to control risk.

Key findings

  • Theorem 3.3: because fee revenue and IL are each linear in the LP's own liquidity depth (Lemmas 3.1, 3.2) and gas is depth-independent, the LP's PnL is affine in own liquidity — a risk-neutral LP should therefore concentrate maximally in the expected most profitable tick range.
  • Fixing the width to the minimum two ticks beats learning the width in most regimes: PPO_narrow reaches 49.91 +/- 0.38 USDC mean PnL vs 42.31 +/- 0.97 for flexible PPO at sigma=0.01, g=2, and 46.11 vs 30.36 in the risk-averse (phi=50) case (p < 1e-10).
  • The concentration advantage is paid for in IL and gas: the PnL decomposition shows PPO_narrow earns more fees but incurs greater impermanent loss and higher gas because narrow positions need more frequent rebalancing.
  • Risk aversion buys asymmetric tail protection: at sigma=0.02, g=4 the risk-averse agents give up roughly 4 USDC of mean PnL to improve 5% CVaR by roughly 27 USDC (e.g. PPO CVaR goes from -36.51 to -9.14).
  • In the adversarial regime (sigma=0.03, g=6) all baselines lose money (ArrivalRebalance -5.38 PnL, CVaR -62.64) while the risk-averse RL agents converge to not deploying at all — PnL 0.00 +/- 0.04 and CVaR -0.68 +/- 1.47 for PPO, effectively a DoNothing policy.
  • The generous frictionless CDM closed-form benchmark still wins on mean PnL in the medium regime (15.01 vs 6.51 for PPO_narrow, significant p) but is beaten badly on CVaR by the risk-averse RL agents (-26.23 vs -8.86).
  • High gas does not just scale PnL down, it changes the policy's topology: at g=6 the risk-neutral PPO_narrow agent holds its position across the entire mispricing range, i.e. the inaction region expands to cover the whole state slice.

Notable quotes

Thus, under sufficiently adverse conditions, the economically meaningful learned behaviour is endogenous market exit rather than superior active liquidity management.
Thus, high gas costs not only reduce PnL mechanically but also expand the inaction region.
PPO_narrow rebalances more often on average, reflecting the greater management required by fixed concentrated positions.
We do not claim universal superiority of RL; rather, we show that it can produce interpretable, state-dependent policies where closed-form solutions are generally unavailable.

Limitations

  • Entirely model-based and synthetic: GBM external price with mu=0, a linear arbitrage-intensity order-flow model, deterministic background liquidity, one-tick jumps, and a small-LP assumption with no market impact — no on-chain or historical data is used, and the authors note an empirical validation of the aggregate-liquidity-shape story would require on-chain data analysis, which is beyond the scope of this work.
  • Each policy is trained and evaluated in the same stationary regime (fixed sigma, gas, phi), so results are conditional on correctly identified, approximately stationary dynamics and say nothing about regime detection, non-stationarity, or transfer; the continuous-time impulse-control problem is only solved in its discretised form with 10 decision opportunities per episode.
  • Benchmark set is thin and unevenly handicapped: two deploy-once rules, one tuned arrival-based rebalancer, and CDM — which is given free, gas-less rebalancing every K_dec steps, making the comparison optimistic for the analytical baseline; gas is also a fixed constant (2/4/6) rather than a stochastic, congestion-dependent cost, and there is no MEV/JIT-liquidity or competing-LP dynamics.

Open questions

  • Does the concentration result survive multi-agent competition among heterogeneous LPs, where one LP's narrow position dilutes another's fee share and the small-LP/no-impact assumption breaks?
  • Can these policies be trained or validated on real Uniswap v3 on-chain data with stochastic gas and MEV/arbitrage flow, and does the 'endogenous market exit' behaviour reproduce there?
  • How do the learned policies change under AMM designs with dynamic trading fees, and can the risk-preference superposition story be identified from observed on-chain liquidity distributions rather than merely being consistent with them?
