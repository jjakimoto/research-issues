Rethinking Synthetic Scenario Realism: Compatibility, Not Fidelity, Drives Hedging Performance

📰 Latest · 2026-08-24 Full Article
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-08-24

Source: arxiv · 2026-08-21
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20842

Deep-hedging teams grade their synthetic path generators on stylized-fact realism, but this paper proves and then empirically shows that realism is the wrong objective: what matters is compatibility (does the generator preserve the risk landscape over YOUR hedger class) plus learnability, and an unrealistic GBM can beat a calibrated Heston or a VAE depending on hedger architecture and option/cost structure.

Executive summary

The authors recast scenario-generator design for deep hedging as a decision problem rather than a fidelity problem. They define generator-hedger compatibility Gamma(P,Q_theta;H) = sup over h in H of |rho_P(h) - rho_{Q_theta}(h)|, prove the true excess risk is bounded by learning error + 2*Gamma (Theorem 1), and prove a structural divergence: with a finite-dimensional realism test-function class you can construct P and Q_theta with realism discrepancy exactly 0 but Gamma > 0 (Theorem 3). Empirically, over 4 generators (GBM, Merton, Heston, VAE) x 3 hedgers (Linear, NTBNet, MLP) x 4 tasks (European/Lookback x 1e-4/1e-3 cost) x 3 risk measures on Nikkei 225 data, generator rankings reorder completely across hedgers and tasks -- GBM, the worst generator on almost every realism metric, wins several settings, and no generator-hedger pair is globally best.

Setup: the two-layered approximation in deep hedging

Single underlying asset, price paths omega = (S_0,...,S_T). The hedger is a sequence of decision rules h in H; C(omega,h) is terminal P&L and rho_mu(h) is the population risk under measure mu. The ideal problem is min over h in H of rho_P(h) under the unknown true law P. In practice a generator induces Q_theta, giving h*_{Q_theta}, and finite sampling of n paths gives the actual estimator. The paper frames this as two stacked approximations -- distributional (P -> Q_theta) and optimization (population -> empirical minimizer) -- with the quantity of interest being rho_P(h_hat_{Q_theta}) - rho_P(h*_P).

  • Compatibility is a worst-case risk-evaluation error over the ADMISSIBLE STRATEGY CLASS, not a distance between distributions.
  • Compatibility is explicitly NOT an intrinsic property of the generator: the same generator has different compatibility for a linear hedger vs an NTBNet.
  • Maturity T = 20 daily decision steps; S_0 = 1.000, strike = 1.000.

Theorem 1: performance gap = learning error + 2 x compatibility gap

The excess risk decomposes as rho_P(h_hat_{Q_theta}) - rho_P(h*_P) <= [learning error under Q_theta] + 2*Gamma(P,Q_theta;H). The operational reading is that a generator need not approximate P globally -- it only needs to preserve the risk ranking of strategies inside H. The authors extract a trade-off: a generator that introduces a nonzero compatibility gap can still WIN if the simplification it induces cuts the learning error by more.

  • 'Decision-relevant realism' replaces global realism as the design target.
  • This is the formal justification for why GBM -- badly wrong as a market model -- can dominate.
  • Neither term is directly observable, which is the paper's own main measurement caveat.

Theorem 3: zero realism discrepancy with strictly positive incompatibility

Both realism and compatibility are written as integral probability metrics over different test-function classes: realism over summary statistics F_real, compatibility over the hedging-loss class F_H = {f_h(omega) = C(omega,h) : h in H}. If F_H is contained in F_real then Gamma <= Delta_real, but the authors stress this is a strong condition because realism suites are moments and tail measures, not hedging losses. Theorem 3 assumes F_real is a finite-dimensional linear subspace and some strategy's loss lies outside F_real + span{1}; a signed-measure Jordan-decomposition construction then yields P and Q with Delta_real = 0 and Gamma > 0.

  • The construction is constructive, not tautological: arbitrarily small realism discrepancy does not bound hedging-risk divergence.
  • A generator can reproduce every stylized fact you measure while systematically distorting the loss landscape the hedger optimizes against.
  • Design prescription: the generator must admit stable optimization under finite data AND preserve the loss landscape of admissible strategies INCLUDING frictions like transaction costs.

Experimental grid and calibration

Four generators: GBM and Merton jump (drift fixed to zero), Heston via Andersen's Quadratic-Exponential scheme, and a VAE (3-layer MLP encoder/decoder, 16 hidden units, latent dim 16). Three hedger classes: LinearHedger, NTBNet (predicts a no-transaction band and only trades when the previous position falls outside it), and MLPHedger (4 layers, 32 hidden units, layer norm). Linear/MLP hedgers see log-moneyness, time-to-expiry, 10-day realized volatility, and previous position; NTBNet sees Black-Scholes delta and previous position. Four tasks = {European call, Lookback call} x {cost 1e-4, 1e-3}. Three objectives: ERM(lambda=1), ERM(lambda=50), CVaR(alpha=0.90).

  • Data: Nikkei 225 daily. Calibration 2018-01-01 to 2022-12-31 (1,218 trading days); test 2023-01-01 to 2026-02-28 (771 trading days).
  • Parametric generators calibrated by moment-matching init then L-BFGS-B on a stylized-facts distance; the VAE is trained on 24 extra tickers over the same period.
  • Learnability is proxied (the learning error is unobservable) by a normalized generalization gap and an epoch-over-epoch validation-loss stability measure, both over epochs >= 100.
  • Realism is an 8-metric suite: KS distance of returns, mean-absolute-return diff, kurtosis diff, skewness diff, Hill tail-index diff, ACF deviation of raw and of absolute returns (lags 1-9), and Hurst-exponent diff.

Results: realism is multi-dimensional and anti-correlated with learnability

The N225 test window has kurtosis 15.943, Hill index of absolute returns 2.557, skewness -0.733, and Hurst exponent 0.642. Across the 8 realism metrics no generator wins more than three columns: Heston is best on KS (4.315e-2) and ACF(Abs); VAE is best on MeanAbsRet (1.120e-4), Skew, ACF(Raw) and Hurst; Merton is best on kurtosis (12.752) and Hill (0.237); GBM wins NOTHING. Yet GBM has the lowest generalization gap in every hedger class, and the GBM advantage shrinks sharply for the high-capacity MLPHedger (4.295e-2 vs 4.579e-2 worst) versus the LinearHedger (2.847e-2 vs 7.296e-2).

  • Explicit realism/learnability trade-off: GBM is unrealistic but structurally simple and therefore easy to learn.
  • Learnability is a joint property of generator complexity and hedger capacity -- the MLPHedger 'absorbs' structural differences across generators.
  • NTBNet has by far the largest generalization gaps overall (7.8e-2 to 19.6e-2): the band architecture is the most sensitive to which generator trained it.

Results: rankings reorder across hedgers and tasks

Averaging generator rank over the three objectives, the best generator flips with both hedger architecture and task. For the LinearHedger, GBM is best on European(1e-4) (rank 1.33) and Lookback(1e-4) (1.00) despite being least realistic, but on Lookback(1e-3) the VAE takes rank 1.00. For NTBNet the GBM-vs-VAE ordering largely reverses. For MLPHedger the preference tracks task difficulty: Merton wins the easy European(1e-4), Heston wins Lookback(1e-4), VAE wins both high-cost tasks. The best generator-hedger pair differs in all four tasks.

  • Mechanistic story: NTBNet's function class is piecewise threshold rules, so threshold-crossing dynamics become decision-relevant structure that was irrelevant for the LinearHedger.
  • As hedger capacity rises, the GBM learnability advantage shrinks and the binding constraint shifts to the richness of the data distribution.
  • Conclusion: balance learnability and compatibility with a risk structure aligned to the target hedger class and task, rather than maximize realism.

Key findings

  • Theorem 1: excess risk <= learning error + 2*Gamma(P,Q_theta;H), so a generator only has to preserve the risk ranking of strategies in H, not approximate P globally -- and a less realistic generator can win outright if the simplification cuts learning error by more than 2*Gamma.
  • Theorem 3 makes the realism/compatibility divergence structural: if F_real is a finite-dimensional linear subspace and some admissible strategy's loss lies outside F_real + span{1}, there exist P, Q_theta with realism discrepancy exactly 0 and Gamma > 0.
  • GBM loses every one of the 8 realism columns yet has the lowest generalization gap for all three hedgers (2.847e-2 Linear, 7.782e-2 NTBNet, 4.295e-2 MLP) -- an explicit realism/learnability anti-correlation.
  • No generator is globally best: the winning pair differs in all four tasks (Merton-MLPHedger, GBM-LinearHedger, Heston-MLPHedger, GBM-NTBHedger), and ranks reorder across hedger classes (VAE goes from 3.50 with the LinearHedger to 1.00 with NTBNet on European(1e-4)).
  • Compatibility is architecture-dependent: NTBNet's threshold/band function class makes threshold-crossing dynamics decision-relevant, largely reversing the GBM-vs-VAE ordering and erasing Heston's edge over Merton.
  • Realism is not one-dimensional: Heston leads on KS and ACF(Abs), VAE on MeanAbsRet, Skew, ACF(Raw) and Hurst, Merton on kurtosis and Hill -- no single realism scalar summarizes generator quality.

Notable quotes

GBM performs the worst across almost all metrics, reflecting its simplifying assumptions of Gaussianity and independent increments.
Overall, these results indicate that learnability is jointly determined by the complexity of the generator and the expressive power of the hedger, rather than by the generator alone.
As shown in the table, no single scenario generator or hedger consistently achieves the top performance across all tasks, indicating that there is no globally optimal choice independent of the task setting.
This indicates that the notion of compatibility is not invariant across hedger classes, but depends on the underlying function class induced by the architecture.

Limitations

  • The authors state in the conclusion that compatibility is difficult to measure directly and must be assessed through indirect proxies -- the central quantity of the paper is never computed, only argued about via realism, learnability proxies, and realized out-of-sample risk.
  • Learning error is likewise unobservable and replaced by two heuristic proxies; on the MLPHedger the stability proxy actually favours Heston (0.296e-4) over GBM (0.309e-4), so even the 'GBM is most learnable' claim is not uniform across both proxies.
  • Narrow empirical scope: one underlying (Nikkei 225 daily), 20-day maturity, at-the-money strike, two payoffs, two cost levels, four generators (no GAN, diffusion, rough-volatility or agent-based generator), and results reported as average RANKS rather than absolute risk numbers, so the economic size of the differences is not visible.

Open questions

  • Can compatibility Gamma(P,Q_theta;H) be estimated or bounded from data for a realistic hedger class, so it becomes an actual selection criterion rather than a post-hoc explanation?
  • The authors propose 'compatibility-aware deep hedging' -- jointly designing generator and hedger to optimize decision performance; what is a tractable objective for that, and does it degenerate into overfitting the historical sample?
  • Do the ranking reversals persist for multi-asset portfolios, longer maturities, higher-frequency rebalancing, and modern generative families (diffusion, rough volatility, agent-based)?
