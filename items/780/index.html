Mandate without Managers: Automated Market Makers as Verifiable Portfolio Products

📰 Latest · 2026-08-16
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-08-16

Source: arXiv · 2026-08-03
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02917

The paper reframes a geometric-mean AMM as a verifiable target-weight portfolio product whose arbitrageurs perform mechanical rebalancing. Backtests find fee bands where it beats selected incumbent funds on both return and tracking error, but the evidence relies on idealized arbitrage-only simulations rather than a deployed product.

Executive summary

A geometric mean market maker simultaneously defines exchange prices and the composition of assets held for liquidity providers. The authors introduce a path-independent fee design that makes competitive arbitrage enforce a bounded target-weight region, then compare simulated pools with VBIAX, EQL, and EDOW. Certain fee bands Pareto-dominate the funds on compound growth and mandate tracking, particularly when the benchmark is the economic target-weight mandate rather than the fund’s legal index.

AMM as portfolio mechanism

For a fee-free G3M, arbitrage against external prices restores every asset to its configured target weight. The invariant therefore acts as a portfolio rebalancing rule, and arbitrageurs become external rebalancing agents paid through the value they extract from liquidity providers.

  • The same mechanism functions as both an exchange and a portfolio commitment.
  • Holdings and contemporaneous prices make compliance with the encoded mandate directly observable.
  • The portfolio interpretation is especially natural for Balancer-style multi-asset pools.

Path-independent fee and rebalancing band

Market operations are decomposed into a common proportional mint or burn, which remains fee-free, and a non-proportional residual, which pays fees. The construction prevents a trader from reducing fees by splitting or routing the same operation.

  • Under active arbitrage, each realized weight satisfies ŵᵢ ≥ (1−γ)wᵢ.
  • The complete band is ŵᵢ ∈ [(1−γ)wᵢ, γ+(1−γ)wᵢ].
  • Total allocation drift is bounded by 2γ(1−minᵢwᵢ).
  • The fee parameter is better interpreted as the width of a portfolio-rebalancing band than as an ordinary exchange fee.

Fund comparisons

The authors simulate arbitrage-only pools using Bloomberg total-return data and compare compound annual growth with tracking error. Dominance requires both higher CAGR and lower tracking error.

  • For VBIAX, no daily-tracking dominance region exists, but monthly tracking produces a 2.73%–3.90% fee region.
  • Against EQL’s economic mandate, the NAV comparison is dominated at fees of 3.22%–7.09%.
  • Against EDOW’s economic mandate, the NAV comparison is dominated at fees of 3.32%–9.90%.
  • Against the funds’ legal-index NAVs, the G3M does not dominate EQL or EDOW.

Economic interpretation

Loss-versus-rebalancing becomes the execution cost of maintaining the mandate rather than purely adverse selection. Positive fees allow temporary mis-weighting while retaining some rebalancing proceeds, which creates non-monotonic return-versus-tracking-error frontiers.

  • The simulated market-making component is non-positive because pools receive only adversarial arbitrage flow.
  • The total portfolio can nevertheless outperform incumbent funds because the invariant supplies a valuable portfolio-management service.
  • The authors propose viewing observable AMM reserves and weight bands as verifiable portfolio infrastructure.

Key findings

  • A multi-asset G3M with the proposed fee structure implements an explicitly bounded band-rebalancing strategy.
  • The fund comparison depends materially on whether tracking is measured against a legal index or the underlying economic mandate.
  • The simulated pools can dominate selected funds even though arbitrage-only market making is non-positive by construction.
  • Positive fees can reduce realized tracking error by allowing the pool to retain rebalancing proceeds rather than transferring every opportunity to arbitrageurs.
  • The encoded mandate and permissible weight deviations are externally verifiable from reserves and asset prices.

Notable quotes

In this sense, an AMM is simultaneously an exchange and a portfolio rule.
Importantly, a portfolio is only dominant if it outperforms in both metrics simultaneously (lower TE with higher CAGR).
Together with the empirical performance found in Section 3, this supports evaluating AMMs not only as decentralized exchanges, but also as verifiable portfolio infrastructure.

Limitations

  • The evidence is historical simulation rather than a live pool or randomized comparison.
  • Arbitrage is frictionless, occurs at most once per day at closing prices, and is the only source of order flow.
  • The dominating fee parameters are high relative to ordinary AMM fees and may not be viable exchange spreads.
  • Traditional fund legal mandates, taxes, custody, governance, and operational requirements are not replicated by the AMM simulation.
  • The analysis does not evaluate smart-contract risk, oracle failure, tokenization frictions, or liquidity depth.

Open questions

  • Do the dominance regions survive realistic gas costs, latency, discrete arbitrage, fees, and uninformed flow?
  • Can a live tokenized pool track a regulated mandate with sufficient depth and governance safeguards?
  • How should the rebalancing band adapt to volatility, liquidity, taxes, and transaction costs without losing verifiability?
