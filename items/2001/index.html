    Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models

    ← Go backTraining Compute-Optimal Large Language ModelsNextHacker News — Front Page: HN front page turned over: Danish… →
    📚 Foundations · 2026-10-05 Full Article
    Read full article →View on GitHub

    Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
    Date: 2026-10-05

    Theme: Compute-optimal scaling for language models
    These papers establish empirical power-law methods for allocating language-model training compute, then show how improved experimental design substantially revised the recommended balance between parameters and data.
    Source: arxiv
    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361

    A foundational empirical study linking language-model loss to parameter count, dataset size, and training compute, and deriving how a fixed compute budget should be allocated among them.

    Executive summary

    Kaplan et al. empirically model Transformer language-model cross-entropy as smooth power laws of non-embedding parameter count, dataset size, and optimized training compute. Across a large WebText2 experiment suite, model shape matters comparatively little once scale is controlled. The fitted laws imply that the compute-efficient strategy in the studied regime is to increase model size rapidly, increase data and batch size more slowly, and add very few serial optimization steps.

    Problem and experimental scope

    The paper seeks simple predictive relationships between language-model loss and model size, data, and compute. It trains decoder-only Transformers on WebText2 with a 50,257-token vocabulary and mostly 1,024-token contexts, while varying non-embedding parameter count from 768 to 1.5 billion and dataset size from 22 million to 23 billion tokens.

    • The principal metric is autoregressive cross-entropy loss on held-out WebText2, with additional evaluations on other text distributions.
    • Non-embedding parameters define model size because including embeddings obscured the clean scaling trend.
    • Training compute is approximated as C ≈ 6NBS, where N is non-embedding parameters, B is tokens per batch, and S is parameter-update steps.

    Empirical power-law method

    The authors fit loss curves in regimes where one resource is limiting while the others are sufficiently large. They find L(N) proportional to N^-0.076, L(D) proportional to D^-0.095, and optimally allocated L(C_min) proportional to C_min^-0.050, with trends spanning multiple orders of magnitude. Depth, width, attention-head count, and feed-forward dimension have only weak effects over the tested range at fixed non-embedding parameter count.

    • The fitted parameter-limited scale is N_c = 8.8 × 10^13 non-embedding parameters.
    • The fitted data-limited scale is D_c = 5.4 × 10^13 tokens.
    • The optimized-compute fit uses C_c^min = 3.1 × 10^8 PF-days.
    • Loss on other evaluated text distributions improves in close parallel with in-distribution loss, separated largely by a distribution-dependent offset.

    Joint model-data scaling and overfitting

    A joint early-stopped loss model combines the parameter- and data-limited terms and predicts when either resource becomes a bottleneck. Its exponent ratio implies that avoiding a fixed overfitting penalty requires dataset size to grow approximately as N^0.74, so an eightfold increase in model size requires roughly a fivefold increase in data in this fitted regime.

    • The model predicts diminishing returns when parameter count grows at fixed data or data grows at fixed model capacity.
    • Using a roughly 0.02 loss-variation threshold, the paper estimates D ≳ 5 × 10^3 N^0.74 tokens to limit overfitting.
    • The joint fit is poor in the smallest-data regime, where an epoch contains only about 40 updates.

    Training dynamics and critical batch size

    Training curves are parameterized by a model-size term plus a power law in the minimum serial steps needed at large batch size. The critical batch size is treated as a function of attained loss rather than model size, enabling observed runs to be adjusted toward minimum-compute or minimum-step regimes.

    • The learning-curve fit gives α_S = 0.76 and S_c = 2.1 × 10^3 steps.
    • The critical-batch fit gives α_B = 0.21 and B* = 2.1 × 10^8 tokens.
    • Training near the critical batch size provides a compromise between compute efficiency and wall-clock parallelism.
    • The fitted curves allow early portions of training to predict later attainable loss, although the power law does not describe the initial transient well.

    Compute-efficient allocation

    Combining the loss, learning-curve, and critical-batch fits yields a compute-efficient frontier. Empirically, optimal model size grows as C_min^0.73, batch size as C_min^0.24, serial steps as only C_min^0.03, and processed data as C_min^0.27; thus most additional compute is assigned to parameters rather than prolonged optimization.

    • A tenfold compute increase corresponds to approximately a fivefold increase in optimal model size in the reported fit.
    • The appendix predicts compute-efficient training should stop around 10% above a model's converged loss.
    • Compared with training to 2% above convergence, the fitted model predicts 2.7× more parameters, 7.7× fewer update steps, and 65% less compute to reach the same loss.
    • Models from roughly 0.6× to 2.2× the estimated optimum incur no more than about 20% excess compute, exposing a practical model-size/inference-cost tradeoff.

    Significance and extrapolation limits

    The work turns observed scaling into a quantitative planning framework for model, data, batch, and compute budgets and became an early baseline for reasoning about large-language-model training. The paper also stresses that the trends must eventually break: natural language has nonzero entropy, its compute- and data-based extrapolations ultimately conflict, and the experiments do not establish that lower loss always yields useful task-level capabilities.

    • The authors explicitly call for a theory explaining the empirical exponents and corrections to scaling.
    • They identify an uncertain extrapolated intersection near 10^4 PF-days, 10^12 parameters, 10^12 tokens, and 1.7 nats per token where their fitted laws cannot all remain valid.
    • They propose testing analogous laws in images, audio, video, and other generative settings.
    • The results motivate model parallelism, sparsity, and growing-network methods because compute-efficient models become large while serial training grows slowly.

    Key findings

    • Language-model loss follows smooth empirical power laws in non-embedding parameters, data, and optimized training compute over the studied ranges.
    • At fixed parameter count, common Transformer shape choices affect performance only weakly compared with overall scale.
    • Model size and data must grow together; the fitted overfitting boundary scales approximately as D ∝ N^0.74.
    • The critical batch size depends primarily on attained loss, allowing training curves from different model sizes to be normalized into a common compute/step framework.
    • Within the paper's regime, compute-efficient scaling assigns most new compute to parameters, with much slower growth in data, batch size, and especially serial update steps.

    Notable quotes

    Unless otherwise noted, all training runs included in our data used a learning rate schedule with a 3000 step linear warmup followed by a cosine decay to zero.
    Since compute-efficient training uses relatively few optimization steps, additional work on speeding up early training dynamics may be warranted.
    At this point we do not know which of our results depend on the structure of natural language data, and which are universal.
    Smooth quantitative change can mask major qualitative improvements: “more is different”.

    Limitations

    • The fitted laws lack a solid theoretical derivation, so their correction terms, universality, and reliable extrapolation range are unknown.
    • The critical-batch extrapolation is uncertain, and the model-data fit performs poorly in the smallest-data regime; regularization and data augmentation were not systematically explored.
    • The compute estimate omits context-length-dependent costs and may be confounded for very long contexts; learning-rate and other hyperparameter tuning was also incomplete.

    Open questions

    • Can a statistical-mechanical or optimization theory derive the observed exponents and predict when the power laws break?
    • Which scaling relations transfer beyond WebText2 language modeling to other data distributions and to image, audio, video, or other generative models?
    • When and how do smooth improvements in cross-entropy translate into qualitative or downstream task capabilities?
