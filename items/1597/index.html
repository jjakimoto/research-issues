    Date: 2026-09-18

    Source: huggingface-daily-papers · 2026-09-14
    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15818

    A 744B-parameter MoE agentic model trained purely on externally verified execution traces ties frontier agents on 16 agentic benchmarks (best on 5), but the more useful half of the paper is the instrumented log of its own development: 769 task records showing agents proposed 64.6% of methods while humans made 85.5% of final calls — a concrete picture of where an agent actually pays off in an R&D loop.

    Executive summary

    Atria Dawn Preview is an agentic LM built on a 744B-parameter MoE base and post-trained through a 'Verifiable Experience Pipeline': every training task runs in a real execution environment and only trajectories whose outcomes are confirmed by external signals (tests, metrics, file/app state, geometric checks, source support) are retained. Across 16 benchmarks it reports the top score on 5 and second on 3, while trailing clearly on SWE-bench Pro (59.6 vs Claude Opus 5's 74.7), Terminal-Bench 2.1 (78.3 vs 90.2), GDPval (1583 vs 1768) and JobBench (50.3 vs 68.0). The second half analyzes 769 task records from 56 participants during the model's own development: AI used in 96.5% of tasks, one-third of completed AI-assisted tasks rated infeasible without AI, agents proposing most options but humans retaining nearly all final decisions. The authors' own caveat is that Table 1 is 'as reported on the official release website' — vendor-published numbers, not an independent reproduction.

    Training recipe: the Verifiable Experience Pipeline

    The post-training story is stated at a high level with no hyperparameters, RL algorithm, dataset sizes, or compute reported. Every task is bound to a live environment; the agent observes state, calls tools, produces intermediate artifacts, adapts to feedback; the outcome is then checked by a domain-specific external verifier rather than a learned reward model or preference judge. A curation stage discards incomplete, contradictory, duplicate, or behaviorally invalid runs, and the surviving record keeps the task-trajectory-artifact-evidence links intact. Failure analysis is a first-class loop input: recurring failure modes become new tasks and new quality checks. For long-horizon work the recorded experience explicitly includes launching jobs, tailing logs, and rewriting execution plans — they train the recovery behavior, not just the happy path.

    • Base model: 744B-parameter mixture-of-experts; no architecture changes described
    • Verifiers used: executable tests, experiment metrics, file/application state, geometric checks, source support, human-defined criteria
    • Named recurring failure modes driving new task construction: ineffective tool selection, incomplete verification, missing evidence, unsuccessful recovery
    • No RL algorithm, reward shaping, data volume, or training compute is disclosed — a capability report, not a reproducible recipe

    Benchmark results and where it actually wins

    Sixteen benchmarks against six comparison models. The wins cluster in tool invocation, deep search, and security. It is second and within 2 points on SkillsBench, Workspace-Bench, and Workspace-Bench-Lite. The losses cluster in long-horizon coding and professional deliverables — exactly the workloads an agent-builder cares most about. Missing entries are marked '-' and not treated as zero, which also means several head-to-head columns are incomplete.

    • Strong: search/browse (BrowseComp 92.5, DeepSearchQA 96.0), tool calling (BFCL v4 77.0), security (CyberGym 86.5)
    • Weak vs Claude Opus 5: SWE-bench Pro 59.6 vs 74.7, JobBench 50.3 vs 68.0, Terminal-Bench 2.1 78.3 vs 90.2, GDPval Elo 1583 vs 1768
    • MLE-bench Lite 86.2 (HumanRank) is mid-pack: below GPT 5.6 sol 88.9, Claude Opus 5 88.0, DeepSeek V4 Pro 86.8
    • The paper concedes GDPval and JobBench 'retain the clearest headroom for transferring general agentic ability to long-horizon professional work'

    Vendor-benchmark caveats worth knowing before you cite the table

    Table 1's caption says the scores are 'as reported on the official release website' — a self-reported leaderboard snapshot, with comparison models' numbers pulled from their own vendors' reports rather than re-run under a common harness. Appendix B discloses per-benchmark protocols in unusual detail, and those protocols vary wildly in harness, scaffold, and judge model, which is where most of the cross-model comparability risk lives.

    • Different harnesses per benchmark: Claude Code v2.1.224 (Terminal-Bench 2.1, 12x timeout multiplier, 500 turns, 4 runs), OpenHands v1.23.0 (SWE-bench Pro, 4.5h, no network), OpenHands/AgentCompass (SkillsBench, GDPval, Workspace-Bench), OpenCode (JobBench, 65 tasks)
    • LLM judges differ per benchmark: GPT-5.5, GPT-5.2 simulator+judge, Doubao Seed 2.0 Lite, glm-5.3 pairwise Elo over 220 questions, Grok-4.3
    • Subsetting: SkillsBench is a 79-task subset over 3 runs; tau^3-Bench Banking is 97 tasks, seed 300; BFCL v4 is 5,217 cases at temp 0.001
    • Search benchmarks run in an in-house harness at 500 steps and 256K context — the least comparable setting in the table

    Case studies: what 'verified' looks like in practice

    Four demo areas, labelled as selected demonstrations rather than average success rates, distinguishing generated artifacts from the checks actually available. The GDN kernel case is the most interesting for agent builders because it shows a measure-implement-remeasure loop and then honestly refuses to promote the development measurement into a benchmark claim.

    • Weather forecasting, web search OFF: >100 GB of data, ViT-based net >0.4B params, 45,000 training steps, 69 meteorological variables; the figure 'does not report forecast accuracy'
    • Gated Delta Network decode optimization: identified launch overhead, migrated Triton to native CUDA, measured a 1.46x ratio of summed baseline vs candidate latencies over seven batch sizes; 52 of 54 formal workloads passed but no complete formal mean, so explicitly not a final benchmark score
    • MiniOS: built from an empty workspace in ~20 minutes with serial shell, disk access, persistent filesystem and an interpreter; verified across two QEMU restarts
    • CAD assemblies with the caveat that renders are 'not mechanical or manufacturing validation'

    The human-AI collaboration study (the part that generalizes)

    56 participants, 769 task records plus agent logs, collected during the actual development of the model. The headline dynamic: delegation volume is rising fast while decision authority is not moving at all. The daily median ratio of agent actions per human prompt went from 11.0 to 28.5 in four weeks, yet humans still selected the final option in 85.5% of method/parameter decisions, 93.4% of goal/scope decisions, and 81.9% of acceptance-criteria decisions. Where AI's share swings is proposal generation (16.9% to 55.4%) while its final-decision share stays pinned between 6.1% and 9.2%. The recovery data is the most actionable for anyone building agent harnesses: when work went wrong, help was overwhelmingly informational, not manual.

    • AI used in 713 of 739 tasks with a clear response (96.5%); across 567 methods and decisions AI proposed 64.6%
    • 151 of 455 completed AI-assisted tasks (33.2%) rated infeasible without AI, spread across 27 of 56 participants; in that subset humans still picked the final goal in 95.4% of cases
    • Of 588 tasks with a recorded difficulty: 76.0% resumed via human intervention, 23.0% self-recovered, 1.0% unresolved
    • Composition of that human help: context/clarification 35.2%, diagnosis/method change 34.7%, partial edits only 3.2%, full takeover only 0.7%
    • Of 627 outputs, 354 (56.5%) were substantively revised; within those the AI did the revising after feedback 75.4% of the time vs 19.2% human direct edit; 95.9% of outputs entered the deliverable

    Open challenges and the RSI framing

    Section 3 sketches three stages (AI as research object, task runner, project-level partner) plus a speculative fourth raising recursive self-improvement questions, and argues benchmark gains do not automatically convert into research judgment. Four open challenges follow: generating genuinely diverse research directions; converting accumulated experience into intrinsic capability rather than retrieval; preserving meaningful oversight as agent chains outrun human inspection; and establishing explicit authority-allocation protocols. They report that researchers used Codex yolo mode and Claude Code skip-permissions purely so long runs would not stall on approvals.

    • Diagnosis of proposal homogeneity: most agent proposals were variations inside a direction a researcher had already fixed
    • Experience does not compound: what an agent gained from a failed run stayed in that session, while the researcher carried the lesson forward
    • Oversight risk framed as humans reduced to reviewers who can only say 'yes', with human involvement becoming a placebo

    Key findings

    • Built on a 744B-parameter MoE base and trained via a Verifiable Experience Pipeline that retains only trajectories whose outcomes are confirmed by external signals; no RL algorithm, data scale, or compute is disclosed.
    • Top reported score on 5 of 16 benchmarks and second on 3 — AutomationBench 53.8 (+4.1 over runner-up), BFCL v4 77.0 (+2.9), CyberGym 86.5 (+2.0), DeepSearchQA 96.0, BrowseComp 92.5 — but clearly behind Claude Opus 5 on SWE-bench Pro (59.6 vs 74.7), JobBench (50.3 vs 68.0), Terminal-Bench 2.1 (78.3 vs 90.2) and GDPval (1583 vs 1768).
    • Table 1 is self-reported from the official release website, and Appendix B shows each benchmark runs in a different harness (Claude Code, OpenHands, OpenCode, in-house) with different LLM judges — cross-model comparability is weak by construction.
    • Delegation volume nearly tripled in four weeks (median 11.0 to 28.5 agent actions per human prompt, Aug 7 - Sep 4, 2026) while AI's share of final decisions stayed flat at 6.1%-9.2%; what scaled was execution per unit of human judgment, not autonomy.
    • 33.2% of completed AI-assisted tasks (151/455, from 27 of 56 participants) were rated infeasible without AI — yet in that same subset humans still selected the final goal in 95.4% of cases.
    • Agent recovery is still weak: only 23.0% of 588 difficulties were self-resolved, and human rescues were 35.2% context/clarification plus 34.7% diagnosis/method change versus 3.2% partial edits and 0.7% takeover — the bottleneck is information the agent lacked, not human hands on the keyboard.
    • The GDN kernel case reports a 1.46x development latency ratio over seven batch sizes with 52 of 54 formal workloads passing, and the paper explicitly declines to present this as a final benchmark score.

    Notable quotes

    Only experience that connects a task to its trajectory, artifacts, and verification evidence is incorporated into the model’s reusable capabilities.
    A model may therefore become better at the tasks it is trained on without becoming better at developing its successor.
    Human help was thus about eighteen times more likely to change what the agent knew than to change who did the work.
    Convenience, rather than any deliberate allocation of authority, is what set the boundary in practice.

    Limitations

    • All benchmark numbers in Table 1 are self-reported from the vendor's release website, with comparison models' scores taken from their own reports and each benchmark run under a different harness and LLM judge — the ranking should not be read as a controlled head-to-head.
    • The training section describes the Verifiable Experience Pipeline only qualitatively: no RL objective, no data volume, no compute, no ablation isolating the pipeline's contribution from the 744B MoE base model it inherits.
    • The collaboration study is single-organization self-report, the case studies are explicitly selected demonstrations rather than average success rates, and several artifacts carry no accuracy or validation check at all.

    Open questions

    • Can an agent generate genuinely diverse research directions and rank their value before results exist, rather than producing variations inside a direction a human already fixed (AI proposed only 16.9% of goal/scope options)?
    • How can an agent absorb experience into intrinsic capability rather than retrieving it — the authors suggest test-time training and hint that architectures separating memory from capability may need fundamental redesign?
    • What authority-allocation protocol should replace the de facto boundary set by convenience (yolo / skip-permissions modes), and how is oversight kept meaningful once each human decision rests on a chain of agent work no one can inspect?
