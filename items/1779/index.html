    Loss Choice or Model Choice? The Role of Forecast Level in Cryptocurrency Volatility Forecasting

    ← Go backReasoning Instructions Can Break Answer Decoding in…NextModel-Free Passive Execution via Order-Level Shadowing →
    📰 Latest · 2026-09-26 Full Article
    Read full article →View on GitHub

    Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
    Date: 2026-09-26

    Source: arxiv · 2026-09-22
    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27024

    Most of the apparent 'training loss matters a lot' effect in crypto volatility forecasting turns out to be a forecast-level artifact: once forecasts are rescaled to match validation-set level, model architecture dominates and cross-loss VaR breach-rate gaps shrink by 97% - a sharp warning against reading raw QLIKE comparisons as evidence about dynamics rather than level.

    Executive summary

    The authors run a walk-forward comparison of 7 training losses x 5 forecasting models on 5 major cryptocurrencies (BTC, ETH, BNB, XRP, ADA) to disentangle how much of the 'loss function matters' effect is really just a difference in forecast level versus genuine day-to-day dynamics. The raw loss effect is about 2.9x larger than the model effect, but a simple validation-based constant rescaling removes 77% of that cross-loss variation on average, flipping the ratio below 1 in the full comparison. The same story holds for one-day 5% Value-at-Risk: raw breach rates span 1.28%-6.31% across losses but converge to 3.48%-3.61% after alignment - though Kupiec coverage tests still reject calibration in the majority of series even post-alignment.

    Motivation and research questions

    Volatility-forecasting research has mostly held the training loss fixed while varying model architecture, treating loss choice as a neutral background decision. But different losses target different conditional quantities (citing Gneiting 2011), so identical models trained on identical data under different losses can differ mainly in persistent forecast level rather than responsiveness to new information - an ambiguity that matters because level feeds directly into risk measures like VaR.

    • RQ1: How large is the effect of loss function choice relative to model choice?
    • RQ2: Does the loss mainly change forecast level, or also its day-to-day dynamics?
    • RQ3: How do these differences affect one-day VaR before and after forecast-level alignment?

    Seven losses and the alignment / LevelShare method

    Using the observed Garman-Klass variance proxy and the one-day-ahead forecast, the seven per-observation losses are grouped by what conditional quantity they target: log-error losses (MSE-log targets the geometric mean; Huber-log with delta=1.0; Pinball targets the median), mean-target losses (QLIKE, Patton-b with b=-1), and relative-error losses (HMAE, HMSE).

    • Marginal variation is compared via the average over 21 loss pairs and 10 model pairs of log mean test QLIKE, computed per block and summarized by the median ratio over 25 blocks.
    • Alignment: one constant, the mean over validation dates of the realized/forecast ratio, is estimated per loss-model fit and applied to test forecasts - changing level while preserving relative movement.
    • LevelShare measures the fraction of the log-forecast difference between two losses under the same model that is a pure level shift; values near 1 mean the difference is almost entirely level.

    Data, models, and walk-forward design

    Daily Binance USDT spot OHLCV data through 31 May 2026 for BTC, ETH, BNB, XRP, ADA (BTC/ETH from 5 October 2017, others from listing). The target is next-day unannualized Garman-Klass variance, with Parkinson and Rogers-Satchell proxies used for robustness. Five models are compared: HAR, HAR-X (adds returns, leverage, alternative proxies, volume, lagged BTC variance for altcoins), NeuralHAR and LightGBM (share HAR-X predictors), and DLinear (uses 48-day target history instead).

    • Five expanding walk-forward folds contain 1,013-2,529 training days, 253 validation days, and 379 test days, with disjoint test windows across the 25 asset-fold blocks.
    • HAR/HAR-X are estimated deterministically; NeuralHAR, DLinear, and LightGBM use 3 seeds with validation-based early stopping.
    • QLIKE is the primary evaluation score; MSE-log, HMAE, and alternative volatility proxies test robustness. Confidence intervals use a stationary bootstrap (1000 samples, expected block length 10).
    • VaR is one-day 5% Gaussian VaR from the variance forecast; coverage is assessed with the Kupiec (1995) test.

    RQ1: the loss effect dwarfs the model effect before alignment

    QLIKE and Patton-b generally achieve the lowest (best) QLIKE scores across HAR, HAR-X, NeuralHAR and DLinear, while HMSE is worst for HAR/HAR-X but becomes competitive for LightGBM (0.659, near that column's best of 0.637).

    • Loss/model variation ratio median = 2.91 (95% CI [2.27, 3.25]) - cross-loss score differences are roughly 2.9x larger than cross-model differences.
    • The ratio stays above 1 with shared predictors (2.73, CI [2.01, 3.44]) and across individual assets (range 1.44-4.55), and under alternative evaluation scores: 3.71 (MSE-log), 3.98 (HMAE).
    • Loss rankings are robust to the volatility proxy: Spearman correlation of 1.000 vs Rogers-Satchell and 0.964 vs Parkinson.

    RQ2 and RQ3: alignment reverses the ranking and collapses the VaR gaps

    LevelShare shows forecast-level differences account for 89% of cross-loss variation for HAR but only 56% for LightGBM, meaning tree-based models retain more genuinely loss-specific daily dynamics. After validation-based alignment, cross-loss variation drops 59%-88% depending on model (77% overall, 95% CI [72%, 79%]), and the loss/model ratio falls from 2.91 to 0.67 (95% CI [0.56, 0.99]) in the full grid - model choice now dominates. With shared inputs the aligned ratio is 0.81 (CI [0.66, 1.22]), which the authors call inconclusive.

    • Raw one-day 5% VaR breach rates range from 1.28% (HMSE) to 6.31% (Pinball) around a 5% nominal target; alignment narrows this to 3.48%-3.61%, removing 97% of cross-loss variation.
    • Spearman correlation between loss rankings under QLIKE vs MSE-log evaluation is -0.200: the 'best' loss depends heavily on which score is used.
    • Kupiec rejects correct VaR coverage in 128 of 175 asset-loss-model series before alignment and still 109 of 175 after.
    • Aggregate aligned breach rates stay under 5%, so cross-loss convergence after alignment does not imply the VaR model is correctly calibrated.

    Key findings

    • Before alignment, cross-loss score variation is ~2.9x cross-model variation (median ratio 2.91, 95% CI [2.27, 3.25]); after validation-based level alignment the ratio flips to 0.67 (CI [0.56, 0.99]) in the full-predictor grid.
    • LevelShare - the fraction of cross-loss variation explained by a constant level offset - is 89% for HAR but only 56% for LightGBM, so tree-based models retain more genuinely loss-driven dynamics.
    • Alignment reduces cross-loss QLIKE variation by 59%-88% depending on model (77% overall, 95% CI [72%, 79%]).
    • Raw one-day 5% VaR breach rates range from 1.28% (HMSE) to 6.31% (Pinball); after alignment they converge to 3.48%-3.61%, removing 97% of cross-loss variation.
    • Even after alignment, Kupiec still rejects correct VaR coverage in 109 of 175 asset-loss-model series (down from 128), so level-alignment narrows but does not fix miscalibration.
    • Loss rankings under QLIKE vs MSE-log evaluation have Spearman correlation -0.200: the choice of evaluation score itself can flip which loss looks best.

    Notable quotes

    Five expanding folds contain 1,013-2,529 training, 253 validation and 379 test days, with disjoint test windows.
    Its median is 2.91 (95% CI [2.27, 3.25]), making cross-loss differences about 2.9 times larger.
    It is 89% for HAR but 56% for LightGBM, which retains more loss-specific daily variation.
    Alignment narrows this range to 3.5%-3.6% and removes 97% of its cross-loss variation.
    Fixed settings exclude loss-specific tuning, while constant alignment cannot correct time-varying bias.

    Limitations

    • Fixed non-loss hyperparameters across all loss-model combinations mean no loss-specific tuning was performed, which could change the magnitude of the raw loss effect.
    • Alignment is a single constant multiplicative rescaling per loss-model fit; it corrects a persistent level offset but cannot correct time-varying bias.
    • VaR results depend on OHLC-based volatility proxies and a Gaussian return/VaR model, so residual miscalibration could partly reflect these choices.
    • Scope is 5 cryptocurrencies, one-day-ahead forecasts, 5 models, and 7 losses; the robustness check with shared predictors gives an aligned ratio of 0.81 with CI [0.66, 1.22], which the authors call statistically inconclusive.

    Open questions

    • Would loss-specific hyperparameter tuning change the magnitude or direction of the post-alignment reversal?
    • Would a time-varying (rather than constant) alignment close more of the residual post-alignment VaR miscalibration, still 109/175 Kupiec rejections?
    • Does the finding that model choice dominates loss choice after alignment hold for other asset classes, longer horizons, or non-Gaussian VaR/ES models?
