    False (and Missed) Discoveries in Financial Economics

    ← Go backPublication Bias in Asset Pricing ResearchNextHacker News — Front Page: Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6 launch… →
    📚 Foundations · 2026-10-08 Full Article
    Read full article →View on GitHub

    Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
    Date: 2026-10-08

    Theme: Statistical guardrails for financial discovery
    Quantitative research is a correlated multiple-testing problem with costs on both sides: accepting false signals and discarding real ones. These papers show why thresholds must be calibrated to the candidate universe, dependence structure, plausible signal prevalence, and publication process rather than treated as universal constants.
    Source: arxiv
    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04269

    Provides data-dependent multiple-testing thresholds that jointly expose false discoveries and missed genuine signals.

    Executive summary

    The paper develops a double-bootstrap framework for calibrating both Type I false discoveries and Type II missed discoveries when many correlated strategies or managers are tested. A prior fraction of true alternatives, p0, makes the high-dimensional alternative tractable: one bootstrap constructs pseudo-data with known true and null cases, while a second measures errors under a chosen threshold or correction. Applications to investment strategies, 18,113 anomalies, and mutual funds show that finite-sample performance is highly data-dependent and that controlling false positives alone can leave severe missed-discovery risk.

    Problem and decision target

    Conventional multiple-testing corrections emphasize false positives, even though investors and researchers also bear costs from overlooking genuine strategies or skilled managers. The paper therefore evaluates false discovery and false omission rates together and introduces a ratio that can reflect their relative economic costs.

    • The realized false discovery rate is the fraction of false positives among all discoveries.
    • The realized rate of misses is the fraction of false negatives among all tests declared insignificant.
    • The false-discovery-to-miss ratio links the two errors; if a false discovery costs ten times as much as a miss, the paper associates the target ratio with 1/10.

    Double-bootstrap calibration

    Given N return series and an assumed fraction p0 of true strategies, the first bootstrap resamples time periods, ranks t-statistics, treats the top p0 fraction as alternatives, assigns them data-derived nonzero means, and sets the remaining in-sample means to zero. The second bootstrap repeatedly resamples this pseudo-population, applies a threshold or testing method, counts true and false positives and negatives, and averages the resulting error measures across both bootstrap stages.

    • Resampling time periods while retaining the cross-section captures dependence nonparametrically.
    • The framework can calibrate a data-specific threshold or audit methods such as BH, BY, Storey, Romano-Shaikh-Wolf, and the Fama-French bootstrap.
    • The authors interpret p0 as either a frequentist plug-in value or a partial Bayesian prior and recommend sensitivity analysis when it is uncertain.

    Investment-strategy evidence

    The empirical strategy samples contain 484 CAPIQ strategies from 1985–2014 and 18,113 mechanically generated anomalies. Their signal environments differ sharply: 22.1% of CAPIQ strategies, or 107 of 484, have t-statistics above 2.0, compared with 5.5%, or 989 of 18,113, among the anomalies, so the same correction does not perform uniformly across the two data sets.

    • For CAPIQ with p0=10%, a 5% false-discovery target implies a t-statistic hurdle of 2.4, leaving 18% of strategies; targeting five misses per false discovery implies 2.6, leaving 15%.
    • BH performs relatively well for CAPIQ, whereas BY is the strict-control choice for the anomaly sample and Storey with theta=0.8 is an alternative when p0 is at most 10%.
    • At a 10% false-discovery target, the preferred corrections classify only about 0.1%, roughly 18, of the 18,000 anomaly strategies as true—not the 10%, or 1,800, inferred in prior work.

    Why percentile rejection overstates discoveries

    The Fama-French procedure tests the joint null that every candidate has zero performance; rejecting a cross-sectional percentile establishes that some alternatives exist but does not identify their proportion. Simulations show that using percentile rejection as a population-share estimator is biased upward because lucky null strategies can crowd genuine strategies in the ranking and shift several percentiles.

    • With an actual true fraction of 0.5%, the inferred fraction averages 2.06%, and the procedure still has a 15% probability of declaring at least the top 10% significant.
    • With an actual true fraction of 10%, the inferred fraction averages 35.68%, close to the simulation's 40% cap.
    • A joint global test and a multiple-testing procedure that identifies individual signals answer different questions and must not be interpreted interchangeably.

    Missed discoveries among mutual funds

    For 3,030 mutual funds over 1984–2006, the Fama-French bootstrap generally controls Type I error but has very low power. When 2% of funds are simulated as outperformers with average annual alpha of 10.66% and an average alpha t-statistic of 3.30, even its best statistic at the 5% level has an 86.9% Type II error; with 5% outperformers, the minimum remains 33.6%.

    • The principal mechanism is undersampling: bootstrapped short-history funds can generate unrealistically extreme t-statistics, distorting the null cross-section.
    • The most extreme statistics are not necessarily most powerful; at larger p0 values, the 98th or 99th percentile often outperforms the maximum and 99.9th percentile.
    • Dropping funds with fewer than 36 or 60 observations, or requiring complete subsample histories, improves power and yields modest evidence of outperformance, but introduces sample-selection concerns.

    Key findings

    • Calibrating only Type I error can conceal a large probability of missing economically meaningful signals.
    • The appropriate hurdle depends on the data and the plausible fraction of true alternatives, so universal t-statistic cutoffs are unreliable.
    • BH, BY, Storey, and Romano-Shaikh-Wolf exhibit materially different finite-sample behavior across CAPIQ and anomaly data.
    • Rejecting a joint null at a given percentile cannot identify the fraction of genuine signals and can exaggerate that fraction by orders of magnitude.
    • The Fama-French mutual-fund test's apparent absence of skilled managers is weakened by Type II error rates as high as 86.9% in a simulation with 2% strong outperformers.

    Notable quotes

    Given the difficulty in determining the Type II error rate, current multiple-testing adjustments often focus only on Type I errors.
    Second, we evaluate error rates using a bootstrap method, which allows us to capture cross-sectional dependence nonparametrically.
    It is therefore important to customize the performance evaluation of a multiple-testing method to the data being examined.
    The Fama and French (2010) approach lacks power to detect outperforming funds.

    Limitations

    • The choice of p0 is inherently subjective, and first-stage ranking can mistake lucky nulls for alternatives.
    • The CAPIQ sample is selected toward historically successful strategies and may contain publication bias.
    • The framework targets statistical selection rather than full portfolio utility, while fund-history filters that improve power can introduce selection biases.

    Open questions

    • Can a full Bayesian model estimate a distribution over p0 rather than relying on selected values and sensitivity analysis?
    • How should magnitude-weighted losses, portfolio covariance, capacity, and investor utility enter the false-discovery versus missed-discovery objective?
    • Can bootstrap designs preserve cross-sectional dependence and retain short-history funds without producing undersampling artifacts?
