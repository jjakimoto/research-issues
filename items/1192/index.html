Rethinking Synthetic Scenario Realism: Compatibility, Not Fidelity, Drives Hedging Performance

📰 Latest · 2026-08-30 Full Article
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-08-30

Source: arxiv · 2026-08-21
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20842

Deep hedging teams routinely pick a synthetic path generator by how well it reproduces stylized facts, but this paper proves the deployed hedger's excess risk splits into a learning error plus a compatibility gap, and that a generator can match every realism statistic exactly while still distorting the hedger's loss landscape. Empirically the generator ranking reshuffles completely across hedger architectures and option/transaction-cost settings, so generator choice is a joint design decision with the hedger, not a standalone fidelity contest.

Executive summary

Hashimoto, Hirano, Ozaki and Imajo (University of Tokyo / Preferred Networks) reframe scenario-generator design for deep hedging from a fidelity problem into a decision-alignment problem. They define generator-hedger compatibility as the worst-case risk discrepancy over the admissible strategy class, prove the deployed hedger's excess risk under the true measure is bounded by the learning error plus twice the compatibility gap, then prove constructively that zero realism discrepancy is compatible with a strictly positive compatibility gap whenever the realism test-function class does not span the loss-relevant directions. A 4-generator x 3-hedger x 4-task study calibrated on Nikkei 225 confirms it: GBM is the least realistic generator but the most learnable and often the best performer, and generator rankings reorder across hedger classes and task difficulty.

Problem: realism is the wrong objective for a training-data generator

Deep hedging trains a neural policy on synthetic price paths because real market data is too scarce. The literature evaluates those generators almost exclusively on realism - how well they reproduce stylized facts (heavy tails, volatility clustering, ACF structure) - under the implicit assumption that more realistic paths produce better deployed hedgers. The paper points out the two research lines are disconnected: hedger design is judged by decision performance while generator design is judged by fidelity, so nobody has established that realism buys hedging performance.

  • Setup: single underlying, price path omega = (S_0,...,S_T), true law P, generator-induced law Q_theta, hedging strategy h in an admissible class H, terminal P&L C(omega,h), risk functional rho.
  • Two layers of approximation: distributional (P replaced by Q_theta) and optimizational (population minimizer replaced by the finite-sample empirical minimizer).
  • The quantity that matters is the deployed gap rho_P(hat-h_{Q_theta}) - rho_P(h*_P), measured under the TRUE law.

Theory 1: the compatibility-gap decomposition (Theorem 1)

Compatibility is Gamma(P, Q_theta; H) := sup over h in H of |rho_P(h) - rho_{Q_theta}(h)| - a uniform risk-evaluation discrepancy taken only over the admissible hedger class, not over all measurable functions. Theorem 1 bounds the deployed excess risk by the learning error plus 2*Gamma. The proof is three lines of add-and-subtract plus optimality of the population minimizer under Q_theta.

  • Consequence 1: the generator does NOT need to approximate P globally - only to preserve the risk evaluation of strategies in H (decision-relevant realism).
  • Consequence 2: a generator with a NON-ZERO compatibility gap can still win if its induced simplification cuts the learning error by more than 2*Gamma. This is the trade-off the experiments exhibit.
  • Compatibility is not an intrinsic property of the generator - it is a property of the (generator, hedger class) pair.

Theory 2: realism and compatibility diverge structurally (Prop. 2 / Theorem 3)

Both notions are written as integral probability metrics. Realism is a supremum over feature/statistic test functions; compatibility is the same supremum over the hedging-loss class. Proposition 2 gives the alignment condition: if the loss class sits inside the realism class then realism upper-bounds compatibility - a strong requirement, since realism classes in practice contain moments and tail measures, not expected hedging losses over a constrained strategy class. Theorem 3 is the negative result: if the realism class is a finite-dimensional linear subspace and some strategy's loss lies outside it plus the constants, there exist P and Q_theta with zero realism discrepancy and strictly positive compatibility gap.

  • Theorem 3's proof is constructive: pick m+2 points making the design matrix full rank, take a nonzero vector in the kernel of its first m+1 rows, form a signed measure, split its Jordan decomposition into P and Q.
  • Every realism test function integrates to zero against the signed measure while the loss function does not, so the measures are indistinguishable to the realism metric yet induce different hedging risks.
  • Design implication (Sec. 4.3): a generator must both admit stable optimization under finite data and limited capacity, AND preserve the loss landscape of admissible strategies including frictions such as transaction costs.

Experimental configuration (Section 5)

Four generators x three hedgers x four tasks, calibrated to the Nikkei 225. Generators: GBM, Merton jump process, Heston stochastic volatility (Andersen Quadratic-Exponential scheme), and a VAE. Drift fixed to zero for GBM and Merton. The VAE encoder and decoder are three-layer MLPs with 16 hidden units and latent dimension 16. Hedgers: LinearHedger, No-Transaction Band Network (NTBNet), and a four-layer MLPHedger with 32 hidden units, ReLU, identity output and layer normalization. Linear and MLP hedgers see four features (log-moneyness, time-to-expiry, 10-day realized volatility, previous position); NTBNet sees Black-Scholes delta and previous position.

  • Tasks: {European call, Lookback call} x {transaction cost 1.000e-4, 1.000e-3}. Initial price 1.000, strike 1.000, maturity 20 days = 20 decision steps.
  • Risk measures: ERM lambda=1, ERM lambda=50, CVaR alpha=0.90 - hedgers trained independently, each evaluated with the SAME measure it was trained on.
  • Data: N225 daily. Calibration 2018-01-01 to 2022-12-31 (1,218 trading days); test 2023-01-01 to 2026-02-28 (771 trading days). The VAE is additionally trained on 24 tickers spanning global indices, ETFs, FX and crypto.
  • GBM/Merton/Heston calibrated by moment-matching initialization then L-BFGS-B minimization of the stylized-facts distance.
  • Eight realism metrics. Learnability is proxied over epochs e >= 100 by GenGap_e = (L_valid(e) - L_train(e)) / L_valid(e) and Stability_e = |L_valid(e) - L_valid(e-1)|, since the learning error itself is unobservable.

Results: realism is multi-dimensional, learnability is joint, and rankings reshuffle

Test-period N225 stylized facts: kurtosis 15.943, Hill index of absolute returns 2.557, skewness -0.733, Hurst exponent of absolute returns 0.642. On realism no generator dominates. On learnability GBM has the lowest GenGap and Stability in every hedger class, but the advantage nearly vanishes for the high-capacity MLPHedger. On performance no generator-hedger pair wins everywhere.

  • LinearHedger: GBM ranks best on European(1e-4) (1.33) and Lookback(1e-4) (1.00) despite being the least realistic generator; on the hardest task, Lookback(1e-3), VAE takes rank 1.00 and GBM drops to 2.00. Heston beats Merton on every LinearHedger task.
  • NTBNet: the GBM/VAE ordering largely reverses - VAE tops European(1e-4) and Lookback(1e-4) at 1.00 each, and Heston's edge over Merton disappears. Attributed to NTBNet's band/threshold policy class making threshold-crossing dynamics decision-relevant.
  • MLPHedger: the preferred generator tracks task difficulty - Merton best on European(1e-4), VAE best on European(1e-3) and Lookback(1e-3), Heston best on Lookback(1e-4), with GBM ranking 4.00 on three of four tasks.
  • Mechanism proposed: as hedger capacity rises the optimization penalty of a complex generator shrinks and the binding constraint shifts from learnability to distributional richness, so richer generators take over on path-dependent, high-cost tasks.

Key findings

  • Theorem 1: deployed excess risk is bounded by the learning error plus 2*Gamma(P,Q_theta;H). A generator does not need global fidelity to P, only preservation of the risk ordering over the admissible strategy class.
  • Theorem 3: if the realism test-function class is a finite-dimensional linear subspace and some admissible strategy's loss lies outside it, one can construct measures with zero realism discrepancy and a strictly positive compatibility gap - perfect realism with a real decision penalty.
  • Realism is multi-dimensional and no generator dominates: Heston wins KS (4.315e-2) and the kurtosis gap (13.876); VAE wins MeanAbsRet (1.120e-4), ACF raw (1.773e-2) and Hurst (0.678e-1); Merton wins the Hill index gap (0.237); GBM is worst on nearly every metric.
  • GBM has the best learnability for every hedger class (LinearHedger GenGap 2.847e-2 vs Merton 7.296e-2; NTBNet 7.782e-2 vs Merton 19.627e-2), but the advantage nearly disappears for MLPHedger (4.295e-2 vs 4.398/4.496/4.579e-2): learnability depends on generator complexity AND hedger capacity jointly.
  • The least realistic generator often wins: GBM takes rank 1.00 on LinearHedger/Lookback(10^-4) and 1.33 on LinearHedger/European(10^-4), and is half of the best pair on two of the four tasks.
  • Rankings reorder across hedger architectures and across task difficulty, with VAE/Heston taking over on path-dependent Lookback options at high transaction costs.

Notable quotes

This highlights that an appropriate generator can outperform a more realistic one when it better balances compatibility and learnability for the given hedger class.
In this sense, a generator may systematically distort the loss landscape perceived by the hedger, despite reproducing stylized facts.
Overall, these results indicate that learnability is jointly determined by the complexity of the generator and the expressive power of the hedger, rather than by the generator alone.
These results indicate that the best generator is not universal, but depends on the interaction between the generator, the hedger architecture, and the task characteristics.

Limitations

  • The paper states its central practical limitation directly: compatibility is difficult to measure in practice and must be assessed through indirect proxies, so the theory cannot yet be turned into a directly optimizable generator objective.
  • Learnability is not measured but approximated by two heuristic training-curve proxies rather than the learning-error term in the Theorem 1 bound.
  • Narrow empirical scope: one underlying (Nikkei 225), 20-day maturity, at-the-money strike, two option types, two transaction-cost levels - and Table 4 reports average ranks rather than raw risk values with confidence intervals, so the magnitude behind the reshuffled rankings is invisible.
  • Theorem 1 is an upper bound, not an equality, and Theorem 3 is an existence result on constructed discrete measures rather than a statement about the generators tested.

Open questions

  • Can compatibility be estimated or bounded from data cheaply enough to serve as a generator-selection criterion, given the paper concedes it is only observable through proxies?
  • The authors propose compatibility-aware deep hedging, jointly designing generator and hedger - what does that objective look like concretely, and does it beat the best fixed pair found by their grid?
  • Does the reshuffling persist across other underlyings, longer maturities, multi-asset portfolios, and richer hedger classes (RL, signature/transformer hedgers)?
