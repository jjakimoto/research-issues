    The Price Impact of Order Book Events

    Date: 2026-09-26

    Theme: Order-flow impact and inventory-aware market making
    This theme links empirical microstructure to stochastic control. Order-flow imbalance provides a compact empirical account of short-horizon price changes, while inventory-aware control converts execution probabilities and risk preferences into optimal bid and ask quotes. Together, the papers define both the signal and decision layers that modern learned market-making systems should outperform.
    Source: arxiv
    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.6402

    Foundational because it introduced order flow imbalance as a parsimonious Level-I measure linking market orders, limit orders, cancellations, liquidity, and short-horizon price changes.

    Executive summary

    The paper models short-horizon price changes as a linear function of order flow imbalance, which aggregates all events changing supply or demand at the best bid and ask. Using April 2010 TAQ data for 50 randomly selected S&P 500 stocks, it finds that OFI explains substantially more price variation than trade imbalance or traded volume and that its impact coefficient is approximately inversely proportional to market depth. A scaling argument explains why a noisy square-root price-volume relation can appear even when OFI is the more direct driver.

    Problem and contribution

    Earlier price-impact studies emphasized trade direction and size while omitting much of the information in quote updates. The authors introduce OFI to summarize changes in displayed supply and demand at the best quotes, treating economically equivalent removals—such as a market sell and cancellation of a buy limit order—alike.

    • Quote updates outnumber trades by roughly 40 to 1 in the sample.
    • The goal is a single-variable model covering market orders, limit orders, and cancellations.
    • Price changes are measured in ticks over short, fixed-time intervals.

    Order-flow model

    Each best-bid or best-ask event receives a signed size contribution: additions to demand or removals of supply are positive, while removals of demand or additions of supply are negative. Contributions are summed into OFI over each interval, and the empirical model is ΔP_k = β_i OFI_k + ε_k. A stylized constant-depth book implies linear impact and motivates an approximately inverse relation between β and average depth.

    • The model uses Level-I quotes rather than the full depth of the book.
    • The noise term represents deeper-book effects, hidden liquidity, and rounding errors.
    • An event's realized impact depends on concurrent order flow within the aggregation window.

    Data and estimation

    The sample contains 21 trading days in April 2010 for 50 randomly selected S&P 500 stocks, using consolidated TAQ trades and quotes from WRDS. The authors reconstruct the NBBO, compute OFI and mid-price changes on a 10-second grid, and estimate ordinary least-squares regressions within 273 half-hour subsamples per stock, typically containing about 180 observations.

    • White heteroskedasticity-consistent errors are used for price-impact regressions.
    • Newey-West errors are used for depth-impact regressions because residuals are autocorrelated.
    • Robustness checks span intervals from 10 quote updates to 10 minutes and alternative trade-direction tests.

    Empirical results

    The linear OFI model achieves a cross-stock average R² of 65%; its slope is significant in 97% of half-hour samples, while the intercept and quadratic term are usually insignificant. Excluding price-changing events from OFI lowers R² but leaves it in the 35%–60% range. The fitted depth exponent averages 0.98, and an exponent of one cannot be rejected for 35 of 50 stocks.

    • Adding the quadratic OFI term raises average R² only from 65% to 68%.
    • The depth regression has a grand-mean R² of 74%.
    • Near the open, depth is about half its average level and price impact about twice its average level; impact is five times higher at the open than at the close.

    Trades, volume, and significance

    OFI explains 65% of 10-second mid-price variation versus 32% for trade imbalance; jointly, trade imbalance adds little and is significant in only 31% of subsamples. The authors derive a noisy square-root relation between OFI and traded volume using a law-of-large-numbers and central-limit-theorem argument, showing how conventional concave volume impact can arise through aggregation rather than direct causation.

    • For absolute price changes, OFI alone has average R² of 58%, versus 23% for powered traded volume.
    • The estimated volume exponent has grand mean 0.18 and varies considerably across stocks and time.
    • The paper explicitly advises against using the noisy volume-scaling model as a substitute for OFI.

    Key findings

    • Short-horizon mid-price changes are approximately linear in contemporaneous OFI across a broad cross-section of stocks.
    • The impact coefficient is approximately inversely proportional to displayed best-quote depth, with a mean fitted exponent of 0.98.
    • OFI explains about twice as much mid-price variation as trade imbalance: 65% versus 32% average R².
    • After controlling for OFI, trade imbalance contributes little incremental explanatory power.
    • A noisy, unstable square-root volume-impact relationship can emerge statistically from aggregation even when OFI is the underlying explanatory variable.

    Notable quotes

    The order flow imbalance is a measure of supply/demand imbalance, which encompasses trades, limit orders and cancelations.
    Our data set consists of one calendar month (April, 2010) of trades and quotes (TAQ) data for 50 stocks.
    This table provides strong evidence of a linear relation between order flow imbalance and price changes.
    Overall, these findings seem to give an intuitive picture of the price impact of order book events, which is somewhat simpler than the one conveyed by previous studies.

    Limitations

    • The data cover only 50 U.S. equities during one month in 2010, limiting temporal, market-regime, and cross-asset generalizability.
    • Level-I TAQ observes only the best quotes; deeper-book structure and hidden orders may matter, especially for the poor depth fits reported for APOL, AZO, and CME.
    • The square-root scaling proposition assumes independent identically distributed event contributions, finite variance, and sufficiently long aggregation windows; dependence or heavy tails can change the exponent.

    Open questions

    • Does the OFI-depth relation remain stable in modern fragmented markets, other asset classes, and stressed-liquidity regimes?
    • How much do full-depth data and estimates of hidden liquidity improve the model for wide-spread, shallow stocks?
    • Can a dynamic model incorporate event autocorrelation, transient impact, and predictive—not merely contemporaneous—price effects while retaining OFI's parsimony?
