    Sharpening Tax in Post-Training

    ← Go backAIM: Agentic Idea Management for Automated ResearchNextWideSWE: Can Coding Agents Coordinate Changes Across… →
    📰 Latest · 2026-10-04
    Read full article →View on GitHub

    Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
    Date: 2026-10-04

    Source: huggingface-daily-papers · 2026-10-01
    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01509

    Across 14 open base/instruct pairs on three tool-use benchmarks, base models wrapped in a simple prompt-and-parse harness solve more distinct tasks given 128 tries than their instruct versions (gemma-4-31B on WebShop: 87.6% vs 56.0% pass@128), even though they score far lower on a single try. Post-training makes models consistent but narrower. If your autonomous researcher has a reliable verifier and a large sampling budget, try base checkpoints, or route between base and instruct using the tax estimated from pilot rollouts. Caveats: the study is observational because the post-training recipes are undisclosed, thinking mode was off, the scaffolding differed between base and instruct models, and the PTGS training fix was tested only on Qwen2.5-7B in two toy grid worlds (5 seeds).

    Executive summary

    The paper tests the RL 'sharpening' hypothesis on multi-turn agentic tasks instead of math or coding. With a light model-agnostic harness, pre-trained base LLMs act as agents. They trail their post-trained counterparts on pass@1 but scale more steeply with repeated sampling and often overtake them on pass@K, sooner for larger models. The mechanism is bimodalization: post-training pushes tasks toward always-solved or never-solved. The authors measure the lost test-time scalability with a 'Sharpening Tax' metric, which is positive in 36 of 42 model-benchmark cases at k=128 and predictable from 8 rollouts. Their posterior-tempered group sampling (PTGS), a per-prompt adaptive rollout temperature, lowers the tax during RL while also raising pass@1.

    Problem: does post-training expand or just sharpen agentic ability?

    Earlier evidence that RL post-training trades coverage (pass@K) for accuracy (pass@1) comes almost entirely from math and coding. There, pre-training exposure is heavy and final-answer checking can credit a lucky but flawed trajectory. Agentic skills (structured tool calls, environment feedback, long horizons) are thought to be learned in post-training, so the authors use them as a stress test. They measure success with three unbiased estimators: pass@1 for accuracy, pass@k for coverage (at least one of k succeeds), and pass^k for consistency (all k succeed).

    • Benchmarks are process-centric, so success depends on the environment state reached and not on matching a final answer string. They are BFCL v4 multi_turn_base (200 tasks, at most 20 tool-call rounds per user turn), WebShop (first 500 goals, 1,000-product index, at most 30 actions, success only when reward = 1.0) and ACEBench (770-task subset, LLM-user-simulator tasks excluded).
    • There are 14 HF base/post-trained pairs from 3B to 35B: Gemma-4 (E4B, 12B, 26B-A4B, 31B), Ministral-3 (3B, 8B, 14B), Qwen2.5 (3B, 7B, 14B, 32B) and Qwen3.5 (4B, 9B, 35B-A3B). That gives 42 model-benchmark cases.
    • Each model draws 128 rollouts per task, served with vLLM at temperature 0.4 (BFCL) or 0.7 (WebShop, ACEBench) and top_p 0.95. Thinking mode is off for post-trained models because the budget counts rollouts, not tokens. A thinking-on ablation leaves the conclusion unchanged.

    The base-model harness

    The harness is one dataset-independent adapter. It renders an OpenAI-style messages and tools conversation into a plain-text completion prompt, then parses the free-text output back into tool calls. It has no per-model presets and no few-shot demos, and it never shows a solved task. Base models are evaluated with the harness. Post-trained models use each benchmark's default chat template and native tool parser, because the harness hurts them.

    • The prompt has five parts in order: the benchmark system prompt; tools written as Python function signatures with docstrings; an instruction to emit a fenced tool_call block holding a JSON array of calls; the conversation under ### User / ### Assistant / ### Tool Output markers; and a trailing ### Assistant header. Base models read Python best but write JSON most reliably, hence the mix.
    • Three stop sequences ('### Tool Output', '### User', and the closing code fence) keep the base model from inventing environment replies. A reply with no tool_call block ends the episode.
    • Harness ablation, averaged over 8 base models at N=32: BFCL pass@1 rises from 5.6 to 15.6 and pass@32 from 15.2 to 49.1. WebShop pass@32 rises from 39.5 to 49.2. ACEBench barely changes. Six of eight base checkpoints score 0 on BFCL without the harness.

    Findings: accuracy-coverage tension and bimodalization

    Post-trained models lead at small k, but harness-equipped base models scale more steeply and cross over in most settings. Larger models cross over sooner: on WebShop the crossover budget falls from k*>128 for gemma-4-E4B to about 3 for gemma-4-31B. Averaged over the largest backbone per family, the base curve overtakes at about k=22. Averaged over the smallest backbones it never does, because there post-training mainly repairs basic skills such as well-formed tool calls.

    • Example on WebShop, gemma-4-31B: base pass@128 is 87.6% vs 56.0% post-trained. Over 128 rollouts, the share of 'pass given compute' tasks falls from 87.6% to 30.0%, 'always pass' rises from 0.0% to 26.0%, and 'always fail' rises from 12.4% to 44.0%.
    • Post-trained models' coverage (pass@K) and consistency (pass^K) curves stay close together. For base models, consistency collapses toward zero while coverage keeps climbing. Gemma-4 sharpens most steeply and Qwen3.5 least.
    • A higher global inference temperature on post-trained models recovers some pass@k but never improves pass@1 in any of the 12 panels. gemma-4-31B-it barely responds to heating.

    The Sharpening Tax metric (in words)

    Raw scalability A(K) adds up the gaps between pass@K and each smaller pass@k: the area under the coverage ceiling, or how much success extra parallel rollouts buy. Calibrated scalability S(K) divides A(K) by (K-1) times the single-shot failure rate, giving a value between 0 and 1. The Sharpening Tax is the base model's scalability minus the post-trained model's, in either raw or calibrated form. A positive value means post-training removed test-time scalability.

    • Proposition 1 interprets A(K) as the expected number of failed attempts before the first success within budget. Tasks solved on the first try, or never solved, contribute zero.
    • Theorem 2: if post-training collapses a fraction lambda of tasks to success probability 0 or 1, the raw tax equals lambda times the base model's A(K). The calibrated tax stays positive whenever some tasks collapse to 0, even if pass@1 improves.
    • In practice: Tax_S(128) > 0 in 36 of 42 cases. It is largest for the biggest checkpoints, such as gemma-4-31B. It is negative mainly for the smallest backbones on BFCL (Ministral-3-3B, Qwen2.5-3B, Qwen2.5-7B), where post-training fixes tool-call formatting.
    • Tax_S(8), estimated from 8 rollouts on half of the tasks, predicts Tax_S(32) on the held-out half with Spearman rho = 0.85. It is the only 8-rollout predictor that tracks all three 32-rollout targets with a strong signal.

    PTGS: posterior-tempered group sampling during RL

    PTGS keeps discounted success and failure counts for each prompt (forgetting factor 0.95). Before each rollout group it draws a Beta-posterior success estimate by Thompson sampling, with a prior of mass 2 centered on a target success rate. It then picks a temperature between 1/tau and tau: hotter for hard prompts, cooler for easy ones. The RL update is unchanged. It was tested in RAGEN by fine-tuning Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with PPO and GRPO on Sokoban (6x6) and slippery FrozenLake, using 200 steps, 8 tasks x 16 rollouts per step and 5 seeds, then evaluating on 64 unseen tasks with 128 rollouts at T=0.5.

    • PTGS raises both pass@1 and pass@128 for PPO and GRPO in both environments and lowers Tax_S(128). Example: GRPO on Sokoban goes from 55.3 to 72.5 pass@128, with the tax falling from 0.081 to 0.025.
    • Under fixed-temperature PPO, entropy collapses steadily toward zero. Under PTGS it stays several times higher and rises repeatedly. On FrozenLake both reach the same validation success but end up with different policies.
    • Fixed rollout temperatures from 0.5 to 1.5, and top-p, top-k and min-p truncation, only move along the trade-off. PTGS is the only sampler that improves accuracy and coverage together.
    • Among tasks that a PPO run loses, a PTGS run solves about half. PTGS also reduces the share of zero-success rollout groups (Theorem 3: more groups contain a success, and more groups mix successes with failures).

    Practical uses: tax-guided routing and inference-time PTGS

    Tax_S(32) estimated from 32 pilot rollouts can decide, per task, whether the base or the post-trained policy gets the remaining budget. An inference-time variant of PTGS runs pilot rollouts on a frozen policy to estimate difficulty, then sets each prompt's temperature.

    • Tax-based routing beats the post-trained model's pass@128 in 5 of 6 panels, by up to 13.6 points (WebShop, largest backbones). Its pass@1 beats the base model's in every panel, and it beats uniform random routing everywhere.
    • Inference-time PTGS on frozen Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (B=256: 128 pilot + 128 scored rollouts) lands beyond the global-temperature frontier on FrozenLake. On Sokoban only two configurations beat every fixed temperature, by just 1-2 of 64 tasks.

    Key findings

    • Harness-equipped base models overtake their post-trained versions in pass@K in most settings. Example: gemma-4-31B on WebShop scores 87.6% vs 56.0% at pass@128, even though the post-trained model wins at pass@1.
    • Larger models cross over sooner. On WebShop the crossover budget falls from k*>128 (gemma-4-E4B) to about 3 (gemma-4-31B). Averaged over the largest backbones the crossover is about k=22, while the smallest backbones never cross over within 128 rollouts.
    • Post-training bimodalizes per-task success. For gemma-4-31B on WebShop, the middle 'pass given compute' share falls from 87.6% to 30.0%, while 'always pass' rises from 0.0% to 26.0% and 'always fail' from 12.4% to 44.0%.
    • The calibrated Sharpening Tax Tax_S(128) is positive in 36 of 42 model-benchmark cases. Tax_S(8) from 8 rollouts predicts held-out Tax_S(32) with Spearman rho = 0.85.
    • PTGS lowers the tax while raising both pass@1 and pass@128. Example: PPO on Sokoban improves from 46.5/55.0 to 61.1/69.7, and GRPO on Sokoban's pass@128 rises from 55.3 to 72.5 (Tax_S 0.081 to 0.025).
    • Routing each task between base and post-trained models using Tax_S(32) from pilot rollouts adds up to 13.6 points of pass@128 over the post-trained model (WebShop, largest backbones), while keeping pass@1 above the base model's.

    Notable quotes

    In short, post-training buys sampling efficiency and consistency by paying with coverage.
    That is, post-training still mainly changes how reliably a model solves tasks rather than which tasks it can solve in the agentic domain.
    Since our goal is not state-of-the-art performance of base models, we use a simple, lightweight universal harness for every base model and every benchmark, without per-model presets, per-family calibration, or few-shot demonstrations.
    A tax estimate from a small pilot thus recovers most of the coverage that post-training gives up, and often more, without falling back to the low single-shot accuracy of the base model.

    Limitations

    • The study is observational: the open checkpoints do not disclose their pre-training or post-training data, so the paper cannot give a causal account. It also uses 'RL post-trained' loosely to cover any undisclosed mix of SFT, DPO and RL.
    • The comparison has confounds: base models get the harness while post-trained models keep the default scaffolding, and thinking mode is off. The benchmarks are basic tool-use suites (one BFCL category, WebShop, and an ACEBench subset without user-simulator tasks), and pass@K assumes a perfect verifier is available to pick out the successful rollout.
    • PTGS was validated only on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct in two small grid-world environments (Sokoban and FrozenLake) with 5 seeds. The tax also requires a binary success signal, so it does not directly apply to open-ended generation.

    Open questions

    • Do RL, SFT and on-policy distillation charge different amounts of Sharpening Tax under controlled data mixes? Does heavier frontier-scale RL ever move beyond elicitation?
    • Does the accuracy-consistency-coverage trade-off, and the benefit of PTGS, carry over to longer-horizon realistic agents (coding, research agents) and to multimodal or VLA models?
    • In real deployments, how much of the base model's coverage edge survives when an imperfect learned verifier or judge has to choose among rollouts?
