    Active Portfolio Management in Concentrated Equity Markets

    Date: 2026-09-27

    Source: arxiv · 2026-09-22
    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27113

    A tractable, reproducible (public GitHub repo) recipe for timing the equal-weight vs cap-weight tilt: model only two SPT state variables (diversity, dispersion), solve a Garleanu-Pedersen-style control with a quadratic cost surrogate calibrated to 15 bps proportional costs, and get an out-of-sample 1995-2024 S&P 500 net IR of 0.35 vs 0.13 for equal weight. Caveats: a single universe and single split, parameters frozen after 1994, alpha only marginal (3-factor t=1.94) and insignificant under 5 factors, and the mean-reverting variant underperforms in the recent AI-driven concentration.

    Executive summary

    The authors (Ceco, Shi, Wong; University of Toronto) formulate active management as choosing a continuous tilt lambda between the cap-weighted market portfolio (lambda=0) and the equal-weighted portfolio (lambda=1), where negative or >1 values mean shorting or levering the equal-minus-market long-short leg. Stochastic portfolio theory reduces relative performance to two macroscopic processes, market diversity and dispersion, which they model jointly with a stochastic diversity-dispersion (SDD) model; the frictional optimum is characterized by a linear FBSDE with an explicit 'aim in front of a moving target' trading rule. Quadratic penalties on tilt size and tilt speed are calibrated in-sample (1977-1994) to match the wealth drag of 15 bps proportional costs, and out-of-sample (1995-2024) backtests on CRSP S&P 500 constituents deduct actual proportional costs. The frictional mean-reverting strategy delivers the highest cumulative net return (44.92 vs 24.33 equal weight and 21.34 market) and a net IR of 0.35, though Sharpe ratios are similar across all portfolios.

    Problem: equal weight wins on average but loses in concentration regimes

    The equal-weighted portfolio has historically beaten the cap-weighted market, but under SPT its log relative value decomposes into the change in market diversity (the log geometric mean of market weights) plus accumulated dispersion (excess growth / volatility harvesting). Dispersion adds a nonnegative drift, but sharp declines in diversity, as in the dot-com bubble and the current AI boom, make equal weight underperform. The paper asks whether diversity/dispersion information can capture diversification benefits while protecting against rising concentration.

    • S&P 500 diversity has recently fallen to levels not seen since the late-1990s dot-com bubble, driven by technology/AI.
    • Prior work (Taljaard and Mare) switches bang-bang between equal weight and market via linear-regression forecasts; this paper produces a continuously varying tilt that also penalizes tilt changes.
    • Claimed first combination of an explicit joint diversity-dispersion model, optimization of a time-varying functionally generated portfolio under quadratic trading penalties, and a stochastic optimal control formulation within SPT.

    Stochastic diversity-dispersion (SDD) model

    Instead of modelling all N stocks, the SDD model specifies only diversity (an Ito process with general adapted drift and volatility) and dispersion (a positive semimartingale). The main tractable case is a mean-reverting model: diversity mean-reverts with stochastic volatility proportional to the square root of dispersion (Hull-White style), and log dispersion is an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, with correlated Brownian drivers. It is Markovian and by construction does not capture regime switching. A discrete-time data-driven 'trending' alternative uses an exponentially smoothed daily diversity trend (21-trading-day half-life) with GARCH(1,1) variance.

    • Calibrated by maximum likelihood on monthly 1977-2004 data: kappa_phi=0.318 (CI 0.089-0.547), long-run diversity -6.934, nu_phi=0.371, kappa_delta=3.179 (CI 1.308-5.049), long-run dispersion 0.011, nu_delta=2.369, rho=-0.20 (CI -0.304 to -0.095).
    • Both mean-reversion speeds are significantly positive; innovations to diversity and log dispersion are negatively correlated.
    • Diversity increments are well fit by a Gaussian and match the model; dispersion is fit poorly: fitted std of log dispersion rises from 0.71 in-sample to 1.46 out-of-sample (GFC 2008-09 and COVID 2020 spikes).
    • The model reproduces the positive nonlinear association between large diversity changes and dispersion level (LOWESS).

    Control problem and the friction surrogate

    Frictionless: maximize expected relative log value minus an active-risk penalty; the optimal tilt is myopic, governed by the instantaneous expected log return of equal weight relative to the market (diversity drift plus the dispersion term) scaled by active variance, plus a quadratic-variation term; it can go negative when concentration pressure dominates or exceed one when diversification benefits are large. Frictional: the tilt is absolutely continuous with a trading rate, and a quadratic surrogate penalizes tilt size (Lambda1, maintenance rebalancing) and tilt speed (Lambda2, extra turnover), both scaled by instantaneous active variance so the normalized problem has constant coefficients. Theorem 3.1 gives existence/uniqueness of a linear FBSDE and a closed-form tilt: the investor trades toward a discounted weighted forecast of future frictionless targets. Theorem 3.4 gives a one-dimensional integral representation of those forecasts under the mean-reverting model.

    • The surrogate is explicitly NOT a trade-by-trade approximation of proportional costs; it is a policy-design device.
    • Two-stage 'effect calibration': Lambda1 matches the in-sample (1977-1994) cumulative active cost of the equal-weighted portfolio; Lambda2 minimizes the terminal in-sample gap between surrogate penalty and realized active cost of the frictional strategy.
    • Mean-reverting calibrated values used in simulation: Lambda1=1.17, Lambda2=0.54, gamma=16.81 (5% annualized active vol target).
    • Discrete implementation: new tilt is a weighted average of the inherited tilt and a forward-looking aim; rolling horizon of 12 months for the mean-reverting model, no forward forecast (current target only) for the trending model.

    Data and backtest design

    Universe: time-varying S&P 500 constituents from CRSP, 1977-2024, processed with Ruf's public CRSP notebooks. Monthly rebalancing; dispersion proxied by monthly realized dispersion from daily returns, scaled by c_delta=0.21 (adjusted R^2 0.95, 1977-1994) to absorb discrete rebalancing and index leakage. In-sample 1977-1994 for all parameter estimation and surrogate calibration; out-of-sample 1995-2024 with parameters frozen (state variables and forecasts update). Proportional costs of 15 bps on all trades via an adaptation of Ruf and Xie allowing shorts; both strategies risk-normalized to 5% in-sample annualized active volatility (gamma_mr=19.91, gamma_trend=30.11).

    • Strategies per model: frictionless target, frictional optimizer, and a clipped [0,1] long-only version (an implementable benchmark, not a constrained optimum).
    • Backtest mean-reverting re-estimate (1977-1994): kappa_phi=0.43, nu_phi=0.30, kappa_delta=5.52, long-run dispersion 0.018, nu_delta=0.99, rho=-0.30; GARCH persistence 0.93.
    • Initial wealth $1,000; extreme daily returns (>100% or <-50%) dropped in in-sample calibration.
    • Simulation check: on 10,000 simulated 48-year paths, the frictionless optimizer wins slightly before penalties, but the frictional optimizer wins clearly after surrogate penalties.

    Results and significance

    Out of sample, all six SDD strategies beat both benchmarks in cumulative net return (26.43 to 44.92 vs 24.33 equal weight and 21.34 market) and in IR (0.21 to 0.35 vs 0.13 equal weight); the frictional mean-reverting strategy is best (Tot. Ret. 44.92, IR 0.35, SR net 0.72). The authors say similar Sharpe ratios across portfolios are expected because the objective is relative to the market. Moving from frictionless to frictional cuts turnover (0.41 to 0.15 mean-reverting; 0.59 to 0.34 trending) while raising IR (0.21 to 0.35; 0.26 to 0.29). Clipping to long-only barely changes IR (0.34 and 0.29). Sensitivity: IR is insensitive to Lambda1; the trending strategy degrades and can turn negative under large Lambda2, while the mean-reverting strategy stays stable. Fama-French: 3-factor annualized alpha 2.47% (t=1.94) mean-reverting and 1.50% (t=1.81) trending, falling to 1.54% (t=1.18) and 1.26% (t=1.47) under 5 factors.

    • The trend strategy mitigates drawdowns in sudden diversity drops (dot-com crash, GFC) and goes negative-tilt around COVID-19.
    • The mean-reverting strategy has recently underperformed as diversity stays persistently below its historical mean; authors cannot say if this is a bubble or a structural break.
    • The mean-reverting frictional strategy runs higher gross exposure (1.46 vs 1.11 trending, about 32% higher), i.e., larger short positions.
    • Code is public (github.com/brianceco/apm-in-cem), making it a concrete replication target.

    Key findings

    • Frictional mean-reverting SDD strategy: cumulative net return 44.92 vs 24.33 (equal weight) and 21.34 (market), net IR 0.35 vs 0.13 for equal weight, net Sharpe 0.72 vs 0.58 / 0.61, after 15 bps proportional costs, 1995-2024.
    • Adding the friction surrogate cuts turnover from 0.41 to 0.15 (mean-reverting) and 0.59 to 0.34 (trending) while raising IR from 0.21 to 0.35 and 0.26 to 0.29; the authors argue the mean-reverting gain is not explained by lower costs alone but by a slower, better-predicting signal.
    • Long-only clipping to [0,1] leaves IR nearly unchanged (0.34 mean-reverting, 0.29 trending) with turnover of 0.05 and 0.15, so outperformance does not hinge on heavy shorting.
    • Max drawdowns are 0.50-0.51 for all SDD strategies vs 0.56 for equal weight and 0.50 for the market; recovery time is 39-52 months vs 44 (equal weight) and 73 (market).
    • Alpha is marginal: 3-factor annualized alpha 2.47% (t=1.94) and 1.50% (t=1.81), dropping to 1.54% (t=1.18) and 1.26% (t=1.47) with five factors; equal weight has negative, insignificant alpha.
    • The mean-reverting model fits diversity increments well but not dispersion: log-dispersion std 0.71 in-sample vs 1.46 out-of-sample, with extreme spikes (GFC, COVID) unreproduced.

    Notable quotes

    Once implementation costs are taken into account, however, tracking this time-varying target closely can require frequent rebalancing, generating substantial turnover and reducing net performance.
    For the mean-reverting model, the significant improvement in relative performance associated with the frictional optimizer cannot be entirely explained by a reduction in transaction costs.
    It is unclear if, as in the lead up to the dot-com crash, this represents a decline in market diversity which is unsustainable, or else a structural break in the mean-reverting dynamics of diversity.
    Excessive smoothing consequently destroys more of the forecasting value of the trending strategy.

    Limitations

    • Single universe (S&P 500) and a single in-sample/out-of-sample split with parameters frozen after 1994; absolute Sharpe ratios are not materially different across portfolios, and factor alpha is only marginally significant (3F) and insignificant (5F).
    • The mean-reverting SDD model is Markovian with no regime switching, fits dispersion poorly (heavy right tail and spikes missed), and the mean-reverting strategy has underperformed in the recent persistent-concentration period.
    • Costs enter the policy only via a quadratic surrogate plus variance normalization, so the policy is not the optimum of the true proportional-cost problem; the theory uses a closed-market master formula and handles index leakage only through a reduced-form dispersion scaling.

    Open questions

    • Is the post-2020 drop in diversity a bubble that will revert (favoring the mean-reverting model) or a structural break (favoring trend-following or regime-switching SDD specifications)?
    • How robust are the gains with rolling re-estimation, other universes (international, Russell), other functionally generated portfolios (e.g., diversity-weighted), and macro or implied-dispersion (DSPX) predictors?
    • Would solving the control problem with proportional costs or price impact directly in the wealth dynamics change the policy or its out-of-sample edge?
