    Packets, Transactions and Queues: Design Principles for HFT Systems from a Measurement Study of CME Market Data

    Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
    Date: 2026-10-02

    Source: arxiv · 2026-09-26
    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32848

    The paper replaces the HFT rule 'always keep the hot path on one thread' with a rule you can measure. The cutoff is the exchange publisher's minimum packet spacing (about 7.5 us on CME NQ, read off the 1st-percentile packet gap, not the mean). A receiver slower than that queues behind bursts that start at the matching engine, and only then do thread hops or a dispatch pool pay off. For quant and agent builders, it is also a 123-page measurement-plus-theory study that the author says was produced with Claude Code under his direction.

    Executive summary

    The paper measures more than a year of CME MDP3 market data for the NQ front-month future (281 RTH sessions, about 3e9 packets and as many matching-engine transactions). It follows each transaction from the matching engine (transactTime), through the market-data publisher (sendingTime), to a receiver. Packet arrivals form near-critical self-exciting clusters (Hawkes branching ratio about 0.8). The clustering comes from the matching engine's transactions, not from how CME packs them into packets. The publisher behaves as a queue that sends at most one packet per publisher period of about 7.5 us. A tandem-Lindley simulator on the real timestamps, closed-form results (a burst-limit throughput identity and an exact reduction of the tandem to its slowest stage), and a live Kaspar-HFT production receiver all point to the same design threshold. Below the publisher period, keep one thread. Above it, cut the chain so the slowest stage is shortest, or send whole packets to a pool of cores if the stage is stateless. At a production decode floor of about 7 us, the remaining tail comes from multi-message packets and from service time that varies packet to packet.

    Problem and dataset

    The design question is whether the ingress-to-order servicing chain (SBE decode, book update, strategy, order construction, total service time T) should run as a single-threaded event loop or as a tandem of N actors on separate threads, where each hop costs h. The usual HFT argument (Thompson's Single Writer Principle, Rigtorp, Chronicle, Aeron) treats each hop as pure cost. The paper tests this against measured arrivals instead of an assumed model.

    • Corpus: 281 sessions of CME MDP3 NQ front-month pcap-derived MBO tapes, 2025-01 to 2026-02, RTH only. This gives 3512 non-overlapping 30-minute windows from 276 sessions.
    • Each record carries packet_seq, transactTime (nanosecond matching-engine event time) and sendingTime (nanosecond publisher per-packet header time). Arrivals are keyed on sendingTime, which shows no ties and no sequence inversions.
    • transactTime is rejected as the arrival clock. The 1.7% of transactions split across packets share one transactTime but leave about 15 us apart.
    • The hop cost h = 1.7 us is half the measured round trip of the Kaspar-HFT BQueue mailbox (mutex + condition_variable). The paper calls this an upper bound because inside a burst the receiver never sleeps.
    • Service times swept: T in {2,4,8,16,32,64,128} us and N in {1,2,4,8} stages, on every window. A refined sweep covers 5 to 16 us.

    What the feed looks like: self-exciting clusters carried by transactions

    Model-free diagnostics reject Poisson on every window. A shuffle of the gaps separates two ingredients. The ordering of gaps into runs is the self-exciting, long-range-dependent part. The heavy-tailed gap lengths themselves leave a flat over-dispersion of about 17. Grouping every message by transactTime shows that packets simply relay the clustering of matching-engine transactions.

    • 73% of packet gaps are shorter than a tenth of the mean gap (Poisson: 9.5%). The Fano factor grows from 52 at 0.1 s to 1027 at 60 s, and the Hurst exponent is 0.74.
    • Exponential-kernel Hawkes MLE gives branching ratio n of about 0.798 and kernel decay of about 167 us. Only about 4% of triggering falls within the 7.5 us publisher period and about 9% within 16 us.
    • 95.9% of packets carry one message and 98.3% of transactions fit in one packet. About 3% of packets carry parts of two transactions.
    • Transaction starts and packets have the same Fano factors (1 s: 105.4 vs 108.4) and the same branching ratio (0.795 vs 0.798).
    • Tight gaps fall between different transactions. Below 17.8 us, 20.6% of packet gaps are that short against 1.24% for Poisson, and only 5.0% of these lie inside a single transaction.

    Exchange side: the publisher is a queue with a ~7.5 us period

    Comparing the two exchange clocks for the same transactions shows the matching engine often processes consecutive transactions within a fraction of a microsecond. The publisher sends at most one packet per period s_pub, so an engine burst reaches the receiver as a train of mostly single-message packets one period apart. The receiver is then the second server in a tandem that starts at the exchange. Its tail threshold equals the publisher's service time.

    • Engine clock: 17.1% of consecutive transactions are under 7.5 us apart, with a 1st-percentile gap of 0.18 us. Publisher clock: 1.04% are under 7.5 us, with a 1st-percentile gap of 7.46 us (range 7.23-7.88 us across 40 sessions).
    • When two transactions are processed under 1 us apart, they share a packet only 15.7% of the time. Otherwise they arrive as separate packets, most often 7.5-10 us apart (43%).
    • Publisher delay (sendingTime - transactTime) has a median of about 100 us. It grows from 86 us to 912 us (p99 from 2.9 ms to 15.0 ms) as the engine's preceding 50 us load rises from 0 to 8-15 transactions. That is the signature of a queue.
    • The publisher drains a backlog with more packets, not bigger ones. Even when it is more than 5 ms behind, packets sent at the period carry 1.1 NQ messages on average and 98% hold a single transaction.
    • Across tight engine gaps, the traffic is ordinary quote traffic (deletes 41.2%, new orders 46.0%). After a trade, the next transaction is another trade 11.7% of the time across a tight gap vs 2.2% across a loose one, which looks like a race signature.
    • Conjecture: publisher capacity acts as an information-side speed limit on feedback cascades. It bounds their speed, not their branching ratio.

    Simulation, theory and the design equation

    An N-stage tandem Lindley recursion is driven by the real sendingTime arrivals and by nine counterfactual streams. Proposition 1 says each stage adds exactly one hop to the median. Theorem 1 reduces a deterministic-service tandem exactly to one server with the largest stage's service, with per-message wait at most W(1)/N + (N-1)h on any arrival path. Corollary 2 says a rate-matched Poisson stream has zero p99 wait whenever utilisation is below 1%, which covers every T up to 16 us at this feed's rate.

    • Real stream: the single-thread p99 equals T up to 4 us, then reaches 34.59 us at T=16, 146.14 at 32, 820.70 at 64 and 5316.85 at 128 (about 41x T). The Poisson null has no tail up to 16 us.
    • Tail onset falls between 7 and 8 us. The share of windows with any tail jumps from 4.5% to 98.6%. Splitting starts to pay about 1.5 us later (67% of windows at 9 us, 99.7% at 10 us, all windows from 12 us).
    • The tail excess depends only on T/N, as the reduction predicts. Diagonals of Table 11 repeat exactly. Once every stage is below s_pub the excess is zero, so the fitted decay exponent is about 3, far steeper than the 1/N bound.
    • Nulls: uniform and 1 s-binned Poisson remove almost all of the tail, so it is not busy-second load. The gap shuffle keeps about 80% at 16 us but only about a quarter at 128 us. Redrawing transaction times (TP) removes it, while swapping sizes (TS), merging into one packet (TM) or spreading packets (TW) leave about 100%.
    • Packet rate does not matter at HFT service times. A 4.66x range of rate moves the normalised p99/T by less than 10% up to 32 us, because the p1 gap stays at 7.43-7.53 us. 'A busy market has more bursts, not tighter ones.' Results hold on 5-minute windows (n=21,060).
    • Design equation: N* = argmin[(N-1)h + kappa*Delta(N)]. At kappa=1, one stage is optimal up to 8 us and eight stages at 64 us and above. A 0.5 us busy-poll hop changes N* only at 8 us.
    • Dispatch pool (M/D/N, stateless stages only) with ingress hop, egress hop and resequencing charged: the median is T+2h at any N. It is level with a two-stage cut at N=2 and better from N=4, and resequencing holds at most 0.14% of packets. Sharding by instrument does not help, because a burst is one instrument's flow.

    Validation against the live Kaspar-HFT receiver

    A live capture on 2026-09-16 (seven months after the corpus ends) covers 6.81 M messages on six streams (ES/NQ/ZN, book and trade) over 53 minutes. Arrivals are keyed on the receiver's socket-read timestamp. The clustering statistics match the corpus. The production decode floor (socket to book) sits just under the publisher period, so the receiver runs at the edge of the tail.

    • Corpus vs live NQ book: share of gaps under mean/10 is 73.2% vs 63.1%. The Fano(5 s) collapse under shuffle is 14.8x vs 12.2x. Hurst is 0.740 vs 0.702. The corpus branching ratio of 0.798 lies inside the live Fano-ceiling bounds of 0.850-0.972.
    • Decode floor by three estimators: ES 7.01/6.98/7.1 us, NQ 7.24/7.23/7.5 us, ZN 6.89/6.83/7.0 us. During an FOMC release the packet rate rose 4.6-9.8x and the floor moved by at most 0.27 us.
    • Each extra message costs 0.31-0.97 us. One-message packets are served below s_pub on all six streams, and two-message packets at or above it (range 7.52-7.80 us).
    • Span-aware rerun at T=8 us: in-packet decode supplies about 66% of the p99 excess and queueing the rest. From 16 us up, message count adds 3% or less and N* is unchanged.
    • ZN book serial handler: p50 7.11 us, p99 63.30 us, p99.9 273.69 us. Empty-queue first-in-packet messages reach only 26.37 us at p99.9, but still about twice the decode floor at p99, from unexplained service variability.
    • Across streams the tail tracks span times cost per message, not packet rate. NQ book is the busiest stream and has the smallest p99.9 (18.94 us).

    Design principles for HFT practitioners

    Part III turns the measurements into a procedure, worked through on Kaspar-HFT's fast_send pipeline. Hot-path micro-optimisation stays necessary. The paper argues against the single-thread chain as an unconditional default and against always-on pre-spinning or pre-warming. Receiver transparency (the same MESSAGE_HANDLER whether invoked by fast_send or send) turns the thread-boundary choice into a one-line, end-of-build decision.

    • Measure the feed's publisher period once, as the 1st-percentile packet gap. Compare it to the upper quantiles of each stage's per-packet service under load, not the median. The mean gap (about 2 ms) is about 300x too large and would say never split.
    • Upper quantiles below s_pub: keep one thread. Median below but tail above (the production regime): cut per-message cost and service spread instead of adding threads.
    • Typical service above s_pub: split stateful stages (the order book) serially at the boundary that minimises the largest stage, with the largest stage at ingress. Merge downstream sub-tasks up to s_max.
    • For stateless stages (SBE decode) with four or more cores, use a dispatch pool with resequencing instead.
    • Worked 16 us chain: a single thread gives 18.59 us of p99 excess. A balanced 8+8 cut gives 0.61 + 1.7 = 2.31 us. Three stages give 4.01 us and four give 5.10 us, so one balanced cut wins by about 8x.
    • The existing ~7 us socket-to-book chain should not be split (N*=1).
    • Busy-poll and pre-warming mainly help the median and the minimum. Every p99 message arrives mid-cluster, when the receiver is already awake and warm (a model prediction, not measured).

    Key findings

    • The receiver's tail threshold is the publisher period, about 7.5 us on NQ (p1 packet gap 7.43-7.53 us in every packet-rate quintile). Tail onset is between 7 and 8 us (windows with a tail jump from 4.5% to 98.6%), and splitting pays from about 9-10 us with h = 1.7 us.
    • Above the threshold the single-thread p99 explodes: 34.59 us at T=16, 146.14 at 32, 5316.85 at 128 (about 41x T). One cut removes most of it (T=32: p99 drops to 52.29 us for a 1.7 us median cost), while a rate-matched Poisson stream has no tail up to 16 us.
    • The tail comes from when matching-engine transactions happen, not from packetisation. Redrawing transaction times leaves only 2-25% of the tail. Merging each transaction into one packet keeps 92-101%. 98.3% of transactions fit in one packet, and the Hawkes branching ratio is 0.795 on transactions vs 0.798 on packets.
    • The engine processes 17.1% of consecutive transactions within 7.5 us (p1 gap 0.18 us), against 1.04% on the publisher clock. Publisher delay grows about tenfold under engine bursts (median 86 us to 912 us), and the publisher drains backlogs with more single-message packets, not bigger ones.
    • Packet rate is irrelevant at HFT service times. A 4.66x range of rate moves p99/T by under 10% up to 32 us, and only from 64 us do rate and branching ratio matter (up to about 1.75-1.91x at 128 us).
    • The live production receiver decodes at about 7 us, just under s_pub. There, multi-message packets (0.31-0.97 us per extra message) and service variability carry the tail. In-packet decode is about two-thirds of the simulated p99 excess at T=8 us, and ZN book reaches 273.69 us at p99.9 vs 26.37 us for empty-queue messages.

    Notable quotes

    Below the publisher period the single-thread argument is simply right, and splitting the chain adds hop cost for nothing.
    The output of a deterministic server cannot contain two departures closer together than its service time, and while the server works through a backlog its departures are spaced by exactly that time.
    Raising the intensity of a self-exciting process raises the rate at which clusters are generated; it does not change the spacing of events inside a cluster.
    The claim that a faster inner loop implies a single thread is preferable conflates two claims: that a faster inner loop lowers latency, which the paper does not dispute, and that a single thread is therefore the best topology, which the paper contradicts above the publisher period.

    Limitations

    • Only one instrument (NQ front-month) was measured, and packet statistics count only NQ messages, not the full channel 318. The ~7.5 us publisher period is assumed, not measured, for ES and ZN. The live validation is one host, one 53-minute afternoon, with no replication.
    • The simulator uses constant (or span-proportional, at NQ's per-message cost) service time and does not model the random service variability the live receiver shows (about 2x the decode floor at p99 with no queue). Simulated tails at the production operating point therefore understate real ones.
    • Packet captures are not redistributable, so only the code (Kaspar-HFT repo) is public. The hop cost h = 1.7 us is a low-load wake-path figure taken from the author's earlier paper, and the paper argues it overstates the in-burst cost.

    Open questions

    • Why do orders reach the matching engine within a fraction of a microsecond of each other: reaction, common reaction to one public signal (latency-arbitrage races), or pacing inside order entry? Telling these apart needs order-entry timestamps the public MDP3 feed does not carry.
    • Does the threshold and the two-channel tail picture replicate on ZN, ES and BTC and on the full channel? This includes cross-excitation along the Treasury curve as a cause of large ZN packets, which a multivariate Hawkes fit could test.
    • Where does per-packet service variability come from, and can parallel decoding of multi-message packets, or a dispatch-decode plus single order-book design, cut the far tail in a production build?
