DeltaSelect: Affordable A/B Testing for Coding Agents

📰 Latest · 2026-09-18 Full Article
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-09-18

Source: arxiv · 2026-09-17
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19607

Most coding-agent benchmark tasks are too noisy to trust from a single run, so DeltaSelect resamples published trials to pick the few that reliably track full-benchmark score, calibrates fractional verifier output onto a common scale, and fits the set into a dollar budget — making baseline-vs-candidate A/B tests cheap enough ($113.08 instead of $3,063.63, 27.1x) to run after every harness change.

Executive summary

DeltaSelect turns an existing benchmark's repeated public trials into a small, frozen, budget-constrained task set for repeated baseline-versus-candidate comparisons during coding-agent development. Using a pinned DeepSWE v1.1 snapshot (22,586 source trials, 22,417 usable, 18 models, 50 model-effort configurations, 113 tasks), it ranks tasks by the 5th percentile of 10,000 seeded one-trial resampled Pearson correlations with the full-benchmark score, maps each task's fail-to-pass (F2P) fraction onto the published full-score scale with per-task OLS, weights tasks by normalized inverse residual variance, and greedily scans the fixed ranking under a dollar budget. Only 19.5% of tasks reach a 5th-percentile correlation of 0.50. A gpt-5.6-luna low-reasoning case study in the Codex harness ran 13 evaluations over an eight-task frozen set for $27.86 total, moving from 36.46% at $4.18 to 42.36% at $1.75.

The problem: one-run noise, cost, and the harness gap

Full benchmarks are too expensive to rerun after every skill or instruction edit. Binary pass/fail discards partial progress, and DeepSWE's four-runs-per-task design quadruples scored-run cost without recovering that information. Separately, the harness that produced a published score is usually not the harness a developer ships, and swapping harnesses alone moves scores by points.

  • Across 10,000 resamples the median task's 5th-percentile correlation with the full DeepSWE score was only 0.321; just 22 of 113 tasks reached 0.50 and only two exceeded 0.70.
  • On Terminal-Bench 2.1, changing only the harness moved GPT-5.5 xhigh by 5.1 points and Opus 4.7 max by 2.8 points.
  • Frontier saturation destroys headroom: a baseline near 100% leaves no room to show improvement, near 0% no room to detect regression.

Why F2P instead of binary or P2P

DeepSWE's released verifier records carry the fraction of new fail-to-pass tests passed (F2P) and the fraction of existing pass-to-pass tests preserved (P2P) alongside binary resolution. The choice of F2P as ranking signal was made empirically under the identical resampling procedure.

  • Under the same 10,000-resample procedure: F2P yields 22 tasks at 5th-percentile correlation >= 0.50; all verifier tests combined yield 15; binary pass/fail yields 11; P2P alone yields zero.
  • The 0.50 threshold is a descriptive reporting cut, not a selection rule — allocation uses the complete ranking.
  • F2P measures verifier tests, not percentage of requirements completed; tests can overlap or encode several assertions.

The statistical method: conservative ranking, calibration, weighting

For each task and each of 10,000 deterministic resamples, one of the four complete trials is drawn per available configuration and the resulting one-run vector is correlated with the fixed full-score vector; the ranking statistic is the 5th percentile of that distribution. Each task's pseudorandom stream is seeded from SHA-256 of the pinned snapshot checksum, a null byte, and the task identifier. A per-task OLS fit maps the four-run mean F2P onto the published full-score scale; weights are normalized inverse residual variance.

  • Tasks with undefined or nonpositive residual variance are excluded to avoid infinite weights; the weights are not an optimal inverse-variance meta-analysis.
  • Calibrated score is not capped at 0-100%; published Luna low calibrated to -0.54%.
  • Incomplete configuration-task cells are excluded rather than imputed; 169 of 22,586 trials were excluded.

Budget allocation and uncertainty reporting

A greedy scan runs once down the fixed reliability order, admitting every task that fits the remaining per-arm budget under configuration-specific observed mean prices. Price and budget never reorder reliability. Repeatability ranges come from 1,000 resamples reported as 5th-95th percentile; when both arms have repeated trials, the weighted calibrated difference and propagated standard error are compared with a two-sided Welch-Satterthwaite test.

  • For the $0.65 published Luna low budget, ranks 1-7 fit, ranks 8-24 do not fit the remaining $0.056, and rank 25 is the next affordable task.
  • The scan is deterministic and inspectable but not optimal: it can skip a better joint combination, pick redundant tasks, or underspend.
  • Tasks, repetitions, calibration coefficients, weights, price assumptions, snapshot checksum and seeds are frozen into one experiment specification used for both baseline and candidate.

Evaluation setup and cost savings

Two experiments run in the Codex harness used by Agent Layer: a harness validation of gpt-5.6-luna on the eight budget-selected tasks at low/medium/high reasoning versus published mini-swe-agent, and a case study reusing those eight tasks across 13 evaluations of ten configurations, one run per task. Separately, a published-data projection estimates the cost of detecting a known 20.3-point gap between Claude Opus 5 medium and Gemini 3.6 Flash high.

  • Nine-task ranked prefix reaches projected p = 0.0473 at $113.08 — 27.1x less than the $3,063.63 recorded cost of the 898-trial full protocol; all 109 complete paired tasks cost $740.58 at p = 0.00378.
  • Total recorded case-study model cost across 13 evaluations: $27.86.
  • Because each Codex endpoint has one run per task, score uncertainty is transferred from published Luna medium in mini-swe-agent.

What the frozen set revealed during iteration

Published numbers did not carry into Codex, and headroom (read from raw F2P, not the unbounded calibrated score) drove the choice of low reasoning after a Luna high pilot left five of six tasks at 96.7-100% F2P. Successive evaluations on the fixed set localized the cost driver to orchestration control flow rather than prompt length, and exposed defects in Agent Dispatch's wait/resume behavior and reviewer scope expansion.

  • Codex vs published calibrated score: low 20.43% vs -0.54%, medium 45.50% vs 19.38%, high 56.33% vs 53.34%.
  • Codex costs for the eight tasks were $0.32 / $1.34 / $3.00 vs DeepSWE estimates of $0.13 / $0.38 / $2.04 — 1.5-3.5x the published estimate.
  • Evaluation 8's resume-until-complete change took the Scriggo task from zero to 46 of 48 checks but raised cost to $2.40; evaluation 13 scored 42.36% at $1.75 vs 39.75% at $1.82 in evaluation 12.
  • Three repeated (unchanged) configurations moved by 0.7-4.3 calibrated points; the primary Codex Luna low run scored 20.43% and an unchanged repeat scored 14.84%.

Key findings

  • Only 22 of 113 DeepSWE tasks (19.5%) reach a 5th-percentile Pearson correlation of 0.50 with full-benchmark score across 10,000 one-trial resamples; the median task reaches only 0.321 and just two exceed 0.70.
  • Signal choice matters more than it looks: under the identical resampling procedure F2P produces 22 qualifying tasks, all verifier tests combined 15, binary pass/fail 11, and P2P alone zero.
  • A nine-task ranked prefix is projected to separate Claude Opus 5 medium from Gemini 3.6 Flash high at $113.08 and p = 0.0473 — 27.1x cheaper than the $3,063.63, 898-trial published protocol.
  • Published numbers did not transfer to the target harness: calibrated score gaps of 21.0 and 26.1 points at low and medium reasoning, and costs 1.5-3.5x the published estimate.
  • Thirteen evaluations on one frozen eight-task set cost $27.86 total and moved the configuration from 36.46% at $4.18 to 42.36% at $1.75, with raw F2P 251 to 265 of 411 checks.
  • The cost win was statistically solid (two-sided exact paired sign-flip p = 0.008) while the score gain was not (SE 0.05902, 29.23 Welch-Satterthwaite df, two-sided p = 0.326).

Notable quotes

Across 10,000 deterministic one-trial resamples, the median task’s fifth-percentile Pearson correlation with the full DeepSWE configuration score was 0.321.
Under the same 10,000-resample procedure, F2P produced 22 tasks with a fifth-percentile correlation of at least 0.50. All verifier tests combined produced 15, binary pass/fail produced 11, and P2P alone produced zero.
The records do not isolate which harness behavior produced the gap. They do show that the same model, reasoning level, and tasks cost 1.5–3.5 times the published estimate.
Requiring statistical significance for every edit would make development impractical.

Limitations

  • Evidence is in-sample and single-source: task ranking, calibration, and the published-data example all use the same DeepSWE v1.1 snapshot, one model family, and one custom harness.
  • The selector is never benchmarked against random, cheapest-first, or median-correlation selection at matched cost, and the contributions of fractional scoring, linear calibration, and inverse-error weighting are not isolated.
  • Each Codex endpoint has only one run per task, so uncertainty is transferred from a different harness; several evaluations changed more than one instruction, so the 58.1% cost cut cannot be attributed to a single edit.

Open questions

  • Do nonlinear calibration, equal-weight sensitivity analysis, or covariance-aware weighting beat the current OLS + inverse-residual-variance scheme?
  • Which component — fewer tasks, F2P instead of pass/fail, or one run instead of four — actually produces the 27.1x saving?
  • How well does planning variance one reasoning level or model tier above the execution setting hold across other harnesses and model families?
