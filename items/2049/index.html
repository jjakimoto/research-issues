    Event History Over Scale: Compact Transformers for Low-Latency Limit Order Book Forecasting

    ← Go backCatching Developers in the Flow: Low-Latency Agentic Program…NextFactorBench: A Portfolio-Aware Benchmark for Automated… →
    📰 Latest · 2026-10-07 Full Article
    Read full article →View on GitHub

    Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
    Date: 2026-10-07

    Source: arxiv · 2026-10-02
    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02917

    A 7.2k-parameter causal transformer that reads the last 128 raw market-by-order (L3) messages matches or beats L2-snapshot models up to 3.14M parameters on mid-price direction (best mean macro-F1 in 11 of 12 market-horizon cells) while running in about 0.48 ms on one CPU thread. The catch: equity gains are only 0.002-0.010 macro-F1, label thresholds use the whole test split, and there is no PnL evaluation, so the case for richer input over bigger models is strongest on thin markets like EPEX intraday power.

    Executive summary

    The authors (ETH Zurich) ask whether a tiny, directly supervised model fed the raw L3 order-event stream can replace much larger models built on sampled L2 snapshots for short-horizon mid-price direction forecasting. MBOFormer (7,203 params; two causal transformer blocks, d=16, 4 heads, 128-event window, 7 features per event) and MBOFusion (14,371 params; adds a causal-conv branch over 16 steps of 12 slow context features) are compared against DeepLOB, BiNCTABL, MLPLOB and three sizes of TLOB on EPEX intraday electricity, NASDAQ AAPL and NYSE BAC. One of the L3 models has the highest mean test macro-F1 in 11 of 12 settings with sub-millisecond batch-one CPU latency and state dicts under 70 kB. The margin is large on thin EPEX (+0.038 at h=10) but marginal on the liquid equities (+0.009 AAPL, +0.002 BAC), and the authors do not claim trading profitability.

    Problem: L2 snapshots throw away event history

    Most deep LOB models consume sampled L2 states (price and volume at 10 bid and 10 ask levels), sometimes with the triggering message and engineered features. Replaying L3 reconstructs L2, but the reverse is lossy: snapshots lose the identity, timing, type and ordering of the messages in between. Large L3 models exist (LOBERT about 1.1M params pretrained on 470M messages; MarketGPT about 100M; TradeFM 524M), but nobody had tested whether a compact supervised model could exploit L3 granularity under tight latency and memory budgets.

    • Online, batch-one inference at each decision point is the deployment target, so latency and memory are reported next to accuracy.
    • First deep-learning treatment of EPEX order-message histories for short-horizon price forecasting.
    • LOBERT was left out because its implementation and weights are not available.

    Task, labels and leakage control

    Decision points sit on an event-time grid: one sample after every 10th book-changing message. The target is the 3-class direction (up/flat/down) of the mid-price at h in {10, 20, 50, 100} sampled steps, using TLOB's two-window smoothing: a 10-step trailing mean versus the 10-step mean ending h steps ahead. The threshold alpha_h is half the mean absolute smoothed change over all valid positions in each labeled sequence. Signed differences are used so that zero or negative power prices work. Each (model, market, horizon) is trained separately with AdamW, early stopping on validation loss, and 3 seeds.

    • Chronological whole-day splits: EPEX 8/2/3 days, AAPL 5/1/1, BAC 35/9/14, so every test day comes after every training day.
    • No label window crosses a split boundary. For EPEX, each delivery contract goes to exactly one split and is labeled as its own sequence.
    • Acknowledged look-ahead: alpha_h is computed over the full test sequence. This affects the label definition, not the inputs, so absolute scores are not achievable online.
    • Horizon h=10 is roughly 0.5 s on AAPL, 5 s on BAC and 2 min on EPEX.
    • The flat class is about 70% of training windows at h=10 on EPEX, 44% on BAC and 38% on AAPL. At h=100 it falls to about 51% on EPEX and 29-33% on the equities.

    Models and inputs: L3 versus L2

    MBOFormer tokenizes each of the last T=128 L3 events using 7 replay-derived features: event type (submit/cancel/execute), signed side, log tick-depth from best quote, log size, log elapsed time, immediate mid-price change, and a best-quote flag. Event type is embedded and the other fields are linearly projected. A learned positional embedding feeds two causal transformer blocks (d=16, 4 heads), followed by a final LayerNorm. The readout concatenates the newest-event vector with the mask-aware mean, and a 2-layer MLP head produces 3 classes. The model carries no state between decisions, so memory is fixed by T. MBOFusion keeps that encoder and adds a 12-dim context vector: drift, volatility, spread, depth, event and execution rates, submit/cancel balance, and time-to-gate-closure where relevant. A causal conv runs over the last 16 sampled steps with last/mean/max readout before fusion.

    • Baselines are L2-based: DeepLOB (100-snapshot window, 40 values), BiNCTABL (10 snapshots), MLPLOB (384 snapshots), and TLOB at 1.20M (128 rows), 23.9k and 7.2k (16 rows).
    • MLPLOB and TLOB also get 14 extra fields per snapshot: 6 for the triggering message plus 8 OFI/VWAP features. No baseline sees a history of individual L3 events.
    • The TLOB adaptation adds dropout, RMSNorm and a more efficient attention implementation, with configurable width, depth and window. The authors report it performs comparably to the released code.
    • Each event-model run trains in at most about 20 min per seed on one RTX 4080.

    Results: accuracy

    At h=10, MBOFormer (7.2k) scores 0.651 / 0.665 / 0.735 (EPEX / AAPL / BAC). That beats both TLOB 1.20M (0.617 / 0.653 / 0.731) and MLPLOB 3.14M (0.612 / 0.657 / 0.732) on every market. Growing TLOB from 23.9k to 1.20M gives no consistent gain. The capacity-matched MBOFormer beats TLOB 7.2k in 11 of 12 settings, even though TLOB gets OFI/VWAP features. The single loss is BAC h=20, where MLPLOB's 0.592 beats the best L3 model's 0.589. Scores fall with horizon for every model. The context branch helps on all AAPL horizons, short EPEX horizons and long BAC horizons, so the event sequence carries most of the gain.

    • Gap between best L3 and best L2 at h=10: EPEX +0.038, AAPL +0.009, BAC +0.002 macro-F1.
    • An always-flat predictor scores about 0.26 (EPEX), 0.19 (AAPL) and 0.22 (BAC) macro-F1 at h=10.
    • Seed standard deviations are mostly 0.000-0.003, but there are only 3 seeds and 1-14 test days, so test-period uncertainty is not quantified.
    • BAC (one-tick spread in about 91% of snapshots) shows the smallest gain. AAPL (about 33% one-tick) is in between. The authors decline to name a causal mechanism.

    Results: deployment cost

    Model-forward latency was measured at batch size one on a single Apple M2 CPU thread in FP32 with synthetic inputs: 100 warm-up calls, then 2,500 timed forwards. Preprocessing, order-book reconstruction, data transfer and order execution are excluded. MBOFormer runs at p50 0.479 ms and p99 0.791 ms with a 39 kB state dict. Compared with TLOB 1.20M and MLPLOB 3.14M, its median latency is 5.1x and 8.1x lower and it uses 113x and 260x fewer FLOPs. TLOB 7.2k and BiNCTABL are faster (p50 0.301 and 0.187 ms) because their temporal inputs are shorter, but they predict worse. On the M2 GPU at batch one, MBOFormer is slower (2.5 ms) because launch and sync overhead dominates.

    • MBOFormer needs about 8x the FLOPs of the parameter-matched TLOB 7.2k (1.091 vs 0.139 MFLOPs), because attention runs over 128 tokens instead of 16 rows.
    • Activation memory is 57-60 kB for the L3 models versus 334-834 kB for the large baselines. This excludes the state needed for book replay.
    • Code is released on GitHub (lassebaerlandstrand/event-history-over-scale). The EPEX data is proprietary. Equity data came from Databento.

    Significance for practitioners

    The practical message is that representation beats scale in this regime. If you already maintain an MBO feed handler, a roughly 40 kB model reading raw messages is a credible sub-millisecond baseline that tracks or beats million-parameter L2 models. On liquid US equities, though, the gains (0.002-0.010 macro-F1) are within what label-threshold choice or test-period variation could move, and the authors say explicitly that the scores do not establish trading profitability. The clearest signal is on thin, slow markets (EPEX intraday power, about 60-tick median spread). Even there, the data is 13 days from March 2021 with 3 test days.

    • The paper makes no PnL, cost-adjusted or execution-simulation claims. Economic value is left to future work.
    • The latency comparison leaves out the extra cost of L3 replay and per-event feature construction in a live pipeline.

    Key findings

    • One of the two L3 models has the highest mean macro-F1 in 11 of 12 market-horizon settings. The only loss is NYSE BAC h=20 (0.589 vs MLPLOB's 0.592).
    • At h=10, MBOFormer (7,203 params) scores 0.651 / 0.665 / 0.735 on EPEX / AAPL / BAC, beating TLOB 1.20M (0.617 / 0.653 / 0.731) and MLPLOB 3.14M (0.612 / 0.657 / 0.732) on every market.
    • The L3 advantage depends on the market: +0.038 macro-F1 on thin EPEX intraday power but only +0.009 on AAPL and +0.002 on BAC at h=10.
    • MBOFormer runs at p50 0.479 ms and p99 0.791 ms with a 39 kB state dict. That is 5.1x and 8.1x faster than TLOB 1.20M and MLPLOB, with 113x and 260x fewer FLOPs. TLOB 7.2k and BiNCTABL are faster still but less accurate.
    • Scaling TLOB from 23.9k to 1.20M parameters gives no consistent macro-F1 gain, which suggests input representation matters more than capacity here.
    • An always-flat predictor reaches about 0.26 / 0.19 / 0.22 macro-F1 at h=10, so all models are well above trivial, but gaps between models on the equities are small.

    Notable quotes

    Every test day therefore postdates every training day, so the reported scores measure out-of-time generalization to later market conditions rather than interpolation within a period.
    The L3 event sequence therefore accounts for the principal gain, with the context branch providing a market- and horizon-dependent refinement.
    MBOFormer therefore offers a favorable quality-efficiency trade-off rather than the lowest latency in isolation and both of our models remain below one millisecond at p99.
    These scores do not establish trading profitability, but they show performance well above the constant baseline and demonstrate that the same compact model family can be applied to both electricity and equity markets.

    Limitations

    • The evaluation is small and somewhat optimistic. There are only three markets, AAPL has 1 test day, EPEX has 3 test days (March 2021), and only 3 seeds are run with no significance testing across test periods. The label threshold alpha_h is computed over each full test sequence, which is a look-ahead in the labels, so absolute macro-F1 is not achievable online.
    • Latency is measured for the model forward pass only, on a laptop CPU with synthetic inputs. It excludes L3 book replay and per-event feature construction, and there is no co-located or production hardware benchmark.
    • There is no economic evaluation: no PnL, transaction costs or execution simulation. Macro-F1 gains of 0.002-0.010 on the liquid equities may not be economically meaningful.

    Open questions

    • Does the L3 advantage over L2 survive online thresholds fixed from past data, longer and more varied test periods, and cost-aware trading or execution backtests?
    • Which market property drives the larger gain on EPEX: wide spreads, slow event rates, thin liquidity, or the longer wall-clock horizon? Would a matched-instrument study (same venue, different liquidity regimes) isolate it?
    • How do these compact models compare with large pretrained L3 models such as LOBERT once those weights are available, and does end-to-end latency including feed handling keep the advantage?
