    Principal component error in high-dimensional factor models

    Read full article →View on GitHub

    Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
    Date: 2026-09-19

    Source: arxiv · 2026-09-17
    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20550

    PCA-estimated factor directions carry large, persistent error when you have many assets but few observations — and this paper splits that error into a part you can actually estimate from data (a hard floor) and a part that data alone can never pin down.

    Executive summary

    In the high-dimension, low-sample-size (HL) regime, the authors decompose the error sin^2 of the angle between the jth sample eigenvector h_j and the true jth principal direction b_j into an out-of-subspace error (distance from the span of the loadings B) plus an in-subspace 'rotation' error, and show both have almost sure limits as p grows with n fixed. Theorem 2 gives a fully observable, strongly consistent estimator of the out-of-subspace term, making it an estimable floor on total error. Theorem 3 shows the rotation error is not merely hard to estimate but completely unrestricted by the data: it can take any value in [0,1]. A calibrated three-factor US equity simulation (1000 paths, up to p=50,000, n up to 250) confirms the limits and shows out-of-subspace error accounts for 80-93% of predicted error.

    Setup: factor model and the HL asymptotic regime

    Observables follow y = Bf + z with latent factors; the targets are the principal directions b_j, the eigenvectors of Sigma_0 = B Sigma_f B^T. The analysis is in the high-dimension, low-sample-size regime where p grows with n held fixed — motivated by finance, where regime changes leave only short usable histories. Spiked eigenvalues grow like p^alpha with alpha = 1, matching empirical financial models.

    • Only Y = BF + Z is observed; B, F, Z and Sigma_f are latent.
    • Estimator: h_j, the jth eigenvector of the sample covariance matrix S.
    • Contrast with classical spiked-model literature (Johnstone, Paul, Jung and Marron, Shen et al.) that mostly grows n as well.

    Theorem 1: the two-term error decomposition

    The squared sine of the angle between h_j and b_j converges almost surely to OE_j + (1 - OE_j) RE_j, where OE_j is the out-of-subspace error (angle to the span of B) and RE_j the asymptotic rotation error inside that span, reflecting deviation of the sample factor covariance from its population counterpart. Notably, these errors stabilize almost surely for large p rather than exploding.

    • Exact finite-p split is orthogonal: out-of-subspace then in-subspace rotation.
    • OE limit has closed form delta^2 / (n lambda_j + delta^2).
    • IE = (n lambda_j / (n lambda_j + delta^2)) * sin^2 angle(nu_j, e_j).

    Theorem 2: out-of-subspace error is estimable

    Using the n x n scaled dual sample covariance W = Y^T Y /(np), with eigenvalues theta_1 >= ... >= theta_n, define the average bulk eigenvalue l as the mean of the n-k trailing eigenvalues. Then l/theta_j has the same almost sure p-limit as the jth out-of-subspace error, giving a strongly consistent, fully observable lower bound on the total error. Corollary 1 aggregates the per-direction floors into a consistent estimator of the Frobenius distance between the sample subspace and B.

    • Proof uses spectral-norm convergence of W plus Weyl's inequality.
    • Equality with total error holds precisely when the in-subspace rotation vanishes.
    • Because each floor is strictly positive, col(H) is NOT a consistent estimator of B in this regime — but its distance to B is consistently estimable.

    Theorem 3: in-subspace rotation error is non-estimable

    Holding the loadings B, the realized factor path F, the specific returns Z, delta^2 and the eigenvalues of K fixed, and letting the factor covariance Sigma range over all admissible matrices, the data, sample directions and out-of-subspace error are unchanged while the rotation error sweeps the entire interval [0,1]. Hence the limiting total error can be anything from the estimable floor up to 1. Even observing F does not help; only exogenous knowledge of G_B, Sigma_f and a prior on FF^T/n yields a distribution for the rotation error.

    • Vacuous for k = 1 (rotation error identically zero); requires k >= 2.
    • The shortfall is in n, not p: growing the cross-section supplies no additional draws of f.
    • Rotation error does vanish in the large-n limit (Corollary 3).

    Simulation: three-factor US equity calibration

    Guided by Bayraktar et al. (2014), factor and specific returns are iid t-distributed (6 and 5 degrees of freedom) with annualized volatilities 0.16 / 0.08 / 0.06 for the factors and 0.40 specific. Loadings are drawn once from N(1,0.25), N(0,1), N(0,1) giving limiting dual Gram matrix diag(1.25, 1, 1); 1000 nested paths with p up to 50,000 and n up to 250. Errors converge to the theoretical limits; out-of-subspace error dominates throughout, and the data-driven estimate tends to underestimate the true finite-p error.

    • At n = 63, p = 500: total-error angles of roughly 17, 39 and 50 degrees for factors 1-3.
    • Out-of-subspace-only angles at p = 500: 16.3, 33.1, 47.0 degrees.
    • With p = 3000, growing n from 20 to 250 cuts errors from 30 to 8.5 degrees (factor 1), 56 to 19 (factor 2), 66 to 24 (factor 3).
    • Even at n = 250 (a year of daily data) path-by-path deviations remain non-negligible.

    Practical implications

    Practitioners fitting latent factor models can compute the observable floor l/theta_j to bound how badly their principal directions are estimated, decide how many observations a given application requires, and weigh a simple iid model against a more complex one that permits a longer history. The results also caution that the estimable floor is itself typically an underestimate of realized error.

    • Applies beyond finance: genomics, machine learning, signal processing.
    • Three information tiers: data only gives the floor; plus G_B and a law for F gives an error distribution; plus Sigma_f gives a full consistent estimator.

    Key findings

    • Total error splits as sin^2 angle(h_j,b_j) approaching OE_j + (1-OE_j) RE_j, with OE_j = delta^2/(n lambda_j + delta^2) and both terms having almost sure p-limits at fixed n.
    • The observable ratio l/theta_j (average bulk eigenvalue over the jth eigenvalue of the scaled dual sample covariance) is a strongly consistent estimator of out-of-subspace error and therefore a data-driven lower bound on total error.
    • The in-subspace rotation error is completely unidentified: over admissible factor covariances it attains every value in [0,1], so total limiting error ranges over [delta^2/(n lambda_j + delta^2), 1].
    • In the three-factor p = 3000 simulation, out-of-subspace error accounts for 80.2%-92.7% of predicted error across n = 20 to 250 (e.g. 90.0%, 81.1%, 89.0% at n = 63).
    • At n = 63 and p = 500, estimation-error angles were about 17, 39 and 50 degrees for factors 1, 2 and 3; out-of-subspace-only angles were 16.3, 33.1 and 47.0 degrees.
    • Even with a full year of daily data (n = 250, p = 3000) average errors were still roughly 8.5, 19 and 24 degrees for the three factors.

    Notable quotes

    The deficiency is one of sample size n rather than cross-sectional size p.
    The out-of-subspace error dominated the total error.
    This indicates that our estimation error is primarily due to the influence of the idiosyncratic returns on the principal components, pushing them away from the span of the population systematic factors.

    Limitations

    • The empirical evidence is a single simulation design resting on one draw of factor exposures B, which the authors note affects results at small p.
    • The finite-p data-driven estimate tended to underestimate the true out-of-subspace error, and the authors state they have not explored whether that bias is generic.
    • Results assume a k-factor model with known factor count k, simple separated spikes growing like p, and iid-in-time factor/specific returns; no real-market data is analyzed.

    Open questions

    • Is the downward bias of the finite-p estimator l/theta_j systematic, and can it be corrected?
    • How do the decomposition and floor behave when the factor count k is misspecified or must itself be estimated?
    • What priors on the factor path F are defensible in practice so that the non-estimable rotation error can be given a usable distribution?
