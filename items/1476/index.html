Optimal market making

📚 Foundations · 2026-09-13
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-09-13

Theme: Inventory-Aware Market Making and Consistent Order-Book Control
These papers trace market-making control from analytically tractable inventory-risk models, through covariance-aware multi-asset quoting, to event-driven order-book models that enforce economically valid price movements. Together they establish why inventory skew, liquidity, volatility, and cross-asset risk matter—and why unrealistic simulators can manufacture apparent profitability.
Source: arxiv
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.01862

A foundational synthesis that unifies major stochastic-control formulations of inventory-aware market making and extends tractable quote approximations to correlated multi-asset portfolios.

Executive summary

Guéant develops a unified Avellaneda–Stoikov-style framework with general execution-intensity functions for both exponential-utility and inventory-penalized market makers. Variable transformations reduce the original four-dimensional Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman problems to finite systems of ordinary differential equations, with existence, uniqueness, verification, and feedback-control results. A continuous-inventory asymptotic approximation yields tractable quote formulas for single- and multi-asset books, while a calibrated CDX.NA.IG/CDX.NA.HY study shows that correlation materially changes optimal quotes and that the approximations work best near flat inventory or under lower volatility.

Problem and modeling framework

The market maker chooses firm bid and ask offsets around an exogenous Brownian reference price while trades arrive through point processes whose intensities decrease with quote distance. Inventory changes in fixed increments and is bounded by position limits, creating a static margin-versus-volume trade-off and a dynamic need to skew quotes against inventory risk.

  • Model A maximizes CARA utility of terminal marked-to-market wealth, optionally net of a terminal liquidation penalty.
  • Model B maximizes expected terminal marked-to-market wealth minus a quadratic running inventory penalty.
  • The framework permits general decreasing bid and ask intensity functions rather than requiring exponential intensities.

Unified stochastic-control solution

For each objective, a value-function ansatz removes cash and reference price from the state, reducing a four-variable HJB equation to a finite nonlinear ODE system indexed by inventory. A common Hamiltonian family parameterized by ξ covers Model A with ξ = γ and Model B with ξ = 0; ξ therefore isolates aversion to non-execution risk from aversion to price risk.

  • The paper proves the Hamiltonians are smooth and decreasing and that their optimizing quote maps are unique and increasing.
  • A comparison principle and Cauchy–Lipschitz argument establish a unique global solution for the inventory-value function.
  • Verification theorems turn adjacent-inventory value differences into optimal bid and ask feedback quotes.
  • With exponential intensities, a further transformation makes the ODE system linear.

Closed-form asymptotic approximations

The discrete inventory equations are approximated by a continuous-inventory nonlinear PDE under symmetric bid/ask intensities and a curvature condition on the Hamiltonian. A Hopf–Cole transformation and spectral asymptotics produce time-independent, almost-closed-form quotes far from the terminal horizon; for exponential intensities these recover the Guéant–Lehalle–Fernandez-Tapia formulas.

  • The approximations express bid and ask offsets through the intensity function and its Hamiltonian transforms.
  • Inventory raises both optimal quoted prices' offsets in the direction that encourages reversion toward a flat position.
  • For exponential intensities, the approximation has an inventory-independent spread and an inventory-linear skew, although the exact numerical solution need not have either property.
  • Higher liquidity acts like lower volatility, while greater price-risk aversion generally widens spreads and strengthens inventory skew.

Multi-asset extension

The paper replaces scalar price risk with a covariance matrix and derives the corresponding multidimensional ODE system, existence result, verification theorems, and feedback quotes. Its asymptotic approximation contains a matrix derived from covariance and liquidity curvature, so every asset's quote can depend on inventories in correlated assets.

  • The multi-asset objective penalizes total portfolio variance, including cross-inventory covariance terms.
  • Cross-asset terms in the approximation formalize why offsetting positions may require little or no skew despite large individual inventories.
  • The approximation avoids solving the potentially enormous nonlinear ODE system needed for a large dealer book.

Credit-index illustration and practical significance

The framework is calibrated to CDX.NA.IG and CDX.NA.HY using first-half-2016 BNP Paribas prices, quotes, and trades, then solved numerically with an implicit scheme and Newton iterations. The numerical experiments reach their asymptotic regime in roughly one hour, show only small differences between Models A and B in this setting, and demonstrate strong cross-asset effects when correlation is high.

  • For both indices, closed-form quotes are satisfactory near flat inventory but become less accurate at larger absolute positions.
  • Halving IG volatility materially improves the approximation.
  • Varying correlation over 0, 0.3, 0.6, and 0.9 shows that inventory in one index increasingly shifts the other's optimal quote as correlation rises.
  • The example demonstrates why independently running single-asset strategies is suboptimal for correlated dealer books.

Key findings

  • Two widely used objectives—terminal CARA utility and expected P&L with running inventory penalty—reduce to the same inventory-indexed ODE family, differing through ξ = γ versus ξ = 0.
  • The paper establishes existence and uniqueness of the reduced equations and verifies the resulting closed-loop optimal quotes for both single- and multi-asset settings.
  • General asymptotic quote approximations extend the Guéant–Lehalle–Fernandez-Tapia formulas beyond exponential intensities and expose how volatility, liquidity, and risk aversion affect spread and skew.
  • The multi-asset formulas incorporate covariance-driven cross-inventory effects, making portfolio-level risk—not each asset's inventory alone—the relevant signal for quote skew.
  • In the calibrated credit-index example, asymptotic behavior emerges within about one hour, Model B closely tracks Model A, and approximation quality deteriorates for large inventories but improves when volatility is lower.

Notable quotes

The results obtained in Theorem 3.2 and Theorem 3.3 are very useful: they state that the optimal quotes of a market maker in both Model A and Model B can in fact be computed by solving a tridiagonal system of nonlinear ordinary differential equations.
This means that a market maker proposes lower prices at the bid and at the ask when his inventory increases, and conversely, higher prices at the bid and at the ask when his inventory decreases.
We also see that the closed-form approximations are satisfactory for small values of the inventory (in absolute value), but more questionable for larger values.
We see that the correlation coefficient has a strong influence on the optimal quote: the more correlated the two assets, the more conservatively (respectively aggressively) the market maker should quote at the bid when he has a long (respectively short) inventory in the other asset.

Limitations

  • The reference-price process is exogenous and independent of trade arrivals; competition among market makers is not modeled, and the framework omits order-book queue priority, variable trade sizes, and endogenous impact.
  • The closed-form formulas rely on heuristic continuous-inventory and long-horizon approximations and, in their main derivation, symmetric intensities; the numerical study shows visible error at large absolute inventories or higher volatility.
  • The empirical illustration uses two credit indices and first-half-2016 bank data, assumes only spread risk rather than default risk, and does not report an out-of-sample or live-trading P&L comparison.

Open questions

  • Can endogenous price impact, adverse selection, competition, queue position, and variable order sizes be added without losing the low-dimensional control structure?
  • Can finite-horizon and boundary-aware error bounds identify when the asymptotic quote formulas are reliable and produce better approximations for large inventories?
  • How robust are the covariance-aware strategies out of sample when liquidity, correlation, volatility, and intensity parameters evolve or are estimated with uncertainty?
