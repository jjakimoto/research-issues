FormalTCS: Benchmarking End-to-End Frontier Formal Theoretical Computer Science Research of Large Language Models

📰 Latest · 2026-08-24 Full Article
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-08-24

Source: arxiv · 2026-08-20
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20153

FormalTCS grades the whole research loop — informal core claim to self-contained theorem to proof sketch to machine-checked Lean proof — on 175 results from 2025-2026 STOC/FOCS/SODA/COLT papers, and localizes exactly where autonomous research breaks: autoformalization (best model 11.5) and research taste (only 6 of 64 self-generated claims survive expert review plus proof verification), not Lean proof search per se. Natural-language reasoning scores of 66-69 are wildly optimistic proxies for the machine-verifiable end of the pipeline.

Executive summary

FormalTCS (Harbin Institute of Technology) is an expert-validated benchmark of 175 instances, each drawn from a distinct paper accepted to STOC, FOCS, SODA or COLT in 2025-2026, with expert-verified Lean 4.32.2 formalizations and complete compiling proofs. It splits the TCS research pipeline into four separately-scored tasks and finds a huge cliff between the natural-language stages (best 68.7) and the formal stages (best 11.5 autoformalization, 28.6 Pass@8 theorem proving). A second experiment runs a planner/formalizer/judger agent loop that generates its own claims: of 64 synthesized claims, 39 are rejected by experts for lack of novelty and 17 for lack of value, leaving 8, of which 6 get verified proofs. The conclusion is that autonomous TCS research is blocked by translating ideas into rigorous formal objects and by research taste, rather than by proof automation.

Why formal TCS, and what prior benchmarks miss

The paper argues that existing LLM-for-TCS benchmarks (LCS-Bench, built from textbook knowledge, and TCS-Bench, which scores natural-language proofs) are structurally unlike real research in three ways, and FormalTCS is designed as the negation of each.

  • Incomplete research pipeline: prior benchmarks score isolated skills, so they cannot say WHERE in the research process a model fails.
  • Outdated content: prior benchmarks come from textbooks or theorems already in Mathlib, which caps difficulty and invites contamination.
  • Simplified problem settings: real TCS papers carry paper-specific definitions, assumptions, and multi-layer lemma/theorem dependencies.
  • FormalTCS answers with end-to-end staged evaluation, frontier 2025-2026 conference content with leakage-aware filtering, and preservation of paper-specific definitions and proof dependencies.

How the 175 instances were constructed

Five PhD-level TCS experts, each with multiple top-tier publications, built the benchmark with GPT-5.6-sol used only as a drafting assistant; every final annotation was manually inspected and revised. Each instance carries metainfo, three natural-language fields (core_claim, nl_claim, nl_proof) and two formal fields (fl_theorem as a Lean file, fl_proof as a Lean project).

  • 175 instances from 175 distinct STOC/FOCS/SODA/COLT 2025-2026 papers; Lean 4.32.2 with matching Mathlib.
  • Difficulty: the expert-validated Lean proofs average 22.0 statements and 29.6 nodes each.
  • Coverage across 13 TCS areas, led by Learning Theory (29), Neural and Generative Models (22), Statistics and Testing (20), Complexity and Lower Bounds (18).
  • core_claim must be under 36 words; two experts write candidates independently and a third picks the stronger one.
  • Formalization follows a proof-blueprint DAG derived from the source paper, with expert checks for rigour and absence of proof-bypassing constructs, then an independent end-to-end second review.
  • The released fl_theorem replaces the target's proof body with sorry, so the target is the unique unresolved obligation.
  • nl_proof was added because preliminary experiments showed models often cannot prove directly from formal statements.

Tasks and verifier grounding — what is machine-checked vs LLM-judged

Four tasks, each fed HUMAN-annotated inputs rather than the previous stage's model output, so errors do not propagate and each stage is attributed independently. Crucially the four are NOT graded the same way: two are machine-verified in Lean, two are graded by an LLM rubric.

  • Theorem Elicitation (CC2NC): core claim -> NL claim. Graded by LLM-Rubric.
  • Autoformalization (NC2FT): NL claim -> Lean theorem statement. Graded by BEq+, which is MACHINE-CHECKED: it attempts to prove both directions in Lean and only counts equivalence if both proofs are constructed.
  • Proof Elicitation (C2NP): NL claim + formal theorem -> NL proof sketch. Graded by LLM-Rubric.
  • Theorem Proving (FT2FP): Lean theorem -> Lean proof. Graded by Pass@k against the Lean COMPILER.
  • LLM-Rubric: four sub-scores (logical validity 0.4, completeness 0.3, correctness 0.2, clarity 0.1), judged by Qwen3.8-Max, deliberately disjoint from every evaluated model.
  • Anti-cheating: set_option warningAsError true makes sorry an error, plus #print axioms with an explicit whitelist.
  • Decoding: 8 samples per instance for the two Lean tasks (temperature 0.6); a single deterministic generation for the two rubric tasks.

Models evaluated and headline results

Three model families were run inside their own agentic harnesses: GPT-5.6 (luna/terra/sol) on Codex v0.146.0, Claude (Haiku-4.5, Sonnet-5, Opus-5) on Claude Code v2.1.220, and DeepSeek-V4 (Flash, Pro) on the DeepSeek Harness v0.1.0-rc.8. Claude-Opus-5 is strongest on most tasks. The pattern across all eight configurations is identical: high natural-language scores, collapse on the formal stages.

  • Best CC2NC is GPT-5.6-sol at 67.4; best C2NP, NC2FT and FT2FP are all Claude-Opus-5 at 68.7, 11.5 and 28.6.
  • Claude-Opus-5 scores 68.7 on C2NP but only 11.5 on NC2FT — roughly a 6x gap between articulating an idea and formalizing it.
  • NC2FT is the lowest-performing stage across the pipeline, with NO model exceeding 11.5, while models reach up to 28.6 Pass@8 when handed a human-written formal statement.
  • Scale monotonically helps within each family, but even the largest models stay in the low teens on autoformalization.

The automated-research framework: 64 claims in, 6 out

Section 5 builds a multi-agent system that proposes its own TCS claims. Three agents — planner, formalizer, judger — share a workspace, all running GPT-5.6-sol with Codex. Each run randomly seeds 16 FormalTCS instances to encourage diversity.

  • Planner proposes a research objective and may reformulate the problem, add auxiliary concepts, strengthen or relax assumptions, or change analytical direction.
  • Formalizer turns the objective into a Lean statement and iterates against the compiler for AT MOST three rounds; non-compiling claims are discarded and fed back.
  • Judger back-translates a compiling statement into NL and assesses novelty/value. Survivors go to a human-expert pool.
  • The authors explicitly did NOT build an automated claim-quality evaluator, calling reliable automatic novelty/value estimation an open problem.
  • Funnel: 64 generated claims -> 39 rejected for lack of novelty, 17 for lack of value -> 8 pass expert evaluation -> 2 unproved, 6 with a verified proof.
  • Notable self-consistency effect: among claims that pass expert screening, the proof success rate is much higher than the 28.6 FT2FP ceiling — attributed to the claims aligning with the model's own reasoning trajectories. A warning about self-generated benchmarks.

Contamination controls, agreement audits, and reliability caveats

Because the benchmark's premise is frontier, unseen content, the authors run a black-box leakage audit and two agreement studies. Reading these matters for anyone treating the numbers as ground truth.

  • Contamination audit: output-only, no retrieval, metadata stripped. Three probe types — theorem completion, proof continuation, result reconstruction — scored with token-level lexical similarity (ROUGE-L/LCS).
  • Aggregate completion similarity is below 9.6% for both probed models; the authors call this low contamination RISK, not proof of non-exposure.
  • Inter-expert agreement: core claim 86%, natural-language claim 90%, formal-language proof 93%.
  • Expert modification rate of LLM-drafted annotations: formal theorem 31% (highest), proof blueprint 24%, formal proof 15%, NL proof 13% — the same ranking as the model results.
  • Rubric validity (16 sampled examples): the LLM rubric systematically scores higher than humans (avg 4.43% on CC2NC, 8.14% on C2NP), but produces EXACTLY the same ranking of all eight configurations.

Key findings

  • Autoformalization is the sharpest bottleneck and it is MACHINE-VERIFIED, not judged: no model exceeds 11.5 on NC2FT under BEq+ (bidirectional Lean proof of equivalence), while the same models reach up to 28.6 Pass@8 on proof generation when handed a human-written formal statement.
  • The gap between informal and formal ability is roughly 6x for the same model: Claude-Opus-5 scores 68.7 on proof-strategy generation versus 11.5 on autoformalization; GPT-5.6-sol is 67.9 versus 10.6.
  • Research taste, not proof automation, is the second wall: the agent loop produced 64 Lean-compiling claims its own judger accepted, but human experts rejected 39 for lack of novelty and 17 for lack of value. The LLM judger's novelty filter is near-useless as a standalone gate.
  • Self-generated claims are much easier for the model to prove than benchmark claims — a direct caution against self-generated evaluation sets.
  • Formal statement construction is hard for humans-with-LLMs too: expert modification of LLM-drafted annotations is highest for the formal theorem (31%) versus formal proof (15%) and NL proof (13%).
  • Contamination appears low but is not ruled out: metadata-stripped, retrieval-free reconstruction probes yield aggregate ROUGE-L/LCS similarity below 9.6%, framed as low risk rather than proof of non-exposure.
  • The benchmark is research-scale: 175 instances from 175 distinct 2025-2026 STOC/FOCS/SODA/COLT papers across 13 areas, with expert-verified Lean 4.32.2 proofs averaging 22.0 statements and 29.6 DAG nodes, and no sorry/extra-axiom escape hatches.

Notable quotes

Rather, the more fundamental challenge lies in correctly identifying the mathematical objects, assumptions, and logical structure underlying a natural-language claim and translating them into appropriate formal definitions and theorem statements.
Unlike evaluation methods based on LLM judges, this procedure relies on deterministic symbolic proof search.
These results indicate that, for current LLMs, the primary bottleneck in conducting end-to-end TCS research lies in generating claims that are both novel and valuable, suggesting that their research taste remains limited.
The explanation is that these claims are generated by the LLMs themselves, making it easier for the LLMs to construct proofs for claims that align with their own reasoning trajectories.

Limitations

  • No human/expert baseline is reported on the four tasks, so the paper never states a numeric human-vs-model gap; the only expert numbers are humans RATING model outputs.
  • Half the headline numbers are not machine-verified: CC2NC and C2NP are scored by an LLM rubric validated on only 16 sampled examples that systematically over-scores. Also, BEq+ can under-count — a semantically fine formalization whose equivalence proof cannot be found in Lean is scored as a failure.
  • The contamination audit is lexical-only and output-only; the authors concede that failure to reproduce a passage cannot rule out training-data presence.
  • The automated-research experiment is a single configuration (GPT-5.6-sol with Codex, n=64 claims, 16 seed instances per run) with no ablation and no repeated trials — so 6/64 is one point estimate.
  • Stage-wise evaluation feeds human-annotated inputs to every task, so the paper never measures true compounding end-to-end performance; the real cascaded success rate would be substantially lower.
  • Results are tied to Lean 4.32.2 and to specific harness versions, so scores measure model-plus-harness, not the model alone.

Open questions

  • Can autoformalization be improved enough to unblock the pipeline — and is the failure mode retrievable definitions/Mathlib grounding, or genuine mathematical modeling?
  • How do you build an automated novelty/value judge? The authors declined to include one, yet their LLM judger passed 56 claims experts later rejected — a reliable research-taste gate is the highest-leverage missing component.
  • How much of the gap between 28.6 Pass@8 and the much higher self-generated proof rate is genuine capability versus reasoning-trajectory self-alignment?
  • Does the picture change with a proof-search-specialized system (DeepSeek-Prover-style, AlphaProof-style) rather than general agentic harnesses?
