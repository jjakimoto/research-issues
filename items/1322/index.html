Deep Hedging Under Realistic Market Frictions: A Regime-Conditional Empirical Study of Dynamic Option Hedging on Bitcoin Options

📰 Latest · 2026-09-05 Full Article
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-09-05

Source: arxiv · 2026-08-29
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29025

A fair, real-data test of deep hedging on Deribit BTC options: all three neural hedgers lose to all three classical benchmarks on every metric, while a 1997 closed-form no-trade band (Whalley-Wilmott) cuts costs by $1.79/episode (p<0.0001) by trading 2.47 times per 24-step episode instead of ~20.4. The mechanism matters more than the scoreboard — cost-awareness in the loss function did not make the networks stop trading; only an explicit structural trade/no-trade rule did.

Executive summary

Sheryan Kumar (VNIT Nagpur) argues the deep-hedging literature is benchmarked unfairly — against frictionless classical baselines, on simulated paths — and re-runs the comparison on five years of real Deribit BTC options data (2020-2024, 61 monthly snapshot days from Tardis.dev) with an identical 5 bp round-trip cost applied to every strategy. Three classical benchmarks (Black-Scholes delta, Leland adjusted-volatility, Whalley-Wilmott no-trade band) are pitted against three deep hedgers (LSTM with CVaR(95%) loss, the same LSTM plus a turnover penalty beta=5, and a feedforward net with beta=100) over 11,546 out-of-sample hourly-rebalancing episodes. Whalley-Wilmott wins outright on mean P&L (-30.16 vs -38.82 for BS delta), CVaR95 (-864.23), mean cost (7.91 vs 9.70) and trade count (2.47 vs 20.40). All three neural configurations converge to a mean trade count of ~20.44 — statistically indistinguishable from continuous delta-hedging — despite a twenty-fold range of turnover penalties. The author is explicit that the P&L edge is regime-conditional: on the calmer validation window Whalley-Wilmott is marginally the WORST of the three classical strategies on mean P&L while keeping its cost edge.

The problem: deep hedging has been graded on a curve

Buehler et al. (2019) train a network end-to-end to minimise a coherent risk measure (CVaR) and internalise transaction costs directly into the objective. The paper's complaint is about the comparison, not the method: reported gains are usually measured against a frictionless or lightly cost-adjusted classical baseline, and evaluated on simulated paths generated by the very process the classical baseline assumes. The narrower question asked here is whether a standard deep hedging policy beats classical methods that were themselves designed for transaction costs.

  • Section 2.2: 'a frictionless comparison does not test whether the network has learned anything beyond what a simpler, already-existing cost-aware adjustment provides'
  • The paper's own framing of the usual setup: 'This is not really a fair test.'
  • Trade-off accepted for fairness: a smaller, real, bounded dataset instead of an arbitrarily long simulated one

Data: Deribit BTC options, 61 sampled days, not a continuous series

Historical Deribit BTC options data via Tardis.dev's free tier, which publishes full option-chain snapshots only for the FIRST DAY of each calendar month (continuous access was quoted at ~$650/month, out of reach for this self-funded study). The dataset is therefore 61 independent 24-hour windows, Jan 2020 - Dec 2024, downsampled from up to ~8.9M tick rows/day to hourly. Each (contract, sampled day) pair is one hedging episode of at most 24 hourly steps. Three filters were applied to 892,912 hourly rows across 22,866 instruments: a two-sided-quote liquidity filter (removed 27.0% of rows), a moneyness filter (K/S outside 0.5-2.0), and a relative-spread filter (>50% of mid), added after tick-size quantisation produced artificial spread spikes at 0.67 and 1.0.

  • Regime label per day = median ATM implied volatility (moneyness 0.95-1.05); range 32%-111%, mean 63%
  • Chronological split, no shuffling: train Dec 2019-Dec 2022 (187,961 rows; COVID crash, 2021 bull, Terra/Luna + FTX), validation Jan-Aug 2023 (64,474 rows, calm), test Sept 2023-Dec 2024 (237,894 rows, 2024 rally)
  • Test set = 11,546 episodes but only 16 unique sampled days — the binding constraint on statistical power
  • Training set = 8,922 episodes, versus the 1e5-1e6 simulated paths typical in the deep hedging literature

Setup: identical 5 bp friction, short-option exposure, hourly rebalancing

Every strategy hedges a SHORT position in a single BTC option using BTC as the hedging instrument, rebalancing hourly. Terminal hedging error per episode sums option mark-to-market P&L, hedge P&L, and transaction costs. Cost is a round-trip rate k = 5 bp, charged as 2.5 bp per individual trade. The author deliberately refuses to use the option's own (much wider) bid-ask spread as the hedging-cost proxy: an earlier pipeline version did that and produced Leland-adjusted volatility above 300%, more than double the underlying implied vol.

  • Metrics: mean terminal P&L, std dev, CVaR(95%), mean transaction cost, mean turnover, mean trades per episode, plus a day-ordered max drawdown
  • Inference: block bootstrap, 5,000 resamples, resampling WHOLE SAMPLED DAYS (16 blocks in test) — episodes on the same day share a price path and are not independent
  • Max drawdown is built from day-averaged terminal P&L because episodes are independent by construction, not one continuous path

The three classical baselines and the three deep hedgers

Classical: (1) BS delta using each contract's own quoted mark_iv, rebalanced to the exact target every hour; (2) Leland (1985) — the same delta with volatility inflated for the 5 bp cost over a one-hour interval; (3) Whalley-Wilmott (1997) — a no-trade band of half-width H around the BS delta target, rebalancing only to the NEAREST BAND EDGE when breached, with risk-aversion lambda = 60 chosen by inspection. Neural: state = 5 features (moneyness, time-to-maturity, implied vol capped at 300%, the contemporaneous BS delta, call/put flag), standardised on train statistics only. LSTM with 32 hidden units; feedforward with two 32-unit ReLU layers over a fixed 4-step lookback. Both emit position via 1.5*tanh. Loss = CVaR at alpha = 0.95 (Rockafellar-Uryasev), optionally plus beta times mean per-episode turnover at beta = 5 and beta = 100.

  • The BS delta is FED TO the network as an input feature, so the networks were not asked to rediscover delta from scratch
  • Deep hedgers had cost-awareness in the loss AND an explicit turnover penalty — the comparison is not a strawman on the ML side
  • The feedforward/beta=100 run exists specifically to test whether recurrence or penalty strength was the missing ingredient

Results: the 1997 band wins; the networks never stop trading

On 11,546 test episodes, Whalley-Wilmott has the best mean P&L (-30.16), the best CVaR95 (-864.23), the lowest cost (7.91) and the lowest turnover (0.545), while trading 2.47 times per 24-step episode against roughly 20.4 for everything else. Mean P&L is negative for all six strategies because every one is short an option through a sustained BTC rally — only the relative ranking is meaningful. The cost saving versus BS delta is -1.79 per episode, 95% CI [-2.21, -1.39], p<0.0001; the P&L advantage of +8.66 (p=0.164) is directionally favourable but NOT significant, attributed to only 16 independent test days. The turnover penalty did the wrong thing: LSTM v2 cut mean turnover from 0.685 to 0.647 and cost by -0.54 (p<0.0001) versus v1, with no P&L improvement (p=0.964) and slightly worse CVaR — it shrank the SIZE of trades without reducing HOW OFTEN the model traded.

  • Mean trade count: WW 2.47, BS delta 20.40, Leland 20.41, all three neural configs ~20.44
  • Feedforward (beta=100) vs LSTM v2 (beta=5): P&L difference -3.66, p=0.506 — neither architecture nor penalty strength explains the gap
  • LSTM v2 vs BS delta P&L: -8.42, 95% CI [-17.21, 0.02], p=0.051 — the neural policy is borderline worse than the naive baseline it was supposed to beat
  • Max drawdown across sampled test days: WW -483.38, BS delta -579.38, Leland -580.06
  • Leland is essentially a no-op here (P&L -38.91 vs -38.82, cost 9.68 vs 9.70) — the adjustment is economically negligible when k is small relative to sigma
  • On a representative episode (BTC-10MAY24-56000-C, 1 May 2024) Whalley-Wilmott executes 6 rebalances in 24 steps while the deep hedger trades 24 out of 24

Honesty check: the P&L result is regime-conditional, the cost result is not

On the calmer Jan-Aug 2023 validation window (3,520 episodes), Whalley-Wilmott's P&L advantage disappears entirely and it becomes marginally the WORST of the three classical strategies on mean P&L (-5.06 vs Leland -4.81 and BS delta -4.94), while its cost edge persists (3.50 vs 4.02, at 2.06 trades vs 18.08). The author treats this as direct in-sample evidence that the two headline results do not have the same evidentiary strength, and explicitly refuses the stronger reading: it is not accurate to say deep hedging 'does not work' in general, only that in this window, under this cost assumption, it did not outperform.

  • The mechanistic claim is the real contribution: cost-awareness in the LOSS is not a substitute for a cost-aware STRUCTURE
  • Diagnosis offered: data scale (8,922 training episodes vs 1e5-1e6 simulated paths) and no structural inductive bias toward sparsity
  • Proposed fix for future work: architectures with an explicit learned threshold or gating mechanism
  • The author self-describes the paper as 'a modest but honest contribution'

Key findings

  • All three deep hedging configurations underperform all three classical benchmarks on every measured metric (mean P&L, CVaR95, cost, turnover, trade count) on 11,546 out-of-sample episodes.
  • The deep hedgers never learned to stop trading: mean trade count ~20.44 per 24-step episode for all three neural configs, statistically indistinguishable from BS delta's 20.40, and 8x more than Whalley-Wilmott's 2.47 — despite a twenty-fold range of turnover-penalty weights.
  • Whalley-Wilmott's cost saving over BS delta is -1.79 per episode, 95% CI [-2.21, -1.39], p<0.0001 (roughly 88% fewer trades); its P&L edge of +8.66 is directionally favourable but insignificant (p=0.164) because only 16 independent sampled days exist in the test set.
  • The turnover penalty reduced trade SIZE, not trade FREQUENCY: LSTM v2's mean turnover fell 0.685 -> 0.647 with a significant cost drop (p<0.0001), but no P&L improvement (+0.11, p=0.964) and slightly worse CVaR.
  • The feedforward control (no recurrence, beta twenty times larger) was statistically indistinguishable from the LSTM (p=0.506) and significantly worse than Whalley-Wilmott (p=0.0044), ruling out both architecture choice and penalty strength as the explanation.
  • Leland's adjustment is nearly a no-op at 5 bp: mean P&L -38.91 vs BS delta's -38.82, cost 9.68 vs 9.70, trade count 20.41 vs 20.40.
  • The P&L result is explicitly regime-conditional: on the calm validation window Whalley-Wilmott is marginally the worst classical strategy on mean P&L (-5.06) while its cost advantage persists (3.50 vs 4.02 at 2.06 vs 18.08 trades).

Notable quotes

This pattern holds across the full test set: the mean trade count for all three neural configurations, about 20.44, is statistically indistinguishable from BS delta’s 20.40, and far above Whalley-Wilmott’s 2.47.
All three neural configurations, the base LSTM, the turnover-penalized LSTM, and the feedforward network, converged to a mean trade count statistically indistinguishable from continuous BS-delta rebalancing, about 20.4 of 24 steps, despite a twenty-fold range in penalty weight.
Whalley-Wilmott holds its position flat for extended stretches whenever the BS delta stays within its calibrated no-trade band, executing only 6 rebalances across the 24 hourly steps.
The final cleaned dataset contains 490,229 rows across the full date range.

Limitations

  • Sampling artefact drives the power: Tardis.dev's free tier gives only the first day of each month, so the test set has 11,546 episodes but just 16 independent sampled days — the block bootstrap's effective n is 16, which is why the large cost effect reached significance while the noisier P&L and CVaR effects did not. Every episode is a self-contained 24-hour window.
  • Single continuous test window (Sept 2023 - Dec 2024) dominated by the 2024 BTC rally, and a single asset class. Mean P&L is negative for every strategy because all are short an option into a sustained uptrend, so only relative rankings are interpretable, and the validation check shows the P&L ranking flips in a calmer regime.
  • Tuning asymmetry and cost realism: the Whalley-Wilmott risk-aversion parameter lambda = 60 was chosen 'by inspection' with no sensitivity analysis, while the networks trained on only 8,922 episodes — orders of magnitude below the literature standard. Cost is a flat 5 bp round trip across all regimes, moneyness levels and liquidity conditions.

Open questions

  • Is the neural underperformance a data-scale artefact? The direct test is retraining on continuous paid historical data or with validated simulation-based pretraining, which this study could not afford.
  • Would an architecture with an explicit gating or learned-threshold mechanism — structural inductive bias toward inaction rather than penalty shaping — close the gap the author attributes to the missing hard trade/no-trade decision?
  • How much of Whalley-Wilmott's edge is regime-specific? The proposed test is repeating the comparison across independent windows isolating a purely calm, a purely crash, and a purely trending period, and extending to equity index options where continuous coverage is available.
