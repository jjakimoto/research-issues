A contribution to the critique of blockchain censorship

📰 Latest · 2026-08-19
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-08-19

Source: arXiv (cs.CR, cross-listed q-fin.TR) · 2026-08-18
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.15640

Challenges the claim that joining a censoring validator coalition is a dominant strategy: once detection cost genuinely binds, large stakers ('whales') can earn strictly less than fair-play value by joining, and their abstention can starve the attack of the power it needs. The counterintuitive corollary is that some stake centralization may protect against this censorship attack.

Executive summary

The paper revisits a prior result showing that joining a blockchain censorship coalition is a dominant strategy, and argues that the result depends on detection being effectively in-built so the attack halts before it is ever detected. Allowing detected attacks — and therefore letting the detection cost, modeled as a crypto price drop, actually be incurred — reverses the conclusion for validators with disproportionate voting power. The authors prove a threshold theorem for a whale/minnow stake model and confirm it by Monte Carlo simulation calibrated on real Ethereum and Solana stake distributions, finding the theorem applies comfortably to Ethereum but is silent for Solana even though simulation still shows the effect there.

The model

Validators are split into a small number of whales with large individual power and many minnows with small power, with arrival order uniformly random over all permutations.

  • n validators with powers normalized to sum to 1; w whales with power alpha each, n-w minnows with power epsilon; w is O(1) and alpha/epsilon is O(n).
  • Key objects: the k-sum of powers, a power-only stopping time, a stopping time with coalition size, and a hypergeometric whale count with expectation kw/n.
  • The detectability threshold eta corresponds to (n-k)/n.
  • 'Whale's gain' is compared against the 'whale fair play value' alpha*w — the payoff from not joining.

The theorem and its bite

The chain of results (Lemmas 1–3, Propositions 1–2, Lemma 4) culminates in a threshold on validator count above which whales strictly lose by joining across a wide band of coalition sizes.

  • Theorem 4.1: for n at or above an explicit n_0, the whale's gain is strictly below alpha*w for coalition sizes between max{k_m, k-hat, w} and n.
  • Threshold set at t = 1/2 in the numerical work.
  • All four simulated gain curves show three regimes: a flat/favorable band, a detrimental transition, and a rise back to fair-play value at k = n — since attacking everyone is equivalent to attacking no one.

Calibration on real stake data

Stake data comes from Dune, an Ethereum staking dashboard, and Helius Solana figures; both chains' stake distributions are best fit by a stretched exponential, which supports the two-type whale/minnow abstraction.

  • Ethereum: roughly 1M validators at 32 ETH each, regrouped by stake share into 10,040 validators. Solana yields 1,397.
  • Ethereum top stakers: Lido 22.3%, Binance 8.3%, ether.fi 5.2%, Coinbase 4.5%, Kraken 3.8%, Figment 3.7%.
  • Solana top stakers: Galaxy 3.23%, Helius 3.22%, Coinbase 02 3.03%, Figment 2.57%.
  • Whale count w is set to the ceiling of the characteristic length from the stretched-exponential fit.

Where the bound holds and where it does not

The honest part of the paper is that its own theoretical threshold is loose enough to miss Solana entirely, while the simulation still shows the predicted effect there.

  • Ethereum: n_0 = 19, far below the 10,040 regrouped validators, so the theorem applies.
  • Solana: n_0 = 224,137, far above 1,397, so the theorem says nothing — yet simulation still shows whale-profit reduction for coalition sizes above 425.
  • For Ethereum the bound gives k* = 7232 against a simulated k of 5781, so the bound holds but is conservative.

Key findings

  • Under a whale/minnow power distribution with a real detection cost, whales can earn strictly less than the fair-play value by joining a censorship coalition — undercutting the practical bite of the dominant-strategy result.
  • If whales abstain, minnows alone struggle to reach the power threshold needed to launch the attack.
  • Some degree of stake centralization may therefore protect against this censorship attack; a pure-minnow chain is more vulnerable.
  • Both Ethereum and Solana stake distributions are best fit by a stretched exponential (R-squared 0.994 and 0.984), supporting the two-type model.
  • The theoretical threshold n_0 scales as O(2^w), which is why it covers Ethereum with room to spare but fails to cover Solana despite simulated evidence of the same effect.

Limitations

  • The theoretical bound is loose — exponential in the whale count — so it is silent for Solana and overshoots the observed Ethereum coalition size (7232 vs 5781).
  • Arrival order is assumed uniformly random; whales bribing or selecting themselves into early positions is explicitly unmodeled, and the authors flag validator reordering as a form of MEV left for future work.
  • The detection cost is assumed much larger than attack rewards, and the results are framed as identifying parameter regimes rather than contradicting the prior dominant-strategy result.

Open questions

  • Does the result survive once validators can influence arrival order — that is, once MEV-style reordering is inside the model rather than assumed away?
  • Can the O(2^w) threshold be tightened enough to cover chains with Solana-like validator counts, where simulation already shows the effect?
  • Is the 'centralization protects against censorship' conclusion robust to whales colluding off-chain, where the detection-cost externality can be renegotiated?
