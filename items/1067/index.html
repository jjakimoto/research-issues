Microprice Drift and the Theta Wall: Why Order-Book Depth Cannot Time Option Execution

Source: ssrn · 2026-08
Link: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7328418

A pre-registered options-microstructure study that separates informativeness from monetizability: top-of-book depth imbalance genuinely predicts next-hour delta-hedged option moves (controlled rank IC 0.104, t = 66.9), yet cannot be traded and cannot even time executions, because option decay swamps the signal. Its portable output is an inequality, g(h) > θ$h, that tells you before you build anything whether execution timing can pay in a decaying instrument.

Executive summary

The paper tests whether depth imbalance — one of the strongest short-horizon predictors in equity limit-order books — exports to options, using 5.7 years of hourly SPY option-chain snapshots (2020-09-01 to 2026-05-12, 1,429 trading days) and top-of-book bid/ask size newly extracted from raw vendor files (20,823,814 contract-snapshots, 100.00% join to the quote panel). Three pre-registered results answer yes, no, and no: the signal is real (controlled rank IC 0.104 at one hour, t = 66.9), it cannot be a standalone strategy because option round-trip toll is flat in horizon while the move grows with √t, and it cannot even time fills for trades a desk was doing anyway because theta decay (≈ $1.2/contract/hour) is five times the depth tilt (+$0.24 to +$0.46). The authors distil this into a general bound, g(h) > θ$h, and use it to reconcile their negative result with Muravyev and Pearson (2021), who find large execution-timing benefits at second-to-minute horizons. For a practitioner the value is a cheap pre-build test rather than another alpha claim: measure the instrument's decay against the signal's conditional drift before writing any code.

The question: does the equity book's best signal export to options?

Depth imbalance forecasts the direction of the next price move in equity books, anchors the microprice, and underlies a generation of execution and market-making models (Gould et al. 2013; Cont et al. 2014; Stoikov 2018). Options look like a natural export market — quotes are wide, so predicting the next quote move is worth more. The authors extract posted bid and ask size, fields they note are routinely dropped in research extractions, from all 1,430 raw daily files, and study the out-of-the-money belt where a premium seller actually operates. Everything is screen-basis: no fill simulation, no queue modelling, no performance claims, with all tests pre-registered (fixed horizons, controls, decision bars) before any statistic was computed.

  • Data: SPY option chain, hourly snapshots, 2020-09-01 to 2026-05-12, 1,429 trading days.
  • 20,823,814 contract-snapshots recovered from raw vendor files, joining the quote panel at 100.00% on exact contract identity.
  • Studied belt: out-of-the-money, |Δ| ∈ [0.05, 0.40], 25–45 days to expiry; median 195 contracts bid, 182 offered — roughly two orders of magnitude above a retail clip, so depth is not the capacity constraint.
  • imb = (bid size − ask size)/(bid size + ask size); 5th–95th percentile −0.81 to +0.82, only 6% of quotes exactly balanced.
  • A confound the design has to handle: the deepest quintile of the book carries the WIDEST relative spreads (1.37% vs 0.83% for the thinnest) — backwards from equity intuition, and a price-level artifact of cheap far-from-the-money contracts.

Result 1 — the signal is real (controlled IC 0.104 at one hour)

Within each snapshot the authors rank-correlate imbalance against the forward delta-hedged mid move, which strips the underlying's directional component and isolates option-specific return. The controlled IC residualises on five covariates: moneyness (|δ|), price level log(mid), relative spread, log(1+volume) and log(1+open interest). Volume and open interest matter most to the argument: because the unsigned activity family carries nothing here, posted depth survives as incremental information rather than repackaged flow. A within-snapshot shuffle null returns essentially zero, and all eight declared tests clear the Bonferroni bar.

  • 1 hour: raw IC 0.110, controlled 0.104, NW t = 66.9, 9,958 snapshots.
  • Decay is monotone across horizons — 0.104 → 0.076 → 0.038 → 0.011 from one hour to nine days.
  • The controlled IC never approaches the paper's stated deployable-standalone requirement of ≈ 0.15.
  • Survival of the volume/open-interest controls is the substantive point: this is standing information, distinct from executed order flow, whose information content in options is already established.

Result 2 — the horizon gradient: flat toll, √t move

Before testing the signal the authors bound its driver with a perfect-foresight oracle ("Gate-0"): full knowledge of the sign of the delta-hedged move, charged the entire quoted spread at entry and exit plus hedge costs. The structural fact that falls out is that option round-trip toll is essentially constant in holding period ($2.36 at one hour, $2.37 at nine days) while the option-specific move accumulates with √t. Friction therefore mechanically rewards patience, and an hourly signal fights it about 19× harder per unit of movement than a nine-day signal. Multiplying the measured IC by the oracle ceiling gives an implied achievable value of ≈ 0.104 × 10.1 ≈ 1.05 at the hourly clock, before estimation error and implementation — below any deployable threshold.

  • Move-to-toll ratio rises 1.78× (hourly) → 2.81× (2h) → 6.79× (daily) → 34.4× (nine days).
  • Oracle Sharpe ceilings are perfect-foresight bounds, not attainable performance: 10.1 / 12.6 / 12.9 at 1h / 2h / 1d.
  • The required standalone IC (≈ 0.15) exceeds anything the positioning literature sustains at daily horizons.
  • Design implication the authors draw: slow expressions of any option signal deserve the first look, not the last.

Result 3 — the theta wall kills execution timing too

The natural fallback when a signal cannot carry a strategy is execution timing — use it to time fills for trades already on the book, where it bears no toll. Tested on a representative premium-seller population (out-of-the-money puts, one-to-two-month tenor, snapshots early enough to permit a same-day wait, n = 93,165): fill now at the bid versus wait one snapshot and fill at the next bid, delta-adjusted for the intervening underlying move. The authors emphasise that the tradeable side, not the mid, is the object, since imbalance predicting mid drift is partly the mechanical microprice adjustment a crossing trader cannot capture. Every imbalance quintile loses. The level is theta — the hedged expected drift of an option price over one hour IS its decay — and the depth tilt cannot climb it. The apparent buy-side gain (+$1.57) is the same theta with the sign flipped, available to anyone without any signal at all.

  • Wait-to-sell, hedged, conditional on the signal being right: −$1.19/contract, NW t = −4.31 — significant in the wrong direction.
  • Theta on this universe ≈ $1.2 per contract per hour; the depth tilt is +$0.24 conditional (+$0.46 in the wider belt, t 5.2) — about five times smaller.
  • Quintile improvements run −$0.90 to −$1.43; there is no book state in which waiting pays.
  • The unhedged variant is worse still: −$6.85/contract, NW t = −6.09.
  • Decision rule tested was fixed and pre-registered: "wait iff imb > 0.33" against always-now, plus the buy mirror and the unhedged variant.

The portable output: g(h) > θ$h, and the Muravyev–Pearson reconciliation

The paper's most transferable claim is an inequality rather than a result: a liquidity taker benefits from signal-timed delay only where the signal-conditional improvement of the tradeable side, g(h), outruns the instrument's deterministic decay, θ$h. The right-hand side grows linearly in the delay while g(h) is capped by the signal's conditional drift forecast, so no improvement in signal accuracy at a given horizon can rescue a violated inequality. This is what reconciles the negative result with Muravyev and Pearson (2021), who show traders timing option executions pay effective spreads under 40% of conventionally measured ones: their effect lives at second-to-minute horizons where decay is negligible, and the authors are explicit that their own linear extrapolation to ≈ $0.02 per minute is an extrapolation their data cannot resolve directly. The two studies bracket the usable timescale of option execution timing.

  • At one hour: θ$h ≈ $1.2/contract measured, g(h) ≈ $0.24–$0.46 measured — the inequality fails by roughly 5×.
  • The bound is instrument-agnostic: any signal, any decaying instrument.
  • Operational advice the authors give: measure decay against conditional drift BEFORE building anything.
  • The reconciliation is a bracket, not a refutation — the two papers sit on opposite sides of one boundary.

A complementary null: the hour cannot sign trades either

The natural escalation from posted depth is executed direction. Signing trade prints against prevailing quotes (Lee and Ready, 1991) works mechanically — 96% of traded contract-hours are signable, since options print at the touch — but the pre-registered tests at the cell granularity used in positioning research (persistence of signed demand, lead onto adjacent cells, a skew-axis aggregate) come in at roughly one-tenth of their declared bars. The diagnostic is that hourly aggregation destroys initiation information outright. The two nulls are complementary in an instructive way: posted depth is informative but unmonetizable, while hourly-signed flow is not even informative.

  • Cell ICs +0.006 to +0.029 against declared bars of 0.09–0.175.
  • Open print and close print of the same hour agree on direction only 59% of the time.
  • Signability is not informativeness — a warning for anyone building positioning signals off bar-aggregated prints.

Key findings

  • Depth imbalance in SPY options is genuine, control-surviving information: controlled rank IC 0.104 at one hour (t = 66.9, 9,958 snapshots), residualised on moneyness, price level, relative spread, volume and open interest, with a zero shuffle null. Because the unsigned volume/open-interest family carries nothing, this is incremental standing information rather than repackaged flow.
  • Option friction has a horizon structure equity intuition does not prepare you for: the round-trip toll is flat ($2.36 at one hour, $2.37 at nine days) while the option-specific move grows with √t, so the move-to-toll ratio climbs from 1.78× hourly to 34.4× at nine days and an hourly signal fights friction ≈ 19× harder per unit of movement.
  • Implied achievable value at the hourly clock is ≈ 0.104 × 10.1 ≈ 1.05 before estimation error and implementation — below a deployable threshold — and the required standalone IC (≈ 0.15) exceeds what the positioning literature sustains at daily horizons.
  • The theta wall: a premium seller who delays one hour forfeits ≈ $1.2/contract of decay to collect a +$0.24–$0.46 depth tilt, so waiting loses −$1.19/contract (t = −4.31) even conditional on the signal being right, and every imbalance quintile is negative (−$0.90 to −$1.43).
  • The general bound g(h) > θ$h governs execution timing in any decaying instrument, and no improvement in signal accuracy at a fixed horizon can rescue it — which is what places this paper and Muravyev and Pearson (2021), whose timing gains live at second-to-minute horizons, on opposite sides of one boundary rather than in contradiction.
  • A complementary null on executed direction: 96% of traded contract-hours are signable via Lee–Ready, but pre-registered positioning tests land at roughly one-tenth of their bars (cell ICs +0.006 to +0.029 vs 0.09–0.175), because the open and close prints of the same hour agree on direction only 59% of the time.

Notable quotes

posted depth is not a repackaging of activity; it is standing information about the direction of the next quote adjustment—the options analogue of the microprice (Stoikov, 2018).
An hourly signal therefore fights friction ∼ 19× harder per unit of movement than a nine-day signal, and an IC of 0.10 at the hourly clock is worth less, deployably, than nothing.
Every imbalance quintile’s wait-to-sell improvement is negative (−$0.90 to −$1.43; Figure 3). The mechanism, once named, is obvious: the hedged expected drift of an option price over one hour is its theta—≈ $1.2 per contract per hour on this universe, the right order for the moneyness belt and tenor studied—and a premium seller who delays forfeits precisely the decay the position exists to collect.
the open print and the close print of the same hour agree on direction only 59% of the time. At hourly aggregation, initiator information does not survive; signability is not informativeness.

Limitations

  • One underlying (SPY) and hourly snapshots only, so the minute-scale regime where Muravyev and Pearson (2021) operate is unobservable; the reconciliation of the two regimes is an extrapolation of the decay line (≈ $0.02/minute), not a measurement of their side.
  • Top-of-book only — hidden and away-from-touch liquidity are unobserved — and everything is screen basis: no queue modelling, no fill simulation, no performance claims.
  • The theta-wall bound applies to liquidity takers; a maker with queue priority faces different economics the data cannot address. The premium-seller universe in Result 3 is representative rather than exhaustive, though belt-wide monotonicity (+$0.46, t 5.2) suggests the tilt generalises.

Open questions

  • Does depth imbalance retain usable information at the second-to-minute horizons where the inequality g(h) > θ$h can actually be satisfied, or does the signal decay below threshold before decay stops binding?
  • How do maker economics differ — with queue priority and no crossing toll, does the same depth tilt become extractable where it is not for a taker?
  • Does the bound bind as tightly in other decaying instruments (short-dated calendars, futures roll structures, perpetual funding) and does the flat-toll/√t-move gradient generalise beyond SPY options?
