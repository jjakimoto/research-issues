Market Making via Reinforcement Learning

📚 Foundations · 2026-09-10 Full Article
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-09-10

Theme: Execution and market making across order books and AMMs
These papers treat execution quality as a control-and-routing problem shaped by market mechanics. Reinforcement learning addresses inventory-aware quoting inside a queue-based limit-order-book simulator, while convex optimization determines how trades should be split across heterogeneous constant-function market makers.
Source: arxiv
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.04216

A foundational empirical study showing how realistic execution simulation, inventory-aware rewards, and temporal-difference learning shape automated market-making policies.

Executive summary

Spooner et al. build an event-driven reinforcement-learning market maker in a historical limit-order-book simulator covering ten securities and eight months of data. Their consolidated agent combines SARSA with eligibility traces, an asymmetrically dampened PnL reward, and a linear combination of tile codings. It generally achieves stronger risk-adjusted out-of-sample performance and much smaller inventories than fixed-spread and online-learning benchmarks, although results are not uniformly profitable across securities.

Problem and empirical setting

The paper treats market making as sequential control of bid and ask quotes under inventory risk and partial observability. Decisions occur whenever the observed order book changes rather than at regularly spaced clock times.

  • The objective is to capture spreads while avoiding prolonged directional inventory.
  • The evaluation uses chronologically later test data rather than mixing observations randomly across time.
  • Normalized daily PnL measures profit relative to the average market spread, while mean absolute position measures inventory exposure.

Historical order-book simulator

The simulator reconstructs the market directly from eight months of 2010 high-frequency data for ten securities from four sectors. It tracks the top five order-book levels and transaction updates, allowing the agent's own orders to join simulated queues.

  • Agent orders do not alter the replayed historical market, so endogenous market impact is absent.
  • Because the data aggregate orders at each price, the simulator cannot identify which queued orders were cancelled.
  • The model assumes cancellations are uniformly distributed through a queue, making the probability of an ahead-of-agent cancellation proportional to the volume ahead.

Actions and reward design

The action space contains nine paired limit-order configurations with different quote depths and biases, plus a market-order action that reduces inventory. The paper compares ordinary incremental PnL with symmetric and asymmetric dampening of the inventory-related mark-to-market term.

  • Asymmetric dampening subtracts only positive inventory-derived gains, preserving speculative losses while reducing the incentive to profit from directional exposure.
  • The experiments show a policy change toward smaller, more neutral inventories beginning around dampening values of 0.05–0.1.
  • The authors identify the inventory component of PnL as both a dominant behavioral incentive and a major source of learning instability.

State representation and learning

The study evaluates Q-learning, SARSA, R-learning, and several double or expected variants, generally with eligibility traces. State representations range from inventory and active quote distances to a full market state containing spread, price movement, imbalance, signed volume, volatility, and relative strength.

  • One-step Q-learning and SARSA failed to obtain useful results; eligibility traces materially improved learning.
  • A monolithic full-state tile coding often degraded or diverged as dimensionality increased.
  • The proposed linear combination of tile codings learns agent-only, market-only, and joint value functions separately, then combines them; the default weights are 0.6, 0.1, and 0.3.

Out-of-sample results and significance

The consolidated SARSA agent uses eligibility traces, asymmetric reward dampening, and the linear-combination state representation. On the forty-day test sample it usually produces lower inventory and more stable PnL than the benchmarks, but its negative results on ING.AS and NOK1V.HE show that the approach does not dominate on every asset.

  • On HSBA.L, consolidated normalized daily PnL is 15.43 ± 13.01 versus 2.80 ± 10.30 for the fixed-depth benchmark, while mean absolute position falls from 2678 ± 1981 to 104 ± 179.
  • On GSK.L, consolidated normalized daily PnL is 7.32 ± 7.23 versus 0.95 ± 2.86 for the fixed benchmark, with mean absolute position falling from 1342 ± 1210 to 57 ± 105.
  • The central methodological lesson is that reward shaping, state factorization, and execution realism can matter more than substituting a more elaborate temporal-difference algorithm.

Key findings

  • Eligibility traces resolve much of the reward-attribution difficulty that made one-step temporal-difference agents ineffective in this setting.
  • Plain incremental PnL encourages speculative inventory and unstable learning; asymmetric dampening produces smaller positions and more consistent returns.
  • Adding all market variables to one tile coding can worsen learning, whereas separately learning agent, market, and joint representations provides a more stable compromise.
  • The consolidated agent substantially reduces inventory exposure and improves risk-adjusted performance overall, but remains unprofitable on some securities.
  • A realistic queue-aware replay environment materially changes benchmark conclusions obtained under simpler execution assumptions.

Notable quotes

This approach to simulating an exchange is highly realistic and makes limited assumptions about the market.
The “natural” reward function for trading agents is profit and loss (PnL): i.e. how much money is made or lost through exchanges with the market.
The key result is that the LCTC marries expressivity and efficiency and helps to prevent divergence even when the market variables have little informational content.
For the consolidated agent, it appears that the learnt policy targets a near-zero inventory, relying less on speculative trading and thus yielding the consistency one expects from a good market making strategy.

Limitations

  • Historical replay does not react to the agent's orders, so the experiments omit endogenous price impact and assume the agent's small order size makes that omission negligible.
  • Aggregate depth data do not reveal the exact position of cancelled orders; queue reconstruction therefore relies on a uniform-cancellation assumption.
  • The tested strategy uses a discrete action set with a single bid and ask and does not model important frictions such as fees, rebates, or latency.

Open questions

  • Does the learned policy remain profitable after endogenous market impact, exchange fees and rebates, communication latency, and adverse-selection effects are modeled?
  • Can recurrent deep reinforcement learning or sequential Bayesian queue reconstruction improve decisions under partial observability without destabilizing training?
  • How should parametrized actions, multiple simultaneous orders, and variable order sizes be introduced while preserving inventory control and sample efficiency?
