Agent Laboratory: Using LLM Agents as Research Assistants

📚 Foundations · 2026-09-13
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-09-13

Theme: Verifier-Grounded Autonomous Research and Code Search
These systems represent three increasingly search-oriented research topologies: a role-specialized multi-agent laboratory, a solution tree over executable ML programs, and an evolutionary archive over machine-graded code. Their evidence shows that autonomous research becomes materially stronger as evaluation moves from LLM judgment toward held-out metrics, exact artifacts, executable checks, and production measurements.
Source: arxiv
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.04227

This work is foundational for autonomous research systems because it specifies and evaluates an end-to-end, multi-agent workflow spanning literature review, executable experimentation, report generation, and optional human checkpoints.

Executive summary

Agent Laboratory is an open-source, human-initiated research pipeline that assigns specialized LLM agents to literature review, experimental planning, data preparation, code search, result interpretation, and paper writing. Its mle-solver and paper-solver combine execution or compilation checks with iterative editing, but their generic quality objectives rely heavily on LLM judges; human reviewers scored autonomous papers far below those judges. Under the paper's protocol, human checkpoints improved paper scores, the cheapest backend cost $2.33 per workflow, and an execution-grounded MLE-Bench evaluation found mle-solver produced valid submissions for all ten selected challenges and earned four medals.

Problem and scope

The system targets the execution bottleneck after a researcher supplies an idea, rather than autonomously inventing the research direction. It produces a code repository and research report and supports autonomous operation or a co-pilot mode with human review after each subtask.

  • The workflow addresses machine-learning research rather than unrestricted scientific experimentation.
  • The autonomous study generated 15 papers from five research questions and three LLM backends.
  • Co-pilot checkpoints let a researcher approve a subtask or request another attempt with high-level feedback.

Architecture and agent topology

The pipeline has three broad phases—literature review, experimentation, and report writing—with role-specialized PhD, Postdoc, ML Engineer, and Professor agents. The PhD agent searches arXiv and curates full texts; PhD and Postdoc agents formulate plans and interpret results; mle-solver iteratively edits executable code; and paper-solver constructs and revises an eight-section LaTeX report before reviewer agents recommend finalization or returning to an earlier stage.

  • Literature review exposes SUMMARY, FULL_TEXT, and ADD_PAPER actions and iteratively curates relevant sources.
  • mle-solver explores programs through EDIT and REPLACE operations, self-reflection, a pool of top programs, and parallel candidate modifications.
  • paper-solver builds a scaffold, can search arXiv, applies line-level edits, compiles LaTeX, and uses simulated NeurIPS reviews for refinement.
  • Three reviewer agents provide final paper feedback, after which the PhD agent may revisit planning, experimentation, or interpretation.

Verifier grounding and its limits

The framework has partial execution grounding: data-preparation and experimental code are run, candidate code is checked and repaired after errors, and LaTeX edits must compile. However, the default mle-solver ranks research programs with a Professor LLM that assigns a 0–1 alignment score from the plan, code, and printed output, while paper-solver uses an LLM reviewer; these are judge signals rather than independent factual verifiers. In the isolated MLE-Bench experiment, the authors replace the LLM score with a held-out development metric and evaluate the selected program on Kaggle's test set, providing stronger execution-grounded evidence.

  • Compilation establishes executability or document integrity, not scientific correctness.
  • The generic research-code and paper objectives remain vulnerable to self-evaluation bias.
  • Human review showed automated paper scores were substantially over-optimistic.
  • MLE-Bench used an 80/20 train/dev split and final test-set scoring instead of the LLM reward model.

Human evaluation, runtime, and benchmark results

Ten volunteer PhD students reviewed three autonomous papers each. o1-preview received the highest report-quality and usefulness ratings, while o1-mini received the highest experimental-quality rating; nevertheless, human NeurIPS-style overall scores were only 3.5–4.0/10, versus 6.1/10 from automated reviewers. Co-pilot papers reached 4.38/10 under external review, while reported end-to-end costs ranged from $2.33 to $13.10 and runtimes from 1,165.4 to 6,201.3 seconds.

  • Automated reviewers averaged 6.1/10 versus 3.8/10 from humans on autonomous papers.
  • External reviewers scored co-pilot papers 4.38/10, an improvement of 0.58 over the autonomous average but below the cited 5.85 NeurIPS accepted-paper average.
  • gpt-4o was cheapest and fastest; o1-preview was slowest and most expensive but had the highest average subtask success rate.
  • On ten selected low-complexity text and tabular MLE-Bench tasks, mle-solver submitted valid solutions for all tasks, earned four medals, and exceeded median human performance on six.

Significance

The paper supplies a concrete decomposition of research assistance into agents, tools, checkpoints, and iterative search modules, making it a useful systems blueprint. More importantly, its evaluation demonstrates that apparent success depends strongly on the verifier: execution-grounded benchmark outcomes, human utility judgments, and self-generated LLM review scores give materially different pictures of quality.

  • Human-in-the-loop operation improved presentation-oriented criteria more than significance or contribution.
  • The paper reports a low-cost route for generating research prototypes rather than conference-ready autonomous science.
  • Its judge-versus-human discrepancy is an important warning for later autoresearch systems that optimize proxy scores.

Key findings

  • A role-specialized pipeline can connect full-text literature retrieval, collaborative planning, executable code generation, result interpretation, and report writing in one continuous workflow.
  • Default program selection and paper refinement are substantially LLM-judge-driven, whereas the MLE-Bench study uses held-out metrics and final test-set execution.
  • Automated reviewers overestimated autonomous paper quality by 2.3 points on average: 6.1/10 versus 3.8/10 from humans.
  • Human co-piloting raised externally evaluated overall scores from 3.8/10 to 4.38/10, mainly through quality, soundness, clarity, and presentation rather than significance.
  • On the selected ten-task MLE-Bench subset, mle-solver earned four medals and exceeded median human performance on six tasks, though comparisons used different backends and excluded invalid baseline submissions from average-score calculations.
  • The gpt-4o workflow averaged $2.33 and 1,165.4 seconds, compared with $7.51 and 3,616.8 seconds for o1-mini and $13.10 and 6,201.3 seconds for o1-preview.

Notable quotes

There are two ways in which Agent Laboratory can be operated: autonomous and co-pilot modes.
Our findings suggest that automated reviewers demonstrate notable discrepancies across all metrics compared with human evaluators, with a tendency to highly over-estimate the contribution of self-evaluated work.
Instead of using an LLM scoring function, the mle-solver score is evaluated on the dev set, which is a 20% random sample taken from the original training set, and the training set is represented by the other 80% split.
Enabling flexible repository-level file modification and execution is a clear next step for future work.

Limitations

  • The central mle-solver and paper-solver objectives rely on subjective LLM self-evaluation, and automated paper scores substantially exceed human scores.
  • The workflow enforces a fixed report structure, limits papers to two figures, and cannot independently manage repository-level code.
  • Observed failures include hallucinated experimental claims, literature-search loops, context-limit overflows, degenerate experiment outputs, process-terminating code, and unsandboxed system-command generation; the MLE-Bench comparison is also limited to ten low-complexity tasks with differing backend and validity protocols.

Open questions

  • Can claim-level provenance and objective, execution-grounded verifiers replace LLM reward scores throughout planning, experimentation, interpretation, and writing?
  • Would a controlled longitudinal study show sustained improvements in researchers' productivity and scientific quality, rather than only short-term perceived usefulness?
  • Can automatically optimized agent topologies and secure repository-level execution improve contribution and significance without increasing hallucination, unsafe code behavior, or evaluation gaming?
