PaperBench: Evaluating AI's Ability to Replicate AI Research

📚 Foundations · 2026-09-10 Full Article
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-09-10

Theme: Execution-grounded benchmarks for autonomous research
MLE-bench and PaperBench replace plausible research prose with executable outcomes. One evaluates complete competition workflows against human medal thresholds; the other independently reruns paper replications and scores implementation, execution, and result matching. Both expose long-horizon planning and recovery as larger bottlenecks than initial code generation.
Source: arxiv
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01848

A foundational execution-grounded benchmark that tests whether autonomous agents can build, run, and reproduce whole AI papers rather than merely propose research or write plausible code.

Executive summary

PaperBench evaluates end-to-end replication of 20 ICML 2024 papers: an agent receives a paper and clarifying addendum, builds a codebase without consulting the authors' implementation, and must reproduce results through a script executed in a fresh environment. Evaluation uses 8,316 granular outcomes in author-approved hierarchical rubrics spanning code development, execution, and result matching, with an auxiliary benchmark validating the automated judge. Frontier agents demonstrate useful partial capability but remain far below expert humans and struggle especially with sustained planning, integration, execution, and debugging.

Problem and execution-grounded task

The benchmark targets real ML research replication rather than isolated coding or idea-generation tasks. Each submission must contain a complete repository and a root-level reproduce.sh; the organizers execute that script from a clean Ubuntu 24.04 environment with an A10 GPU so claimed results must survive independent execution.

  • The dataset contains 20 ICML 2024 Spotlight and Oral papers spanning 12 topics.
  • Agents receive the paper and an author-informed addendum but may not consult the original implementation or other blacklisted replications.
  • Fresh execution separates reproducible outputs from artifacts or values hard-coded during the agent's development run.

Hierarchical rubrics and automated judging

Each paper has a weighted tree of requirements co-developed with one of its authors. Leaf requirements are binary and fall into Code Development, Execution, or Result Match categories; weighted scores propagate upward to a single Replication Score. SimpleJudge grades leaves independently, selecting the ten most relevant files when a submission exceeds the model context, while JudgeEval measures agreement with expert labels.

  • Across the 20 papers, the rubrics contain 8,316 leaf nodes.
  • The three requirement types distinguish plausible source code from successful execution and reproduced empirical outcomes.
  • On JudgeEval, o3-mini SimpleJudge reaches 0.83 F1 at an estimated $66 per paper; o1 reaches 0.84 F1 at $830 per paper.

Agent and human evaluation design

The main experiment runs six frontier models with BasicAgent on every paper for three runs per paper and a maximum agent runtime of 12 hours. IterativeAgent removes the agent's ability to terminate early and repeatedly asks for the next concrete step. The human baseline recruits eight ML PhD participants, gathers three independent attempts on each of four papers, and uses best-at-three as its expert score.

  • Each agent environment provides one A10 GPU, internet access, shell and Python execution, browsing, and a paginated file reader.
  • BasicAgent is a ReAct-style tool loop; IterativeAgent forces continued work and encourages piecemeal progress.
  • An extended o1 IterativeAgent run lasted 36 hours with snapshots graded at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 36 hours.

Results and scaffold sensitivity

Claude 3.5 Sonnet leads the BasicAgent setting at 21.0%, while o1 scores 13.2%. IterativeAgent raises o1 to 24.4% and o3-mini from 2.6% to 8.5%, but lowers Claude 3.5 Sonnet from 21.0% to 16.1%, showing that scaffold choices interact strongly with the underlying model. PaperBench Code-Dev is much easier: o1 IterativeAgent scores 43.4%, reinforcing the gap between writing code and obtaining independently reproduced results.

  • The 36-hour o1 IterativeAgent score is 26.0%, only modestly above its 12-hour 24.4% result.
  • On a common three-paper subset after 48 hours, the human best-at-three baseline scores 41.4% versus 26.6% for o1.
  • The appendix reports large within-paper variance and recommends multiple seeds for reliable comparisons.

What the benchmark reveals

Log inspection shows that most models terminate early, fail to prioritize under a limited budget, or cannot turn multi-step plans into sustained action. The human comparison shows a characteristic horizon effect: o1 starts faster but largely plateaus after its first hour, while humans overtake it after 24 hours. PaperBench therefore operationalizes autonomous-research capability as durable execution and verified empirical reproduction, not persuasive plans or code volume.

  • Requirement-stratified results are weakest on Execution and Result Match and stronger on Code Development.
  • The benchmark exposes integration, testing, monitoring, and troubleshooting as separate bottlenecks from code generation.
  • Its expensive, nondeterministic automated judge is itself evaluated rather than assumed reliable.

Key findings

  • PaperBench turns research replication into an executable artifact evaluated in a fresh environment, preventing plans, unrun code, and hard-coded results from counting as successful reproduction.
  • Author-approved hierarchical rubrics award partial credit while separately measuring implementation, execution, and empirical result matching across 8,316 leaf requirements.
  • The strongest BasicAgent result is only 21.0%, and the 48-hour human best-at-three baseline exceeds o1 by 41.4% to 26.6% on the shared three-paper subset.
  • Forcing continued, piecemeal work substantially improves o1 and o3-mini but harms Claude 3.5 Sonnet, demonstrating material model-by-scaffold interaction.
  • Agents' largest deficits concern long-horizon strategy, integration, testing, and successful execution rather than initial code production.

Notable quotes

A submission is only considered to have replicated a result when that result is reproduced by running the submission in a fresh setup.
All agents failed to strategize about how best to replicate the paper given the limited time available to them.
Notably, o1’s scores mostly plateau after the first hour, suggesting that the model is proficient at writing a lot of code quickly at the beginning of the attempt, but fails to effectively work beyond this time horizon to strategize how to improve its submission.
Despite our judge demonstrating good performance in our JudgeEval, it is not as accurate as an expert human judging submissions.

Limitations

  • The benchmark contains only 20 papers and its selection filters exclude work requiring distributed multi-node training, closed-source models, or new human data, limiting coverage of the full research landscape.
  • Creating each author-reviewed rubric requires many tens of expert hours, making benchmark expansion difficult; the human baseline itself covers only four papers.
  • SimpleJudge is nondeterministic and less accurate than expert grading, while full rollouts and grading remain expensive; future-model contamination is also possible because original implementations are online.

Open questions

  • Can dependency-graph rubrics and human-in-the-loop model assistance reduce rubric-construction effort without weakening correctness or enabling specification gaming?
  • Which planning, memory, prioritization, and recovery mechanisms let agents sustain productive experimental work beyond the early code-generation phase?
  • Can automated judges become cheaper, deterministic, and adversarially robust enough to assess complex execution and result matching with expert-level reliability?
