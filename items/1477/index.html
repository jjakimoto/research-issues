Market Making under a Weakly Consistent Limit Order Book Model

📚 Foundations · 2026-09-13
Read full article →

Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
Date: 2026-09-13

Theme: Inventory-Aware Market Making and Consistent Order-Book Control
These papers trace market-making control from analytically tractable inventory-risk models, through covariance-aware multi-asset quoting, to event-driven order-book models that enforce economically valid price movements. Together they establish why inventory skew, liquidity, volatility, and cross-asset risk matter—and why unrealistic simulators can manufacture apparent profitability.
Source: arxiv
Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.07222

This paper is foundational for event-driven market making because it formalizes limit-order-book consistency and demonstrates how inconsistent price/order dynamics create phantom profitability.

Executive summary

The paper defines direction, timing, and volume consistency for limit-order-book models, then constructs a tractable level-one model that enforces direction and timing consistency through twelve marked order-event processes. Market making becomes a combined optimal-switching and impulse-control problem whose HJB quasi-variational inequality is solved with an explicit finite-difference penalty scheme. In one million simulated 6.5-hour sessions, optimal control reduced profit standard deviation from 10,258 to 516 and raised the information ratio from 0.80 to 12.55, while inconsistent price-direction models overstated mean profit by 20%–63%.

Problem: consistency before optimization

The paper argues that many market-making models inherit exogenous diffusion prices from quote-driven settings and can therefore move prices in directions incompatible with the triggering order. It defines a consistent LOB through direction, timing, and volume conditions and calls a model weakly consistent when it satisfies the first two but does not track enough depth information to satisfy the third.

  • Direction consistency prevents, for example, an ask-price decline upon a buy market order.
  • Timing consistency requires quote changes to occur only at order-arrival or cancellation times.
  • Volume consistency links price changes to whether an aggressive order exhausts the best queue.
  • Ignoring these constraints can reward a market maker for economically impossible favorable moves after adverse-selection trades.

Twelve-event marked point-process model

Top-of-book activity is partitioned into twelve combinations of market orders, limit orders, and cancellations, split by side and aggressiveness. Aggressive events carry discrete price-jump marks, market orders carry volume marks, and common event processes make prices and order flow co-jump in the proper direction.

  • Types 1–6 are aggressive events that move the appropriate bid or ask; types 7–12 are non-aggressive.
  • The framework permits arbitrary volume and jump-size distributions rather than constant order sizes and one-tick moves.
  • Aggressive-limit-order intensities are forced to zero at a one-tick spread so the spread cannot fall below one tick.
  • The reduced-form model deliberately omits queue depths and is therefore weakly, rather than fully, consistent.

Switching and impulse control

The market maker chooses whether to provide liquidity on each side and may submit market-order impulses to reduce inventory. Expected terminal wealth is optimized net of a quadratic running inventory penalty, switching costs intended to approximate lost time priority, and impulse costs including exchange fees and slippage overhead.

  • A fixed participation rate replaces explicit tracking of every market-maker order in the queues.
  • Controls comprise intervention times, new bid/ask on-off regimes, and signed market-order sizes.
  • An ansatz removes cash and absolute price from the effective state, leaving time, inventory, spread, and bid/ask regimes.
  • The resulting HJBQVI contains both integral and intervention operators and is approximated with an explicit finite-difference penalty scheme.

Numerical policy behavior

The numerical example uses QQQ Nasdaq order-book statistics from May 8, 2014 as motivation, then imposes symmetric buy/sell parameters for a five-minute control problem. The solution exposes interpretable inventory thresholds governing whether to keep quoting, switch one side off, or immediately trade with a market order.

  • The base case uses a 10% participation rate, 0.01 tick size, and lognormal market-order volumes.
  • The bid impulse threshold reaches an equilibrium of 750 shares at 108 seconds remaining.
  • At the 750-share threshold, the optimal impulse begins at 230 shares and then equals inventory minus the 520-share impulse anchor.
  • Market-order impulses dominate switching for narrow spreads in the base case; switching becomes preferable at sufficiently wide spreads or different cost settings.

Simulation evidence and significance

The authors simulate one million 6.5-hour sessions under the weakly consistent book and three counterfactual books in which price direction is randomized independently of order type. Optimal control sacrifices some mean profit but sharply improves dispersion and tail shape, while breaking directional consistency materially inflates the apparent profitability of both strategies.

  • Under the consistent model, optimal-control mean profit is 6,471 versus 8,224 unconstrained, but standard deviation falls from 10,258 to 516.
  • The information ratio rises from 0.80 to 12.55, skewness changes from -0.44 to 0.10, and kurtosis falls from 7.81 to 3.19.
  • Inconsistent LOBs overstate mean profit by 49%–50% for unconstrained trading and 63% for optimal control in LOB1/LOB2.
  • Even LOB3, whose price direction is correct 80% of the time, overstates mean profit by 20%–25%.

Key findings

  • A useful market-making simulator must couple each order event to an economically admissible contemporaneous price move; correlation alone does not guarantee this consistency.
  • A twelve-type marked point-process representation captures order side, aggressiveness, volume, and jump size while remaining tractable at level one.
  • Optimal switching models lost price-time priority, while impulse control lets the market maker use market orders for immediate inventory reduction.
  • The optimal policy greatly reduces simulated risk and improves the information ratio despite lowering mean profit.
  • Even modest directional inconsistency substantially exaggerates expected market-making profit and can distort strategy comparisons.

Notable quotes

We associate a label of weakly consistent to a LOB if the model only complies with direction and timing consistency.
It is easy to see that our model is direction- and timing-consistent, however since we do not keep track of the depths of best quotes, our model is not volume-consistent.
The overstatement of mean profit is around 50-60% in LOB1 and LOB2.
Our weakly consistent reduced-form model provides a tractable alternative which still includes many of the important order-book features.

Limitations

  • The reduced-form model does not track best-queue depths and therefore cannot satisfy volume consistency.
  • Because only best quotes are modeled, the average execution price of an aggressive market order is approximated by the best bid or ask even when the order walks the book.
  • Existence, uniqueness, comparison, value-function linkage, and convergence of the HJBQVI numerical scheme are conjectured rather than rigorously proved.

Open questions

  • Can the viscosity-solution theory and convergence of the explicit numerical scheme be established for this system of QVIs with integral and intervention operators?
  • Can stochastic intensities, dependent volume/jump marks, and partial queue-depth information be incorporated without making optimization intractable?
  • How should switching costs from lost queue priority be estimated empirically and validated in multi-day, out-of-sample or live execution tests?
