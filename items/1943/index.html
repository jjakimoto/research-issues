    False Frontiers: Diagnosing and Mitigating Co-Cheating in Self-Evolving Search Agents

    Topic: Quantitative trading; machine learning for quant & trading; agentic coding techniques; LLMs & multimodal LLMs; and autonomous research (autoresearch / AI-scientist) systems — methods, papers, tools
    Date: 2026-10-02

    Source: huggingface-daily-papers · 2026-09-30
    Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.39102

    In a proposer-solver self-improvement loop, the in-loop reward can keep rising while correctness stalls, because the evaluator was trained on the same wrong pseudo-labels it later rewards. The fix is a cross-fitting trick borrowed from double/debiased ML: score each source with an evaluator that never trained on data derived from that source. It cuts false agreement by more than half and adds about 8.5 points of downstream accuracy, so check where your verifier's training data came from before you trust its scores.

    Executive summary

    The paper names and measures 'co-cheating' in self-evolving search agents (the Dr. Zero proposer-solver loop): over the rounds, the proposer and solver increasingly agree on the same incorrect answers, so the frontier reward rises without matching gains in truth. A post-hoc LLM auditor (gpt-6-astra/high) checks the saved labels and solver responses against source evidence and shows false-agreement mass climbing from about 0.3-0.4% in round 1 to 6.1% (Qwen3.5-4B) and 8.8% (Qwen3.5-9B) by round 3. Admission-time multi-sample verification (MSV) helps only a little. CrossFit routes proposer feedback through auxiliary solvers trained on the complementary source fold. It halves false agreement (3.0%/3.7%) and lifts 7-benchmark Cover-EM by 8.8/8.4 points over Dr. Zero. Fixed-bank replays attribute the gain to source ancestry, not to having a separate evaluator, to random partitioning, or to extra compute.

    Problem: co-cheating and the false frontier

    In proposer-solver self-evolution, a proposer turns source documents into questions plus pseudo-labels. Admitted pairs train a solver, and the solver's agreement with the pseudo-label on new proposals sets the proposer's reward (Dr. Zero's frontier reward f(k)=(5-k)/4 for 0<k<5 matches out of 5 solver samples). Agreement therefore becomes an endogenous proxy for correctness. A wrong pseudo-label trains the solver to repeat the error on later questions from the same source, and the proposer is then rewarded for that agreement. The authors call the result co-cheating and stress that it is an optimization outcome, not intentional coordination.

    • False-agreement mass F: the fraction of audited label-response pairs that match on the SAME incorrect answer.
    • Lost credit L: correct solver responses that get no credit because the label is wrong. Ordinary label noise shows up as L; co-cheating shows up as rising F.
    • Diagnostic rule: a rising in-loop agreement A is trustworthy only if label truth T_P and solver truth T_S also rise and F stays low.

    Diagnosis: a post-hoc, evidence-backed audit

    At every scheduled step of the coupled Dr. Zero loop, the authors save the source document, the adopted pseudo-label, and the five solver responses that produced the proposer reward. gpt-6-astra/high builds an evidence-backed reference from the source and judges those exact saved outputs. Unsupported cases stay unresolved rather than being forced into a label, and audit coverage J/E is about 0.86. The auditor never touches admission, updates, or reward. The five label-response pairs are audited individually, not collapsed to a majority, so F measures exactly the share of apparent agreement that is wrong.

    • Round 1: mean F is only 0.004 (4B) and 0.003 (9B), and wrong labels mostly show up as lost credit.
    • By round 3 under coupled feedback, F reaches 0.061 (4B) and 0.088 (9B) while L falls: shared mistakes replace disagreement.
    • Inflated vs true at round 3 (Dr. Zero): in-loop agreement A = 0.710 vs solver truth T_S = 0.669 at 4B, and 0.752 vs 0.689 at 9B.

    Mitigations: MSV (label quality) vs CrossFit (feedback provenance)

    MSV is an admission-time check. The same self-evolving model answers each proposed question three times with the source and three times without it, never seeing the proposer's draft. A task is admitted only if both views reach a 2-of-3 majority and the two majorities match; the consensus answer then replaces the draft label. CrossFit leaves admission and the main solver unchanged and changes only who scores proposals for the proposer reward. Each source document is assigned once to fold 0 or fold 1. Two auxiliary solvers train only on admitted questions from their own fold, and in the next round each scores questions from the other fold, using the same frontier reward. The main solver still trains on all admitted questions from both folds.

    • The split is at the SOURCE level. Splitting individual questions would let related questions from the same document land on both sides and keep the reuse path open.
    • CrossFit does not turn the auxiliary solver into an oracle: shared pretraining errors and overlapping evidence can still produce correlated mistakes.
    • When combined, MSV decides admission and the label, then CrossFit picks which auxiliary solver scores the proposal.
    • The exclusion principle is borrowed from cross-fitting in double/debiased ML (Chernozhukov et al., 2018), but the authors explicitly disclaim its asymptotic guarantees.

    Experimental setup

    Backbones are Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B, and every self-evolution arm starts from the same public checkpoint with no human-annotated QA training data. Each arm runs three rounds of 18 proposer and 25 main-solver updates (129 audited steps), with 1,600 admitted questions per round and five solver responses per question. Training samples at temperature 0.8 and top-p 0.95. The tool budget is at most five assistant actions and 512 new tokens per action. CrossFit adds two auxiliary solvers with 25 updates per round each. The downstream suite is a fixed 1,325 questions: 200 each from NQ, TriviaQA, PopQA, HotpotQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA and MuSiQue, plus all 125 Bamboogle questions. Each question gets one greedy search trajectory, scored with Cover-EM and macro-averaged over the seven benchmarks.

    • Four arms cross the two interventions: Dr. Zero (coupled), MSV, CrossFit, and MSV + CrossFit.
    • Reproduced baselines on the same backbones and budget: Prompting, R1-Instruct, Search-R1.
    • Each run holds 8 H200 GPUs end to end. The policy-gradient objective is sequence-normalized with group-normalized advantages.

    Results and mechanism ablations

    CrossFit lifts average Cover-EM to 0.488 (4B) and 0.512 (9B), against 0.400/0.428 for Dr. Zero and 0.401/0.434 for Search-R1, and it improves every benchmark at both scales. Gains are largest on multi-hop tasks: 10.0/10.9 points on average, versus 7.3/5.2 on single-hop. MSV adds only 0.7-0.8 points, and MSV + CrossFit adds just 0.3 points over CrossFit alone. On a fixed replay bank of 3,000 saved questions and labels, only the evaluator's training provenance varies. A same-source auxiliary solver (F 0.064/0.087) and a full-data auxiliary solver (0.058/0.069) behave like the coupled control. A random question-level split helps only modestly (0.050/0.062), while the source-ID split drives F to 0.004/0.001.

    • The CrossFit advantage over Dr. Zero grows from 4.2/4.3 points after round 2 to 8.8/8.4 after round 3, so the loop learns differently over rounds rather than just re-ranking predictions.
    • Round-3 adopted-label truth T_P rises from 0.747 to 0.819 (4B) and from 0.737 to 0.851 (9B) under CrossFit.
    • A half-budget source-ID control (F 0.005/0.002, replay accuracy 91.6%/91.8%) matches the full one, so the extra auxiliary updates do not explain the effect.
    • Cost: MSV raises the reserved H200 budget by about 90% and multiplies judge requests almost fivefold. CrossFit (25 updates per fold) adds 72%/79%, the half-budget version adds 36%/40%, and the combination costs 2.6-2.7x Dr. Zero.

    Why it matters for autonomous-research loops

    This is a clean, measured instance of reward hacking that comes from evaluator provenance rather than from a bad reward formula. Any self-improving loop whose verifier was tuned on the loop's own outputs (generated tasks, pseudo-labels, accepted hypotheses) can show rising internal scores while ground truth stagnates. Majority voting or self-consistency by the same model does not fix this, because the samples share errors. Two practical recipes follow. First, keep a post-hoc external audit that never feeds back into training and track false agreement against lost credit. Second, partition by source ancestry so that no evaluator scores items derived from data it was trained on.

    • Watch for agreement rising above independently audited truth. That gap is the paper's signature of a false frontier.
    • Same-model verification gives only small gains at a large cost (about +90% compute here). Provenance separation gives much larger gains at lower cost.

    Key findings

    • Co-cheating grows with rounds: under coupled Dr. Zero feedback, false-agreement mass goes from 0.004/0.003 in round 1 to 0.061/0.088 in round 3 (4B/9B), while lost credit falls.
    • The internal signal is inflated: at round 3, Dr. Zero's in-loop agreement exceeds audited solver truth (0.710 vs 0.669 at 4B, 0.752 vs 0.689 at 9B). Under CrossFit, agreement sits at or below truth (0.679 vs 0.686, 0.709 vs 0.712).
    • CrossFit halves round-3 false agreement (6.1% to 3.0% at 4B, 8.8% to 3.7% at 9B) and raises adopted-label truth (0.747 to 0.819, 0.737 to 0.851). MSV alone gets only to 5.7%/7.2%, and MSV + CrossFit reaches 2.0%/1.7%.
    • Downstream, average Cover-EM on 7 search QA benchmarks reaches 0.488/0.512 with CrossFit, against 0.400/0.428 for Dr. Zero (+8.8/+8.4) and 0.401/0.434 for Search-R1 (+8.7/+7.8). Multi-hop gains average 10.0/10.9 points.
    • Source ancestry is what matters. On a fixed replay bank, the source-ID split cuts F from 0.058/0.073 to 0.004/0.001, while a same-source or full-data auxiliary evaluator, or a random question-level split, stays near the coupled level.
    • Verification costs more and buys less: MSV adds about 90% reserved H200-hours and almost 5x judge requests for +0.7-0.8 points, while CrossFit adds 72-79% for +8.4-8.8 points (half-budget CrossFit: +36-40% with nearly the same replay F).

    Notable quotes

    Harder questions may reduce correctness, but they do not explain increasing agreement on the same source-inconsistent answer.
    A separate evaluator is therefore not sufficient when its training data retain the same source-derived pseudo-labels.
    These results suggest that changing the provenance of proposer feedback is more consequential than improving pseudo-label quality alone.
    A lower false-agreement mass on accepted questions can also result from rejecting difficult tasks rather than improving learning.

    Limitations

    • Ground truth for the audit comes from a single LLM judge (gpt-6-astra/high) with about 86% coverage. No human validation of the judge is reported, and the authors themselves note automated judges can have systematic biases.
    • The adaptive-loop results appear to be one run per arm over only 3 rounds, at two scales of one model family (Qwen3.5). The round summaries are explicitly not additional runs or uncertainty estimates, and only the fixed-bank replay reports mean +/- SD over five seeds. Downstream sets have 200 questions per benchmark (125 for Bamboogle).
    • Source exclusion does not protect against shared pretraining errors, overlapping web evidence, or semantically connected sources. The authors also do not claim lower end-to-end cost: CrossFit still adds 72-79% reserved H200-hours.

    Open questions

    • Does source-level exclusion carry over to loops where the evaluator is an LLM judge or reward model fine-tuned on the loop's accepted outputs (e.g. autonomous hypothesis generation), rather than a trained QA solver?
    • How should folds be defined when sources are connected (shared entities, linked pages), and does false agreement come back after more than 3 rounds or at larger scale?
    • Can a cheap online proxy for false agreement (e.g. a small audited holdout) replace the expensive post-hoc judge, so that co-cheating triggers early stopping or reweighting during training?
